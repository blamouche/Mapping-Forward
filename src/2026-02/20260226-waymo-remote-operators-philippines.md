# Waymo's Self-Driving Cars Get Remote Guidance From Workers in the Philippines
**Source**: https://futurism.com/advanced-transport/waymos-controlled-workers-philippines
**Date**: February 26, 2026
**Author**: Futurism Staff
**Keywords**: Waymo, autonomous vehicles, remote operators, Philippines, robotaxi, safety, Tesla

## Elevator pitch
Congressional hearing reveals Waymo and Tesla robotaxis rely on overseas human operators in the Philippines for real-time guidance, raising safety and cybersecurity concerns among lawmakers.

## Takeaways
- Waymo's "self-driving" vehicles use remote human operators ("fleet response agents") based in the US and abroad, including the Philippines, who provide guidance when cars encounter atypical situations
- These operators view real-time feeds from exterior cameras and can suggest lane changes or paths for the vehicle to consider, though they don't directly control steering
- Senator Ed Markey raised concerns about cybersecurity vulnerabilities, outdated information risks, and the potential for hostile actors to gain "driver-like control" of thousands of vehicles
- Tesla's VP of vehicle engineering confirmed their vehicles also use similar remote operators and actively participates in hacking events to test security
- The revelation comes a week after a Waymo struck a child near a Santa Monica elementary school, and NHTSA data shows Tesla robotaxis crash three times as much as human drivers

## Synthesis
During a February 2026 Congressional hearing titled "Hit the Road Mac: The Future of Self-Driving Cars," Waymo's chief safety officer Mauricio Peña disclosed that the company's autonomous vehicles receive remote assistance from human operators located both domestically and overseas, including the Philippines. This admission has sparked significant debate about the true nature of "autonomous" driving and raised serious national security concerns.

The operators, whom Waymo calls "fleet response agents," function as a "phone-a-friend" system for the AI. When the vehicle's driving software encounters unusual situations, it can request human assistance. These operators view live exterior camera feeds and can provide contextual guidance, such as which lane to choose or suggesting a path for the vehicle to consider. Waymo maintains that the vehicle's autonomous system remains in control of all dynamic driving tasks and doesn't rely solely on human input.

However, Senator Ed Markey (D-MA) expressed serious reservations about this arrangement. He argued that "having people overseas influencing American vehicles is a safety issue," citing potential problems with outdated information, cybersecurity vulnerabilities, and the lack of verification that operators hold US driver's licenses. In a formal statement, Markey warned that overseas operations "may be more susceptible to physical takeover by hostile actors" who could potentially gain control of thousands of vehicles transporting passengers on American roads.

Waymo responded that their fleet response teams undergo thorough vetting, including background checks, driving record evaluations, random drug screenings, and verification of valid passenger car or van licenses. Tesla's VP Lars Moravy similarly confirmed that Tesla uses remote operators but emphasized that core driving controls (go, stop, steer) exist in a secure embedded layer that cannot be accessed externally. Tesla also noted they actively pay hackers to attempt to breach their vehicles' security.

The hearing's timing is particularly significant, coming just a week after a Waymo robotaxi struck a child near a Santa Monica elementary school, triggering a federal investigation. Meanwhile, NHTSA data indicates Tesla's robotaxis crash at three times the rate of human drivers, even with safety monitors present. Tesla has also quietly paused "unsupervised" robotaxi operations.

This disclosure fundamentally challenges the marketing narrative of fully autonomous vehicles and highlights the complex human infrastructure supporting current self-driving technology. As autonomous vehicles become more prevalent, the question of who controls them—and from where—will likely become a central regulatory battleground.
