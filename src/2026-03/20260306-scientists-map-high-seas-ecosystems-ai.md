# Scientists to map vulnerable ecosystems in the High Seas with AI
**Source**: https://oceanographicmagazine.com/news/scientists-to-map-vulnerable-ecosystems-in-the-high-seas-with-ai/
**Date**: March 6, 2026
**Author**: Rob Hutchins
**Keywords**: Deep Vision, AI, marine ecosystems, High Seas, Atlantic Ocean, conservation, biodiversity mapping, Plymouth Marine Laboratory

## Elevator pitch
The Deep Vision project uses AI to analyze decades of unexamined seafloor imagery and create comprehensive maps of vulnerable deep-sea ecosystems across international waters.

## Takeaways
- Deep Vision, spearheaded by Plymouth Marine Laboratory and University of Plymouth, uses AI to process vast seafloor imagery archives
- The project is supported by the Bezos Earth Fund's AI for Climate and Nature Grand Challenge
- Target ecosystems include deep-sea coral formations, sponge communities, and crinoid ecosystems in the Atlantic
- International collaboration spans Norway, Ireland, Germany, Portugal, Gibraltar, and other nations
- The methodology is designed to scale to other ocean regions after completing Atlantic mapping

## Synthesis
The Deep Vision initiative addresses a fundamental challenge in marine conservation science: the vast majority of seafloor imagery collected by research expeditions remains unanalyzed due to the time-intensive nature of manual review. Scientists can collect far more data than human analysts can process, creating a growing archive of potentially critical ecological information that goes unused. AI acceleration changes this equation fundamentally.

The project's approach — applying self-supervised vision models to both archival footage and newly captured imagery — enables rapid, consistent identification of sensitive marine habitats that would take years to classify manually. The target ecosystems are specifically chosen for their ecological importance: deep-sea coral formations, sponge communities, and crinoid assemblages function as keystone habitats supporting diverse marine life. They also play roles in carbon storage and nutrient cycling that make their health relevant to broader climate dynamics.

The timing matters. The announcement was made at the World Ocean Summit in Montreal, linking the scientific initiative to the ongoing international governance discussions about the High Seas Treaty and the establishment of marine protected areas in international waters. Accurate ecosystem maps are not merely scientific outputs — they are the evidentiary foundation for policy decisions about where protections should be established and how they should be enforced.

The Bezos Earth Fund's AI for Climate and Nature Grand Challenge funding signals growing institutional interest in applying AI capabilities to environmental monitoring at scale. Deep Vision represents one of the more methodologically grounded applications in this space: rather than AI generating predictions from limited data, it is being deployed to systematically process evidence that already exists but has been practically inaccessible. The expansion ambition — scaling the approach to other ocean regions beyond the Atlantic — suggests the initiative is conceived as a long-term infrastructure project rather than a bounded scientific study.
