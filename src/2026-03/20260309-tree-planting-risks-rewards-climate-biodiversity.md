# Study maps tree-planting risks and rewards for climate and biodiversity
**Source**: https://news.mongabay.com/2026/03/study-maps-tree-planting-risks-and-rewards-for-climate-and-biodiversity/
**Date**: March 9, 2026
**Author**: Jeremy Hance
**Keywords**: afforestation, reforestation, biodiversity, carbon sequestration, forest restoration, biome analysis, Environmental Research Letters

## Elevator pitch
A global study maps where tree-planting can simultaneously benefit climate and biodiversity, finding tropical forests highly compatible while grasslands pose significant ecological risks.

## Takeaways
- Tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forests show the highest potential for combined carbon capture and biodiversity gains
- Grasslands, shrublands, and savannas are poorly suited to afforestation, as tree-planting could displace existing ecosystems
- Temperate coniferous forests offer good potential, while temperate grasslands pose biodiversity risks despite moderate carbon benefits
- The study defines afforestation as establishing trees in areas unforested for 30+ years, reforestation as restoring more recently cleared land
- The analysis assumes native species use — many real-world projects plant non-native, fast-growing species, undermining ecological benefits

## Synthesis
Published in Environmental Research Letters, this global biodiversity modeling study provides a spatial framework for evaluating where afforestation and reforestation efforts can deliver dual benefits for climate and wildlife. The research addresses a significant policy gap: tree-planting has become a popular climate mitigation strategy, but its ecological consequences vary dramatically depending on where and how it is executed.

The study's core contribution is biome-level mapping of compatibility between tree-planting objectives and conservation goals. The finding that tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forests represent the highest-potential zone is consistent with existing understanding of these ecosystems as carbon-dense and biodiversity-rich, but the granular analysis reveals substantial variation within this biome that generic "plant trees in the tropics" policy cannot capture.

The grassland finding carries significant practical implications. Grasslands, savannas, and shrublands are frequently targeted for afforestation because they are perceived as "empty" or "degraded" land, but they contain specialized biodiversity adapted to open conditions. Tree planting in these systems does not restore forests — it eliminates non-forest ecosystems that have their own ecological value and carbon dynamics. The study's explicit mapping of this risk provides evidence for conservationists and policymakers arguing against indiscriminate afforestation programs.

The caveat about native versus non-native species is crucial. The entire analysis is predicated on native species restoration, but the economic pressures of carbon credit markets and the availability of fast-growing commercial species mean that many real-world tree-planting programs plant monocultures of non-native timber trees. These plantations may deliver short-term carbon accounting benefits while providing minimal biodiversity value and sometimes negative ecological impacts.

The study's value lies in providing a spatial decision framework rather than a binary endorsement of tree-planting. Localized planning that accounts for biome type, existing ecosystem condition, and species selection is positioned as the prerequisite for responsible afforestation investment.
