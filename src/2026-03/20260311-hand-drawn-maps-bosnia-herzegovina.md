# How hand-drawn maps are revealing real stories from Bosnia and Herzegovina
**Source**: https://from.ncl.ac.uk/how-hand-drawn-maps-are-revealing-real-stories-from-bosnia-and-herzegovina
**Date**: March 11, 2026
**Author**: Dr James Riding
**Keywords**: Bosnia and Herzegovina, subjective atlas, participatory mapping, post-conflict, cultural geography, Dayton Agreement, peacebuilding, cartography

## Elevator pitch
An atlas of hand-drawn maps by young people in Bosnia and Herzegovina challenges conventional post-conflict cartography by capturing domestic scenes, inherited trauma, and community resilience rather than political borders.

## Takeaways
- The project ran workshops in Sarajevo, Srebrenica, and Vitez producing over 80 personal mappings using drawings, collages, and photography
- The atlas deliberately rejects objective territory representation in favor of subjective, personal geographic experience
- Maps capture everyday life: domestic scenes, local food, inherited trauma, and community solidarity
- The project acknowledges ongoing challenges — unemployment, corruption, pollution — while emphasizing hope
- Available through Subjective Editions in English and BCS languages for €27.50 (print) or €6.00 (PDF)

## Synthesis
Dr. James Riding's description of the Bosnia and Herzegovina subjective atlas represents a methodologically distinct approach to geographic representation in post-conflict contexts. Rather than analyzing the country through the lens of its 1992-1995 war and the political geography of the Dayton Agreement — which divided the country into entities along ethnic lines — the project invites young residents to map their own lived geographic experience.

The participatory cartography methodology — workshops using drawing, collage, and photography rather than digital mapping tools — serves a specific purpose. It removes the technical and political authority from the mapmaking process, returning it to the people who inhabit the mapped territory. In a country where official maps and administrative boundaries carry the weight of internationally negotiated post-war settlement, this is a form of counter-cartography: insisting that geography means more than boundary placement.

The subjects that emerged from participants are revealing: domestic interiors, shared meals, family stories, environmental observations, and expressions of both grief and resilience. These are geographic experiences that conventional cartography has no language to express, but which constitute the actual texture of life in a specific place. By collecting them in atlas form, the project creates a composite portrait of Bosnia and Herzegovina that no official map can replicate.

The inclusion of sites like Srebrenica — the location of the 1995 genocide — in the workshop program is particularly significant. That young people chose to participate in mapping exercises in this context, and that some expressed working through "heavy topics through art," suggests the project is functioning as both geographic documentation and informal therapeutic process.

The scholarly context — published by a Senior Lecturer in Cultural Geography at Newcastle University and made available in local languages at accessible price points — ensures the project reaches both academic and community audiences, bridging knowledge production and peacebuilding practice.
