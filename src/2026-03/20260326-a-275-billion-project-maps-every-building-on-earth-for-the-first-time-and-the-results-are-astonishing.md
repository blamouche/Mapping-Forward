## Elevator pitch
The Global Building Atlas uses satellite imagery to map nearly all buildings on Earth in high-resolution 3D, creating a new global baseline for urban planning and risk analysis.

## Takeaways
- Researchers modeled about 2.75 billion buildings worldwide from satellite data since 2019.
- The atlas delivers ~3m resolution 3D footprints and heights for most buildings.
- Coverage and accuracy surpass earlier datasets like Open Buildings or World Settlement Footprint.
- The dataset enables analyses of built volume per capita and spatial inequality.
- Applications include urban planning, infrastructure gaps, disaster risk, and climate adaptation.

## Synthesis
The article presents the Global Building Atlas, a large-scale Earth observation project that claims the first high-resolution 3D map of virtually all buildings on the planet. Using thousands of satellite images collected since 2019, a research team led by Prof. Xiaoxiang Zhu at the Technical University of Munich modeled approximately 2.75 billion buildings across all continents, including rural regions that are typically underrepresented in global datasets. The resulting coverage is close to complete, with only a small fraction of structures missing.

What distinguishes this atlas is not just the size but the fidelity. The 3D models are built at roughly 3-by-3-meter resolution, which the article says is significantly more detailed than earlier global efforts such as Google’s Open Buildings or the World Settlement Footprint. The team reports that around 97% of buildings have enough resolution to capture footprint, shape, and height. This quality makes it possible to estimate built volume per capita, a new global indicator that links the mass of the built environment to population distribution.

The article highlights the practical value of this dataset for urban planning and policy. When integrated into large-scale spatial models, the atlas can reveal patterns of urbanization, population density, and socio-economic disparities within and across regions. Planners can identify where housing, schools, healthcare centers, or sports facilities are needed most, enabling more targeted and inclusive development. Because the dataset is three-dimensional, it provides insight not just into where people live but into how much built infrastructure exists in a given area.

Beyond planning, the atlas supports risk assessment and resilience. High-resolution building data improves the speed and accuracy of flood or landslide risk modeling, which can inform disaster prevention and emergency response. It can also contribute to climate adaptation by highlighting zones with high emissions or heightened exposure to heat. The ability to quantify built volume at scale helps identify which neighborhoods are most vulnerable and where mitigation measures could be prioritized.

Overall, the article frames the Global Building Atlas as a foundational layer for modern spatial analysis. It advances global mapping by combining near-complete coverage with detailed 3D geometry, opening up new research and policy uses. If access and maintenance are sustained, this dataset could become a reference point for global urban analytics, helping governments and researchers monitor growth, inequality, and environmental risk with unprecedented granularity.
