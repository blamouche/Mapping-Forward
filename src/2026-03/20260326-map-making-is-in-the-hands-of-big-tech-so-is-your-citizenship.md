# Map-Making Is in the Hands of Big Tech. So Is Your Citizenship.

**Date**: Unknown

**Keywords**: cartography, digital borders, platform power, sovereignty, algorithmic geography

## Elevator pitch
The essay argues that digital maps now wield state-like power, as platform decisions about borders and routing reshape sovereignty, rights, and everyday access.

## Takeaways
- Digital maps inherit cartography’s history as an instrument of state power and territorial control.
- Big Tech now controls map-making at scale, shifting sovereignty from states to corporations.
- Platform map outputs adjust to market access, reinforcing competing territorial narratives.
- Mapping choices can erase or marginalize communities, affecting services, mobility, and safety.
- Algorithmic geography extends into policing and labor, embedding bias into spatial systems.

## Synthesis
The article traces cartography’s long relationship with power and argues that digital maps have moved that power into the hands of Big Tech. Historically, political maps were tools to make land “legible” for taxation, conscription, and control. Early modern mapping practices claimed scientific objectivity while erasing indigenous presence and enabling conquest; straight-line borders were administrative conveniences that fractured communities. In this view, maps were never neutral: they were instruments for consolidating authority and shaping who belongs where.

In the contemporary era, the author contends that corporations have inherited this map-making power. The turning point was the commercialization of precise location data after the US ended GPS signal degradation in 2000. Google’s acquisition of Keyhole and the rise of commercial satellite operators expanded the ability of private firms to map the planet at scale. These firms operate across national jurisdictions and increasingly negotiate with states as near-equals. The article cites episodes where platforms or governments leveraged their power against each other—blocking news in Australia or seizing assets in Russia—as evidence of a new sovereignty dynamic in which corporate decisions can materially influence state outcomes.

Maps are highlighted as the clearest manifestation of this shift. Platform operators adjust borders depending on the viewer’s location and the company’s market interests. Kashmir appears as part of India inside India but as disputed inside Pakistan; Crimea appears as Russian inside Russia. These choices are framed not as political stances but as calculated decisions to preserve market access. Yet, by presenting these boundaries as authoritative cartography, platforms still legitimize state narratives and influence public perception.

The article argues that the consequences are not only geopolitical but also lived and local. When maps erase or obscure territories, they also erase people’s presence. The essay points to the partial or missing representation of Palestine on major platforms, and to routing and navigation decisions that steer some users away from certain neighborhoods. These effects ripple into local economies and everyday mobility, as routing choices can divert traffic and limit access to services.

Beyond mapping borders, the essay extends the critique to algorithmic geography: delivery platforms and ride-hailing systems that use geofencing, pricing algorithms, and surveillance to shape how workers and residents move through cities. Predictive policing is presented as a further extension, where historical bias becomes spatialized through heat maps that mark certain neighborhoods as inherently high-risk. The broader argument is that when platforms control the map, they control the terms of belonging—who is visible, who is served, and who is treated as a risk.

Ultimately, the piece frames digital mapping as a new frontier of sovereignty. States once held the authority to draw and enforce boundaries; now corporate platforms mediate those boundaries, often with minimal accountability. The essay calls attention to the political and ethical stakes of this shift: maps are not just navigation tools, but governance infrastructures that can grant or deny access to rights, resources, and recognition.
