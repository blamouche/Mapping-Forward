# New UN-backed atlas maps migratory lifelines of highly vulnerable bird species across the Americas
**Source**: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1121605
**Date**: March 26, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: migratory birds, flyways, atlas, conservation, eBird

## Elevator pitch
A UN-backed Americas Flyways Atlas uses millions of eBird observations and modeling to map critical migratory bird hotspots, giving policymakers a shared evidence base to target conservation across borders.

## Takeaways
- The Americas Flyways Atlas maps breeding, stopover, and wintering hotspots for 89 vulnerable species.
- It is built from billions of citizen-science observations processed with scientific models.
- The tool identifies “Bird Concentration Areas” to prioritize conservation action.
- The atlas is designed to support international policy coordination at CMS COP15.
- It highlights the fragility of cross-border habitat chains needed by migratory species.

## Synthesis
The release announces the Americas Flyways Atlas, an online platform developed by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology and the Convention on the Conservation of Migratory Species of Wild Animals. The atlas maps the annual journeys of an initial 89 highly vulnerable migratory bird species across the Americas, with the goal of pinpointing the most critical breeding, stopover, and wintering sites. It is positioned as a practical decision tool for governments, conservationists, and scientists, providing a shared evidence base to coordinate actions across national boundaries.

The atlas is built on a massive body of citizen-science data collected through eBird. Those observations—over two billion—are combined with advanced modeling to identify “Bird Concentration Areas,” or hotspots where large numbers of at‑risk species gather during key phases of migration. The emphasis is on turning distributed, volunteer-generated data into actionable spatial intelligence that can guide targeted conservation interventions.

The release situates the atlas within the broader context of the CMS COP15 meeting in Brazil. It frames the platform as a response to growing concern about the global decline of migratory species and the fragility of the chain of habitats that support them. Across the Americas’ flyways, hundreds of migratory species rely on ecosystems spanning 56 countries, making the success of conservation efforts dependent on coordinated international planning. The atlas aims to close a coordination gap by making those ecological linkages visible at a continental scale.

Specific examples in the release underscore the diversity of species and habitats involved, from Arctic-breeding shorebirds like the Hudsonian godwit to high-Andean flamingos and forest songbirds like the Cerulean warbler. The message is that a single broken link—such as a degraded wetland or fragmented forest—can undermine entire migration routes and populations. By mapping these dependencies, the atlas offers a way to prioritize the most consequential sites for protection and restoration.

The tool’s design purpose is explicitly policy-oriented. It is described as supporting ongoing negotiations to list additional species and strengthen international cooperation on habitat protection and ecological connectivity. The platform’s data and visualizations are intended to inform real-world actions, helping parties decide where conservation investments and regulatory attention can deliver the greatest impact.

Overall, the release positions the Americas Flyways Atlas as a convergence of citizen science, spatial modeling, and policy need. It highlights the value of open, shared mapping of ecological corridors and presents the atlas as an instrument to translate broad conservation goals into location-specific priorities across the hemisphere.
