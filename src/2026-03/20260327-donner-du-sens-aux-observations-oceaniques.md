## Elevator pitch
The EU-funded OBAMA-NEXT project turns diverse ocean observations into standardized, decision-ready information products—high‑resolution maps, habitat models, and indicators that help policymakers manage marine ecosystems.

## Takeaways
- OBAMA-NEXT focuses on transforming raw ocean data into usable information products for management and policy.
- The project combines satellites, drones, in‑situ sensors, eDNA, machine learning, and statistical modeling.
- Standardized algorithms help convert heterogeneous observations into comparable, high‑resolution outputs.
- Information products include habitat and species distribution models, coastal vegetation maps, and fish spawning predictions.
- Co‑design with stakeholders ensures alignment with EU marine policy frameworks and real‑world needs.

## Synthesis
OBAMA-NEXT addresses a recurring gap in marine observation: the world is collecting more data than ever, but decision‑makers still lack consistent, actionable information. The project’s core contribution is not a new sensor or dataset, but a systematic approach for translating heterogeneous observations into standardized information products. By focusing on algorithmic pipelines that turn “raw” ocean data into comparable, policy‑ready outputs, OBAMA-NEXT aims to make marine monitoring useful at the pace and scale required for climate adaptation, biodiversity protection, and resource governance.

The project builds on decades of global ocean observing efforts such as GOOS, but expands the toolbox with newer technologies. It integrates satellite imagery, drones, in‑situ measurements, environmental DNA, and machine‑learning‑driven analytics. The emphasis is on combining these sources to improve both the spatial resolution and ecological relevance of outputs. Instead of isolated datasets, the project delivers coherent products like habitat suitability models, species distribution maps, and coastal vegetation layers that can be used across sites and agencies.

A key element is standardization. OBAMA-NEXT develops shared algorithms to harmonize data processing across multiple observation methods and locations. This standardization matters because policy decisions depend on comparability—maps and indicators must be consistent across regions, time periods, and observing platforms. Without shared processing rules, a high‑resolution map in one region may not be comparable to another, undermining continent‑scale monitoring and management.

The project also prioritizes stakeholder co‑design. Policymakers, conservation managers, and practitioners participate in defining the information products and evaluating their usefulness. This iterative approach links technical outputs to real decision contexts, such as the EU’s Marine Strategy Framework Directive and related protected‑area and biodiversity initiatives. The result is a product suite that is explicitly aligned with policy frameworks rather than being a purely scientific exercise.

OBAMA-NEXT demonstrates its approach across 12 “learning sites” that represent Europe’s major marine regions: the Atlantic, Baltic, Black Sea, and Mediterranean. The sites were chosen not just for ecological diversity, but for data availability—high‑resolution products require dense observations, so the project targeted areas where advanced monitoring infrastructure already exists. This pragmatic selection strengthens the reliability of the outputs and provides testbeds for scaling methods elsewhere.

Beyond mapping habitats and species, the project explores operational monitoring needs. For example, it develops tools for assessing coastal vegetation changes, forecasting fish spawning zones, and using AI‑assisted drone analysis to estimate macroplastic accumulation on beaches. These examples highlight how information products can support both long‑term ecological management and immediate operational decisions such as cleanup priorities.

Overall, OBAMA-NEXT reframes ocean observation as an information‑product pipeline rather than a data‑collection exercise. By standardizing algorithms, integrating diverse sensors, and embedding stakeholder requirements, it aims to deliver high‑resolution, policy‑ready insights that improve marine stewardship. The project’s emphasis on translation—from data to decisions—positions it as a model for future ocean monitoring programs that need to scale outputs across jurisdictions while remaining directly useful to practitioners.
