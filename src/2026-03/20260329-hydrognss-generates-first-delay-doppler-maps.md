# HydroGNSS generates first Delay Doppler Maps

**Date**: March 29, 2026
**Author**: Unknown

## Elevator pitch
ESA’s HydroGNSS mission is already producing Delay Doppler Maps from reflected navigation signals, demonstrating how compact satellites can map water signals across land and sea during early commissioning.

## Takeaways
- HydroGNSS uses reflected GNSS signals (GNSS‑R) to detect water signatures on Earth’s surface.
- The mission produces Delay Doppler Maps that visualize reflection strength across time and space.
- Early tracks show strong reflections over France, the Mediterranean, and North Africa.
- Additional passes over Tanzania and Malawi highlight rivers and lakes in the derived maps.
- The mission is delivering usable scientific data just months after launch, even before full commissioning ends.

## Synthesis
The HydroGNSS mission is demonstrating how GNSS‑Reflectometry can turn everyday navigation signals into a remote‑sensing tool for water monitoring. Just three months after launch, ESA’s twin HydroGNSS satellites are already generating Delay Doppler Maps—two‑dimensional representations of reflected signal power over delay and Doppler frequency—that reveal surface water signatures across land and sea. This early success suggests the mission’s core technique is working well in orbit and can produce scientifically useful outputs even before the satellites reach full operational status.

HydroGNSS exploits reflections from navigation satellites to “scout” for water. Compared with traditional active radar or optical sensors, GNSS‑R has the advantage of using existing signal sources and lightweight payloads, enabling compact satellites to deliver broad coverage at relatively low cost. The Delay Doppler Maps encode how signals scatter off Earth’s surface; strong reflections often indicate smoother or wetter surfaces, making the maps valuable for detecting rivers, lakes, wetlands, soil moisture, and flooded areas.

The video highlights tracks from HydroGNSS‑2 over France, the Mediterranean Sea, and North Africa. In these passes, orange and red segments mark stronger reflections, and the corresponding instantaneous maps show how the signal responds at dual polarization and dual frequency. The ability to capture dual‑frequency and dual‑polarization information is significant because it can improve discrimination between land cover types and surface conditions, potentially enhancing hydrological inference compared with single‑channel observations.

Additional tracks from HydroGNSS‑1 over Tanzania and Malawi demonstrate the mission’s sensitivity to inland water bodies. The Delay Doppler Maps align with rivers and lakes, indicating the system can identify hydrological features over complex terrain. This is promising for regions where conventional monitoring is sparse or where cloud cover and rapid hydrological change make optical imagery less reliable.

Beyond the immediate visualizations, the mission’s early outputs signal broader potential. GNSS‑R data can support near‑real‑time flood detection, soil moisture estimation, and seasonal water‑availability monitoring. When combined with other Earth‑observation data, HydroGNSS could contribute to more robust hydrological models and climate‑resilience planning. The fact that meaningful products are already emerging during commissioning suggests a short path to operational utility.

Overall, HydroGNSS is validating a flexible, low‑cost approach to global water monitoring. By transforming reflected navigation signals into actionable maps, the mission expands the remote‑sensing toolkit and offers a path toward more frequent, globally consistent water observations. As calibration and processing mature, the mission’s Delay Doppler Maps are likely to evolve from compelling visuals into routine inputs for hydrological science and decision‑making.
