# Geologic Map of the Emmons Lake Volcanic Center, Alaska
**Source**: https://www.usgs.gov/maps/geologic-map-emmons-lake-volcanic-center-alaska
**Date**: April 1, 2026
**Author**: Thomas P. Miller, Christopher F. Waythomas, Margaret T. Mangan, Frank A. Trusdell, Andrew T. Calvert
**Keywords**: USGS, geologic map, Alaska, volcanic center, Emmons Lake, Pavlof Volcano, stratovolcano

## Elevator pitch
The USGS releases a detailed geologic map of the Emmons Lake volcanic center in Alaska — a complex of stratovolcanoes and calderas anchored by Pavlof Volcano, one of Alaska's most historically active volcanoes.

## Takeaways
- The Emmons Lake volcanic center comprises a cluster of stratovolcanoes and calderas on the Alaska Peninsula
- Pavlof Volcano is identified as one of the most historically active volcanoes in Alaska over the past 300 years
- Underlying geology spans Late Jurassic through early Tertiary sedimentary formations
- Quaternary glaciation has substantially shaped the landscape and interacted with volcanic activity
- Map is published as USGS Scientific Investigations Map 3519 (DOI: 10.3133/sim3519)

## Synthesis
The USGS has published a new geologic map of the Emmons Lake volcanic center, a spatially clustered group of stratovolcanoes and calderas located on the southwestern Alaska Peninsula. The map, authored by a team of five volcanologists and geologists, provides the most comprehensive cartographic documentation of this geologically complex and hazardous region.

The Emmons Lake volcanic center encompasses several distinct volcanic features: Pavlof Sister, Pavlof Volcano, Little Pavlof, Double Crater, Mount Hague, and Mount Emmons. Of these, Pavlof Volcano stands out as the dominant hazard — it is characterized as one of the most historically active volcanoes in Alaska over the past three centuries, and regularly poses risks to aviation, local communities, and marine traffic in the region.

The underlying geology reveals a layered geological history. Beneath the volcanic edifices lie sedimentary rocks from the Late Jurassic through early Tertiary, including the Naknek Formation and Belkofski Formation, composed primarily of sandstone, siltstone, and conglomerate. These pre-volcanic rocks represent the basement that the volcanic center built upon over geological time.

The Quaternary glaciations that characterized much of Alaska's recent geological history have profoundly shaped the Emmons Lake landscape. The interaction between volcanic activity and glacial ice — including phenomena like phreatomagmatic eruptions triggered by volcanic heat melting glacier ice — is a defining characteristic of the area and a significant factor in hazard assessment.

Geologic maps of active volcanic centers serve multiple practical purposes beyond academic documentation. For emergency management agencies, aviation safety authorities, and local governments in the Alaska Peninsula region, accurate geological mapping provides the foundational spatial data necessary for eruption hazard zonation, evacuation planning, and risk communication. The DOI-registered publication ensures long-term accessibility of this data for researchers, planners, and the public.
