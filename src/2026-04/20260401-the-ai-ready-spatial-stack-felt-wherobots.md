## Elevator pitch
Wherobots and Felt are redefining the GIS landscape by building an AI-ready spatial stack that combines cloud-native data processing with collaborative mapping tools.

## Takeaways
- The traditional GIS stack is being replaced by cloud-native, AI-ready spatial platforms
- Wherobots provides scalable spatial data processing built on Apache Sedona
- Felt offers collaborative, browser-based mapping for non-technical users
- Together they represent a new spatial data pipeline from ingestion to visualization
- The emerging AI spatial stack prioritizes accessibility, scale, and real-time collaboration

## Synthesis
The article from GeoAwesome examines how two companies — Wherobots and Felt — are at the forefront of transforming traditional GIS into an AI-ready spatial stack. This shift reflects broader trends in geospatial technology, where cloud-native architectures and AI integration are displacing legacy desktop and server-based GIS workflows.

Wherobots, built on top of Apache Sedona (an open-source spatial computing system), focuses on the data processing layer. It enables large-scale spatial analytics at cloud scale, making it possible to process massive geospatial datasets with the distributed computing paradigms familiar to data engineers. The platform is designed to integrate seamlessly with existing data infrastructure, lowering the barriers for organizations that want to incorporate spatial analysis without adopting entirely new toolchains.

Felt, on the other hand, addresses the visualization and collaboration side of the spatial stack. Its browser-based, no-code mapping interface democratizes cartography by enabling non-GIS specialists — planners, analysts, communicators — to create and share maps without deep technical knowledge. This approach contrasts sharply with legacy tools like ArcGIS Desktop, which required installation, licensing, and significant training.

The convergence of these two platforms illustrates a broader architectural pattern emerging in the spatial industry: a separation of concerns between data processing (Wherobots) and presentation/collaboration (Felt), connected by open standards and APIs. This modularity mirrors trends seen in the broader data engineering world, where specialized tools handle ingestion, transformation, and visualization independently.

What makes this stack "AI-ready" is its design philosophy. Both platforms are built to ingest, process, and display AI-generated spatial outputs — whether from computer vision models detecting objects in satellite imagery, predictive models forecasting urban growth, or LLM-driven spatial queries. The ability to feed AI model outputs directly into a spatial workflow without manual export/import cycles is a key differentiator.

This article signals an important industry inflection point: GIS is no longer primarily a desktop discipline for specialists, but an infrastructure layer that AI systems and business analysts can access directly. The implications for enterprise GIS adoption, urban planning, environmental monitoring, and logistics are significant.
