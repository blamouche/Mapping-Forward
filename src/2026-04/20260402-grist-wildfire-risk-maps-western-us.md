# These Maps Show Exactly Where the West Might Burn This Summer
**Source**: https://grist.org/extreme-weather/these-maps-show-exactly-where-the-west-might-burn-this-summer/
**Date**: April 2, 2026
**Author**: Tik Root
**Keywords**: wildfire, risk maps, Western US, NICC, snowpack, drought, climate, fire forecast

## Elevator pitch
Updated wildfire forecasts from the National Interagency Coordination Center show dramatically escalated fire risk across the Western US for summer 2026, driven by record-early snowmelt, historic heat, and severe drought conditions.

## Takeaways
- The National Interagency Coordination Center's April forecast shows unprecedented fire risk expansion across the Southwest, Rockies, Pacific Northwest, and Northern California
- The Four Corners region experienced snowmelt four to six weeks earlier than any previously recorded date
- Albuquerque recorded its earliest 90-degree temperature on March 21, more than six weeks earlier than the prior record from 1925
- Simultaneous fires across multiple regions could overwhelm firefighting resources, as crews depend on moving between hot spots
- Hoped-for spring precipitation remains the primary potential mitigating factor

## Synthesis
Grist's Tik Root reports on updated wildfire risk forecasts from the National Interagency Coordination Center (NICC) that paint a striking picture of elevated fire danger across the Western United States for summer 2026. The maps, which use color gradients to indicate risk levels, show red spreading across the Southwest and into the Rockies, Pacific Northwest, and Northern California — a significant expansion from earlier projections.

The forecast is driven by a convergence of three exceptional conditions. First, snowpack deficit: the Four Corners region experienced snowmelt not just weeks but four to six weeks earlier than the previously recorded earliest melt-off dates — a remarkable departure from historical records. Early snowmelt means drier ground entering the fire season, extending the window during which vegetation can dry out and become fuel.

Second, unprecedented heat: Albuquerque recorded its earliest 90-degree temperature on March 21, more than six weeks earlier than the prior record from 1925. Early-season heat accelerates vegetation drying and increases ignition risk. Third, overall drought conditions: the region faces less snowpack and higher temperatures than nearly any previously recorded winter, creating conditions where even normally resilient landscapes face elevated fire risk.

Matthew Hurteau, director of the University of New Mexico's Center for Fire Resilient Ecosystems, provides critical operational context: simultaneous fires across multiple regions create a resource allocation crisis, as firefighting crews are typically deployed by moving between active hot spots. When multiple regions burn simultaneously, the ability to concentrate resources where most needed is severely constrained.

From a mapping perspective, this article illustrates the critical role of risk visualization in emergency preparedness communication. The NICC's monthly outlook maps translate complex multi-variable forecasting models — integrating snowpack data, temperature anomalies, drought indices, and fuel moisture models — into accessible visual communications that support decision-making by emergency managers, land use planners, and the public. The power and limitation of such maps is their necessary simplification of continuous risk gradients into discrete color categories for public communication.
