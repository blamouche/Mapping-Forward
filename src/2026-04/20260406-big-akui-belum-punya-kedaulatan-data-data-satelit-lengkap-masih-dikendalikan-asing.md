# BIG Akui Belum Punya Kedaulatan Data, Data Satelit Lengkap Masih Dikendalikan Asing
**Source**: https://spatialhighlights.com/news/big-akui-belum-punya-kedaulatan-data-data-satelit-lengkap-masih-dikendalikan-asing
**Date**: 2026-04-06
**Author**: Spatial Highlights
**Keywords**: Indonesia, geospatial data, satellite imagery, sovereignty, BIG, radar, defense

## Elevator pitch
Indonesia’s data-sovereignty debate is widening from consumer maps to remote sensing, with officials arguing that the country still lacks control over the most complete satellite and geospatial data about its own territory.

## Takeaways
- BIG says Indonesia still relies on foreign platforms such as Google Maps and HERE Maps for the most complete geospatial datasets.
- The RUU Satu Data discussion is positioning geospatial capability as a strategic state function rather than a narrow mapping problem.
- Cloud cover makes optical imagery less reliable in Indonesia, raising the importance of radar-based Earth observation.
- Aircraft-based acquisition is presented as a practical route to very high positional accuracy when satellite options are limited.
- Officials connect sovereign mapping capacity directly to defense targeting, territorial monitoring, and regular data refresh cycles.

## Synthesis
This article extends Indonesia’s geospatial sovereignty debate by focusing on who controls the country’s most complete geographic and satellite data. BIG’s acting chief argues that Indonesia still does not fully command either the technologies or the datasets needed to map its territory independently. In that framing, dependence on platforms such as Google Maps and HERE Maps is not just a market issue. It is evidence that a critical layer of national information infrastructure still sits outside direct domestic control.

The political setting matters. The comments were made during discussions around the RUU Satu Data, which turns what might otherwise look like an administrative modernization problem into a legislative and institutional one. The article suggests that the debate is no longer limited to better public-sector data sharing. It is becoming a broader argument about whether Indonesia should build sovereign geospatial pipelines, from acquisition to storage to operational use. That shift matters because laws and procurement rules often determine whether national mapping capacity remains fragmented or becomes a coordinated public asset.

A second theme is the constraint imposed by physical geography. Indonesia’s heavy cloud cover weakens the usefulness of conventional optical satellite imagery for consistent national coverage. The article therefore points toward radar as a more realistic strategic capability because it can operate through clouds and support regular monitoring. It also highlights aircraft as an alternative for high-resolution acquisition. Together, those examples imply that sovereignty will not come from copying a single foreign platform. It will require a mixed sensing stack adapted to local terrain and weather conditions.

The article also makes the defense connection explicit. Arief frames autonomous mapping and imaging capacity as part of national security, because targeting, territorial awareness, and rapid updates all depend on trusted spatial data. That broadens the significance of the story for the mapping sector. It signals continued demand for sovereign geospatial infrastructure that blends civil administration, remote sensing, and security use cases rather than treating them as separate domains.
