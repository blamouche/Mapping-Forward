# BIG ke DPR: Data Satelit RI Masih Bergantung Asing
**Source**: https://www.beritasatu.com/nasional/2982651/big-ke-dpr-data-satelit-ri-masih-bergantung-asing
**Date**: 2026-04-06
**Author**: Beritasatu.com
**Keywords**: Indonesia, satellite data, data sovereignty, BIG, geospatial policy, radar imaging

## Elevator pitch
BIG told Indonesian lawmakers that the country still relies on foreign satellite and mapping platforms, underscoring how strategic geospatial data remains externally controlled.

## Takeaways
- BIG says Indonesia still lacks full sovereignty over satellite-derived geospatial data.
- The article frames foreign dependence as a strategic risk for national planning and defense.
- Cloud cover is identified as a structural obstacle for optical satellite mapping across Indonesia.
- Radar satellites and aircraft are presented as alternatives for more reliable and precise geospatial acquisition.
- The Single Data bill is portrayed as a possible lever for strengthening domestic geospatial capability.

## Synthesis
Beritasatu’s version condenses the parliamentary exchange into a clearer strategic narrative: Indonesia depends on foreign satellite and mapping platforms for foundational geospatial data, and that dependence limits national autonomy. The article treats spatial data as a form of infrastructure, not just content, which is important because it places mapping alongside other sovereign capabilities such as communications, defense systems, and critical digital services.

One useful element is the distinction between technological progress in general and sovereign access in practice. Satellite imagery keeps improving globally, but Indonesia does not automatically benefit from the most capable systems. The article points out that even when commercial imagery is available, geography still interferes. Dense cloud cover makes optical sensing unreliable for many routine needs, forcing agencies to look at radar or airborne acquisition if they want timely and accurate mapping. That shifts the conversation from generic innovation to operational fitness.

The article also links geospatial dependence to risk. If a country cannot control how quickly data is refreshed, how precisely objects are located, or what sensing modes are available in adverse conditions, it becomes harder to support defense, emergency response, infrastructure planning, or administrative modernization with confidence. In this sense, the argument is not only that foreign platforms own valuable data, but that they mediate access to a core layer of territorial knowledge.

The piece therefore reinforces a broader trend visible in many regions: mapping is being reclassified as strategic infrastructure. National basemap programs, sovereign cloud debates, and domestic satellite initiatives are increasingly part of the same policy conversation. Here, the Single Data bill becomes a possible institutional anchor for that shift, signaling that Indonesia may use legislative reform to align governance, sensing technology, and national data control more closely.
