# BIG: RI Belum Berdaulat Data Geospasial, Data Lengkap Punya Asing Semua
**Source**: https://nasional.kompas.com/read/2026/04/06/15542081/big-ri-belum-berdaulat-data-geospasial-data-lengkap-punya-asing-semua
**Date**: 2026-04-06
**Author**: Kompas.com
**Keywords**: Indonesia, geospatial data, satellite imagery, sovereignty, BIG, Google Maps, HERE Maps

## Elevator pitch
Indonesia’s geospatial agency says the country still depends on foreign mapping platforms and satellite capabilities, turning data sovereignty into a strategic policy issue.

## Takeaways
- BIG says complete geospatial datasets for Indonesia are still largely controlled by foreign platforms such as Google Maps and HERE Maps.
- Publicly accessible civil satellite imagery has improved, but the highest-resolution capabilities remain inaccessible.
- Indonesia’s cloud cover limits the usefulness of optical satellite imagery for routine mapping.
- Radar satellites and aircraft-based surveying are presented as practical complements for higher-quality mapping.
- The debate links geospatial data autonomy to defense, infrastructure planning, and faster national data updates.

## Synthesis
This article frames geospatial data as a sovereignty question rather than just a technical one. Indonesia’s acting geospatial agency chief argues that the country still lacks control over both the technologies and the underlying datasets required to map its own territory comprehensively. The striking point is not only that foreign firms dominate consumer mapping, but that the most complete and operationally useful data about Indonesia is still seen as sitting with outside platforms such as Google Maps and HERE Maps.

The discussion also highlights the uneven access built into the remote-sensing market. Satellite technology keeps improving, but the best capabilities are not equally available. Public civil imagery is already detailed, yet the article stresses that the most advanced systems remain outside Indonesia’s reach. That matters because data quality is not just about sharp pictures. For national basemaps, defense use cases, and precise large-scale mapping, positional accuracy and update frequency are as important as headline resolution.

Indonesia’s geography complicates the problem. Persistent cloud cover reduces the reliability of optical imagery, which means a country with strong need for current national-scale mapping cannot rely on one sensing mode alone. The article points to radar as a strategic alternative because it can image through clouds and operate day or night. It also notes that aircraft still matter for high-precision mapping, especially when very fine positional accuracy is required at large scales. In other words, the path to data sovereignty is likely to be hybrid rather than purely satellite-based.

The broader significance is political. By connecting geospatial capability to defense and national decision-making, the article turns mapping infrastructure into a matter of state capacity. The call to build domestic satellite capability is less about prestige than about reducing dependence on external platforms for strategic data. For the mapping ecosystem, that suggests continued pressure for national geospatial stacks that combine domestic sensing, sovereign data management, and operational update pipelines.
