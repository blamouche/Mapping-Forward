# Listening to the Land, and the People Who Know It Best
**Source**: https://www.geoweeknews.com/news/listening-to-the-land-and-the-people-who-know-it-best
**Date**: April 8, 2026
**Author**: Abigail Hart
**Keywords**: Colombia, GIS, land cover change, community knowledge, sustainable agriculture

## Elevator pitch
A Geo Week profile of researcher Paulina Vergara Buitrago shows how GIS-based land-change analysis becomes more useful when it is grounded in farmers’ knowledge, economic realities and community voice.

## Takeaways
- Paulina Vergara Buitrago is mapping twenty years of land cover change in Colombia’s páramo while pairing remote sensing with field interviews.
- The article argues that local farmers hold crucial knowledge about soil, crop rotation and ecosystem pressures that satellite data alone cannot reveal.
- It highlights how unsustainable land-use patterns are often driven by weak policy and market conditions rather than by ignorance within communities.
- Paulina’s research approach aims to preserve community narratives instead of rewriting them through academic bias.
- The piece frames geospatial research as more credible and actionable when results are returned to the people whose landscapes are being studied.

## Synthesis
This article profiles Paulina Vergara Buitrago, a University of Minnesota researcher using GIS to study long-term land cover change in Colombia’s central Andean páramo. The story is not mainly about a technical workflow, even though mapping and remote sensing are central to the work. Instead, it argues that geospatial analysis becomes more meaningful when it is combined with local ecological knowledge, especially in landscapes where environmental change is inseparable from farming livelihoods, public policy and economic constraint.

Paulina’s project tracks two decades of landscape change in a high-altitude region where potato farming, water resources and fragile ecosystems overlap. The article makes clear that satellite imagery and GIS provide only part of the explanation. The more difficult question is why change happens in the first place. By speaking directly with farmers, Paulina learns that many local practices already reflect a sophisticated understanding of environmental limits. Farmers know, for example, that short crop-rotation cycles degrade soils, yet they also operate under market pressures that force them into intensive production. The article therefore rejects simplistic narratives in which environmental degradation is caused by a lack of knowledge at the local level.

That point matters for geospatial practice because it reframes the role of mapping. In this account, maps are not the final authority on land systems; they are one layer of evidence that must be interpreted alongside lived experience. Paulina treats communities not as data sources to be mined, but as knowledge holders who can explain the mechanisms hidden behind land-cover signatures. This makes the research more diagnostic and potentially more useful for restoration or agricultural policy, since it ties visible environmental outcomes to the actual social and economic structures producing them.

Another important theme is representation. Paulina criticizes a common research pattern in which community testimony is collected and then rewritten through academic assumptions. Her aim is to preserve the words and logic of the people she works with rather than replace them with a cleaner external narrative. That approach positions the researcher as a translator and editor rather than the sole author of meaning. For geospatial work involving Indigenous or rural communities, this is a significant methodological stance: it treats narrative integrity as part of analytical rigor.

The article closes on the question of reciprocity. Paulina wants her findings to move back from the university to the farm, rather than circulating only in conferences and journals. For Mapping Forward, that is the key insight. Geospatial research gains value not only when it improves observation, but when it improves the conversation between institutions and the communities living inside the mapped landscape. The article presents GIS as a bridge between ecological evidence and local agency, not merely as a tool for extracting patterns from space.
