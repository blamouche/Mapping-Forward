# The Map Is Never Finished
**Source**: https://www.geoweeknews.com/news/the-map-is-never-finished
**Date**: April 8, 2026
**Author**: Abigail Hart
**Keywords**: geospatial workforce, leadership, cloud-native geospatial, quantum computing, diversity

## Elevator pitch
Three senior geospatial leaders argue that the field’s next phase will depend less on tools alone than on curiosity, translation skills, cloud-native practices and more inclusive leadership.

## Takeaways
- The article links future geospatial change to both frontier technologies such as quantum-enabled space systems and operational shifts such as cloud-native geospatial workflows.
- Dr. Shawana Johnson frames curiosity and constant learning as essential because expertise in geospatial quickly becomes outdated.
- Dr. Julia Wagemann argues that AI will change geospatial work rather than erase it, pushing practitioners toward contextual and critical thinking.
- Izabela Miller emphasizes that leadership in GIS depends on translating technical capability into organizational language, priorities and decisions.
- All three interviewees connect the industry’s workforce gap to inclusion, mentorship and stronger recognition of often invisible leadership labor.

## Synthesis
This article synthesizes three separate Geo Week interviews into a broader reflection on where the geospatial sector is heading and what kinds of people will shape it. Rather than focusing on a single product launch or technical milestone, it treats the industry as an evolving professional ecosystem. The piece brings together perspectives from Dr. Shawana Johnson, Dr. Julia Wagemann and Izabela Miller, each of whom works on a different layer of the geospatial stack: space and commercialization, workforce development and training, and organizational leadership.

One important theme is that the technical frontier is widening in multiple directions at once. Johnson points to quantum computing in space as a serious strategic development rather than a speculative headline. In her telling, the significance lies in what quantum-enhanced infrastructure could do for communications, precision navigation, timing and cybersecurity. Wagemann, by contrast, focuses on more immediate change inside geospatial practice itself, especially the move toward cloud-native workflows and standards such as STAC. Together, these examples show a field being reshaped both by frontier infrastructure and by maturing data conventions that make geospatial work more scalable and interoperable.

A second theme is that easier tools do not reduce the need for judgment. Wagemann explicitly pushes back on claims that AI will remove the need for geographers, arguing instead that automation shifts work upward toward interpretation, validation and decision support. Johnson makes a similar point from another angle, warning against over-reliance on generative tools and urging professionals to preserve independent thinking and writing. The article therefore treats technical progress as a reason to deepen human rigor, not bypass it.

Miller’s contribution grounds the discussion in organizational reality. She describes the transition many geospatial professionals face when they move from being expert problem-solvers to becoming leaders expected to manage priorities, budgets and executive conversations. Her central insight is that GIS leadership is not about having the most technical answers, but about building conditions for better decisions. That means framing maturity, risk and opportunity in language that non-specialists can act on. In that sense, the article presents strategic communication as core geospatial infrastructure, not as a secondary soft skill.

The final thread is workforce development and inclusion. All three interviewees describe a field that still struggles to welcome and retain enough talent, especially women and other underrepresented groups. The article argues that mentoring, sponsorship and recognition of invisible coordination work are not peripheral social concerns; they are practical requirements for sustaining capability in a niche industry with a persistent skills gap. Overall, the piece presents geospatial as a domain whose future will be shaped by the people who can connect innovation, institutions and community rather than by technology alone.
