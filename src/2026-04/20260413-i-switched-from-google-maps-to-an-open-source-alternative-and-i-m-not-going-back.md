# I switched from Google Maps to an open source alternative and I'm not going back
**Source**: https://www.howtogeek.com/switched-from-google-maps-to-open-source-alternative/
**Date**: April 13, 2026
**Author**: Nick Lewis
**Keywords**: OpenStreetMap, OsmAnd, privacy, offline navigation, Google Maps alternatives

## Elevator pitch
A How-To Geek editor argues that OsmAnd beats Google Maps for privacy, offline reliability, and user control.

## Takeaways
- The author switched because Google Maps felt cluttered, ad-heavy, and too intrusive while driving.
- OsmAnd is presented as a privacy-focused, open-source navigation tool built on OpenStreetMap.
- Offline routing is the default experience rather than an optional fallback.
- The article argues that OsmAnd often provides better detail for trails, remote roads, and outdoor use.
- The tradeoff is a more utilitarian tool, but one the author sees as more predictable and safer.

## Synthesis
How-To Geek makes a consumer case for moving from Google Maps to OsmAnd, an open-source navigation app built on OpenStreetMap. The author’s argument is not that Google Maps fails at navigation, but that it has drifted too far from that core task. Sponsored listings, unsolicited prompts, pop-ups while driving, and broad data collection have turned the app from a clean utility into what he sees as an overly busy, privacy-invasive product.

One trigger for the switch was Google Maps’ automatic rerouting behavior. The article describes a situation in which Google suggested a route change with a short timeout that defaulted to acceptance if the driver did not interact quickly enough. The author frames that as more than a nuisance: it becomes a safety problem because it demands attention while driving. Combined with unwanted toll-road behavior and the sense that Google’s design now privileges engagement over simplicity, it pushed him to look elsewhere.

OsmAnd is presented as the opposite design philosophy. It is open source, can be used without creating an account, and relies on OpenStreetMap’s community-maintained data rather than a corporate map platform. For the author, the biggest advantage is that offline navigation is not a secondary feature but the main mode of use. After downloading a region, searches, routing, and rerouting work without a live connection, which is particularly valuable in remote or outdoor settings where mobile data is unreliable.

The article also stresses that OsmAnd behaves more like a configurable tool than a mass-market discovery app. Users can tune map styles, overlays, routing behavior, and transport profiles to suit specific needs. For hikers, cyclists, and people traveling off the beaten path, the author argues that the app often offers richer trail and terrain detail than Google Maps. In this framing, OpenStreetMap’s community model becomes an advantage in places where local contributors care about path quality, elevation, and lesser-known roads.

The broader mapping takeaway is that the competition is no longer only about who has the largest map or the cleanest route engine. It is also about privacy, interface discipline, offline resilience, and user control. The article does not claim that OsmAnd is universally better, but it makes a clear case that a growing segment of users wants navigation without ads, identity-linked tracking, or constant cloud dependence. That reflects a larger opportunity for open mapping ecosystems to differentiate themselves against heavily commercialized consumer map platforms.
