# Pourquoi les trajets proposés sur Waze et Google Maps ne sont plus aussi courts qu’avant ?
**Source**: https://www.ouest-france.fr/leditiondusoir/2026-04-14/pourquoi-les-trajets-proposes-sur-waze-et-google-maps-ne-sont-plus-aussi-courts-qu-avant-ed508601-93f7-461a-9337-e0603dd5775a
**Date**: April 14, 2026
**Author**: L’édition du soir
**Keywords**: Waze, Google Maps, eco-routing, carbon emissions, traffic regulation

## Elevator pitch
French routing rules are pushing Waze and Google Maps to prioritize lower-emission and lower-congestion trips over raw travel time.

## Takeaways
- A 2022 French decree requires navigation services to highlight routes with the lowest greenhouse-gas impact.
- The change has recently become visible as platforms integrated those constraints more explicitly into their routing logic.
- Apps can now prefer slower alternatives, especially when they avoid higher-speed segments and emissions-heavy driving patterns.
- Traffic authorities such as Île-de-France Mobilités also pushed platforms to spread flows and reduce congestion on major axes.
- The shift turns mapping apps into policy instruments balancing convenience, emissions, and public traffic management.

## Synthesis
Ouest-France explains why some drivers now feel that Waze and Google Maps are no longer defaulting to the shortest or fastest routes. The article links the change to France’s decree no. 2022-1199, which requires driving-assistance services to prioritize itineraries with the lowest greenhouse-gas impact. What was once mostly a time-optimization product is therefore being nudged toward environmental optimization, with direct consequences for route ranking.

The piece notes that the policy only became obvious to users in recent weeks, when routing platforms started to operationalize the rule in a more visible way. The main route proposed is no longer necessarily the fastest one, but the one judged least polluting. Carbon footprint indicators are now surfaced to users so they can compare alternatives, making sustainability a first-class parameter in consumer navigation rather than a hidden calculation in the background.

Another practical effect is that certain fast road segments are de-emphasized. The article cites reporting that if a route depends on sections above 110 km/h, services may be expected to suggest alternatives with lower speeds. That means route engines are being asked to optimize for a different tradeoff: slightly longer travel times in exchange for lower emissions and, potentially, smoother traffic distribution. For mapping products, this is significant because it changes both user expectations and the meaning of “best route.”

The article also frames this as a traffic-management tool. Authorities in the Paris region pushed Google Maps and similar services to reduce concentration on the same corridors, especially around high-pressure periods such as the 2024 Olympics. In that sense, navigation apps are no longer just reflecting traffic conditions; they are participating in how traffic is distributed. Mapping platforms become intermediaries between public policy, infrastructure constraints, and driver behavior.

The backlash is understandable because Waze and Google Maps built their reputations on time savings and directness. Yet the story shows a broader shift in digital cartography: routing is becoming policy-aware. Navigation systems are being asked to encode climate priorities and congestion management, not merely compute the mathematically quickest path. That makes maps more consequential as governance infrastructure, while leaving users to decide whether they accept a greener recommendation or switch back to a faster option.
