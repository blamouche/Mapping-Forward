## Synthesis
Tchad Infos reports that Togo, acting on behalf of the African Union, plans to bring the “Correct The Map” initiative to the United Nations in an effort to move international institutions away from the Mercator projection and toward Equal Earth. The article frames the issue in strongly political terms: this is not just about cartographic technique, but about correcting symbolic distortions that make Africa appear smaller than its actual size.

At the heart of the argument is a familiar critique of Mercator. Designed for navigation, the projection preserves angles but exaggerates land masses near the poles and compresses equatorial regions. The article stresses how this distortion affects global perception, especially when widely used in education, media, and public imagination. Equal Earth is presented as a more balanced alternative that better represents relative continental sizes.

What makes the story notable is its institutional ambition. By preparing a resolution for the UN General Assembly, the campaign is trying to move projection politics from activist and educational circles into formal diplomatic space. That raises the stakes. If an international body endorses a different world map, the issue shifts from cartographic critique to symbolic governance: how should the world officially be visualized, and whose perspectives should that visualization center?

The article also uses the language of “justice cognitive,” which signals that map design is being interpreted as part of a broader postcolonial struggle over knowledge representation. In this view, the persistence of Mercator is not merely historical inertia. It reproduces an inherited visual hierarchy rooted in European navigation, colonial expansion, and normalized northern prominence.

For mapping and cartographic culture, the initiative is important because it shows that debates over projections remain alive even in an age dominated by interactive digital maps. Projection choice may seem abstract compared with routing or satellite layers, but world maps still shape educational narratives and geopolitical imagination. Togo’s effort to internationalize the issue demonstrates that cartography remains contested symbolic infrastructure. Maps do not merely describe the world; they help decide how the world is mentally ordered.
