## Synthesis
Sara Bronin’s essay introduces the National Preservation Atlas (NPA) as an ambitious attempt to consolidate and map historic-preservation data across the United States. The project’s central premise is simple but powerful: information about historic places is scattered across agencies, paper records, registers, handwritten nomination forms, and uneven local processes. Without a unified dataset, policymakers, preservationists, owners, and researchers lack a national view of what is protected, where it is located, and under which legal frameworks it falls.

The article emphasizes that the NPA is not only mapping places; it is also mapping the legal and regulatory regimes attached to them. That makes it more than a cultural inventory. It becomes a legal-geography platform. Users could identify whether a property qualifies for incentives or tax credits, see what kinds of protections apply in a jurisdiction, and analyze how preservation policy shapes land use and development. This expands the atlas from a heritage-visibility tool into an infrastructure for public decision-making.

A major theme of the piece is data quality and standardization. Bronin describes the painstaking work required to digitize preservation records: illegible scans, hand-drawn maps, vague boundary descriptions, inconsistent formats, demolished buildings still listed on registers, and incomplete georeferencing. In that sense, the article is also about the hidden labor of building a usable map from institutional archives. The atlas is valuable precisely because it imposes structure, comparability, and quality control on materials that were never designed for national analysis.

The essay also suggests that the atlas can change preservation from a reactive discipline into a proactive one. Instead of scrambling late in planning processes to discover whether a site is protected, architects, planners, and agencies could consult a synthesized map early. Likewise, owners might discover eligibility for preservation incentives that they would otherwise miss. That functional framing is important: the map is meant to guide action, not just document heritage.

Finally, Bronin highlights the narrative and political dimension. By mapping what has been protected and comparing it to what has been overlooked, the NPA could expose biases in official heritage recognition. The project therefore sits at the intersection of GIS, legal analysis, public history, and social equity. It shows how atlas-building can become a way to interrogate not only where history is located, but whose history has been made visible by institutions.
