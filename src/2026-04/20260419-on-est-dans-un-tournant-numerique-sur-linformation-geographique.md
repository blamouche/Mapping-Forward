## Synthesis
RTS frames cartography as a political technology rather than a neutral mirror of geography. Drawing on comments from political geographer Frédéric Giraut, the article argues that the current moment is not just another incremental improvement in mapping, but a structural digital shift in who produces and controls geographic information. Historically, states were the sovereign makers of official maps. Today, those state-produced representations still exist, but the maps most people actually consult are the ones produced and distributed by major platforms such as Google and Apple.

The article uses Crimea to illustrate the stakes. Google Maps shows the peninsula differently depending on where the user is located, presenting it as Russian territory for Russian users while treating it as disputed elsewhere. Google frames this as compliance with local law and a form of neutrality, but the piece challenges that logic. Giraut’s point is that adapting cartographic representation to national legal demands can end up normalizing occupations or contested borders in ways that diverge from international law. In other words, platform cartography is not post-political; it embeds geopolitical compromises directly into user interfaces.

The second half broadens the discussion beyond digital platforms to world maps and projections. The article revisits the long-running critique of Mercator, noting that projection choice, centering, and even the convention of putting north at the top all contribute to visual hierarchies. These are not merely technical decisions. They can produce symbolic dominance by enlarging some spaces, marginalizing others, and reinforcing historically Eurocentric ways of seeing the world. The mention of the Equal Earth campaign supported by the African Union shows how projection debates remain active and politically charged.

A useful contribution of the article is that it connects local product decisions in digital maps with older cartographic debates about worldview, power, and representation. The transition from state cartography to platform cartography does not eliminate political bias; it relocates it. Territorial disputes, naming controversies, and projection choices are now filtered through platform governance, local-law compliance, and interface design. For anyone tracking the mapping sector, the piece is a reminder that competition in maps is not only about better routing or fresher POIs. It is also about who gets to define the visible world, for whom, and according to which legal or political compromises.
