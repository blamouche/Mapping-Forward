# Google Maps a supprimé 292 millions d’avis frauduleux en 2025
**Source**: https://www.abondance.com/20260420-2186214-google-maps-suppression-292-millions-avis-frauduleux-2025.html
**Date**: 2026-04-20
**Author**: Johan Sellitto
**Keywords**: Google Maps, moderation, fake reviews, Gemini, business profiles

## Elevator pitch
This article shows Google Maps becoming a more aggressively moderated trust platform, with Gemini helping block fraudulent reviews, fake business profiles and suspicious edits at scale.

## Takeaways
- Google says it removed or blocked 292 million fraudulent reviews in 2025, up 21% from 2024.
- The platform also removed more than 13 million fake business profiles and blocked 79 million inaccurate or unverified edits.
- Gemini is presented as a reasoning layer that can catch nuanced policy violations before publication.
- Google now uses temporary review freezes, owner alerts and user-facing banners when suspicious review spikes occur.
- The article notes that Google no longer discloses visual-content moderation figures, leaving part of the picture opaque.

## Synthesis
This Abondance article reviews Google’s 2025 Google Maps safety report and frames it as evidence that moderation on mapping platforms is becoming more industrialized and more dependent on AI. The headline figure is striking: Google says it removed or blocked 292 million fraudulent reviews in 2025, a 21 percent increase from the previous year. The article places that number in a longer trend line, showing a sharp rise from earlier years and suggesting that the company’s detection capabilities are expanding quickly.

The text makes an important distinction. A higher number of removals does not necessarily prove that fraud itself is rising at the same rate. It may instead indicate that Google is getting better at finding abuse. That is where Gemini enters the story. According to the article, Google credits its advanced AI model with identifying problematic content before publication, including cases involving local nuance or political and social language that older filtering approaches might miss. In other words, moderation is shifting from reactive cleanup to anticipatory screening.

The article also explains how Google responds when suspicious activity clusters around a business listing. Rather than simply deleting bad reviews after the fact, the company may remove fraudulent content, pause new reviews temporarily, notify the business owner and display a banner to users. This matters because trust on Maps is not just about geospatial accuracy. It also concerns whether the reputational layer around places remains credible enough for consumers and merchants to rely on.

Another major theme is business profile integrity. Google reportedly blocked 79 million inaccurate or unverified edits in 2025, a reminder that place data itself is a large attack surface. The report also promises more proactive email alerts for verified owners so they can review major changes before publication. That suggests Google is trying to build a tighter governance loop between platform automation and business oversight.

One useful nuance in the article is its mention of missing transparency. Google no longer reports how many fraudulent photos or videos it has removed, whereas earlier reports gave at least partial figures. That omission leaves uncertainty around visual moderation, even as the company highlights gains elsewhere.

Overall, the article presents Google Maps less as a passive directory and more as a managed trust infrastructure for local discovery. Reviews, place edits and business profiles all shape commercial visibility, and Google is using Gemini to control those layers more assertively. The takeaway is that the competitive value of a mapping platform now depends not just on location accuracy, but also on how effectively it protects the credibility of the information wrapped around each place.
