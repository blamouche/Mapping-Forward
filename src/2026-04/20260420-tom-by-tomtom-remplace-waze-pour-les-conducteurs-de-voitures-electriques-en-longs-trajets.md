# TOM by TomTom remplace Waze pour les conducteurs de voitures électriques en longs trajets
**Source**: https://www.tuxboard.com/tom-by-tomtom-remplace-waze-pour-les-conducteurs-de-voitures-electriques-en-longs-trajets/
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Christophe Cohen
**Keywords**: TomTom, electric vehicles, navigation, Waze, onboard systems

## Elevator pitch
This article argues that TomTom’s TOM device fills a specific gap for EV drivers by adding road alerts to native in-car navigation without sacrificing battery-aware route planning.

## Takeaways
- The article presents TOM by TomTom as a companion device rather than a replacement for built-in car navigation.
- It argues that EV drivers need native navigation to preserve battery preconditioning and energy management.
- The product’s main value is delivering traffic and hazard alerts similar to community navigation apps.
- TomTom is framed as better aligned with modern connected vehicles than a smartphone-only navigation setup.
- The piece suggests that screenless, low-distraction alerts can be useful on long-distance EV journeys.

## Synthesis
This Tuxboard article makes the case for TOM by TomTom as a complementary navigation accessory designed for a very specific use case: long trips in electric vehicles. Its premise is that while smartphone apps such as Waze remain strong for community-based traffic alerts, they can become suboptimal in EV contexts where the car’s native navigation system plays a direct role in battery management. The article therefore presents TOM not as a replacement for embedded navigation, but as a bridge between onboard route intelligence and real-time road incident awareness.

A core argument concerns the different needs of electric driving. On a long EV journey, route planning is tied to energy strategy, charging stops and sometimes battery preconditioning ahead of fast charging. According to the article, these functions are best handled by the vehicle’s native navigation stack, which has deeper access to vehicle systems than an external app. That means drivers face a tradeoff if they switch entirely to Waze or a phone-based app: they may gain useful live alerts, but lose tighter coordination with the car’s energy logic.

TOM by TomTom is presented as a way around that compromise. The device reportedly works alongside the built-in GPS, relying on app-connected TomTom traffic intelligence while avoiding the distraction of another full screen interface. The article emphasizes the object’s simplicity, describing it as a small screenless box that lights up and emits alerts discreetly. In that framing, TomTom’s product is not trying to dominate the dashboard but to extend the native system where it is weakest, namely in surfacing road incidents and danger zones in the style users associate with community-driven navigation tools.

The article also shows how navigation products are being rethought for software-rich vehicles. Instead of one application trying to do everything, there may be value in layered navigation experiences where each component handles what it does best. The embedded system manages state of charge, charging logic and route coherence, while an auxiliary layer contributes fresh traffic warnings and low-friction situational awareness.

Overall, the piece is favorable to TomTom and aimed at a broad consumer audience, but it points to a real product design question in the EV era. As cars become more integrated and more dependent on native software for energy optimization, smartphone navigation can no longer be judged only by route quality or interface polish. TomTom’s TOM is framed here as a response to that shift, tailored to drivers who want Waze-like alerts without breaking the intelligence of the vehicle itself.
