# Le Togo va demander aux États membres de l’ONU d’utiliser une carte représentant la taille réelle de l’Afrique
**Source**: https://www.bbc.com/afrique/articles/cn43ndy7qxzo
**Date**: 2026-04-21
**Author**: Abdou Aziz Diédhiou, Noel Kokou Tadegnon, Catarina Demony
**Keywords**: Africa, cartography, Mercator, Equal Earth, United Nations

## Elevator pitch
This article shows map projection becoming a geopolitical issue, as the African Union and Togo push the UN to abandon Mercator in favor of an Equal Earth view that better reflects Africa’s real size.

## Takeaways
- The African Union supports the “Correct the Map” campaign against the Mercator projection.
- Togo has been mandated to table a UN resolution promoting the Equal Earth projection.
- Advocates argue that Mercator distorts Africa’s size and shapes global perceptions in education, media and politics.
- Supporters frame the issue as scientific accuracy and cognitive justice rather than symbolic cartographic reform.
- The debate shows how map projections still carry political and cultural power far beyond technical cartography.

## Synthesis
This BBC Afrique article examines the political campaign led by the African Union and carried diplomatically by Togo to replace the Mercator world map in international institutions with the Equal Earth projection. At first glance, the issue may seem purely technical, but the article argues that map projection is deeply tied to power, perception and representation. The central criticism is that Mercator, while historically useful for navigation, dramatically distorts landmass sizes, making Africa appear much smaller relative to northern regions than it really is.

The article notes that Africa covers more than 30 million square kilometers, yet on many familiar maps it appears similar in size to Greenland, which is vastly smaller. For campaigners, this is not an innocent visual quirk. They argue that repeated exposure to this distortion affects how the continent is imagined in classrooms, media narratives and political discourse. In that sense, the debate moves beyond cartographic design into the domain of symbolic power and collective cognition.

Togo’s role is especially important. The country has been mandated by the African Union to present a resolution at the next United Nations General Assembly calling for wider adoption of a projection that better reflects Africa’s true scale. The article cites Togolese foreign minister Robert Dussey, who frames the initiative as a matter of scientific truth and institutional recognition. Supporters insist that the aim is not to establish a hierarchy among world regions, but to correct a longstanding visual bias embedded in official and educational materials.

The article also links this campaign to broader ideas such as “cognitive justice” and even “decolonising geography.” Those phrases highlight how cartographic conventions can reinforce inherited worldviews, especially when they continue to privilege historical European norms in supposedly neutral global imagery. The Equal Earth projection is presented as a more faithful compromise for general world representation because it reduces size distortions while remaining readable.

Importantly, the article shows that this is not just an activist campaign operating outside institutions. The African Union has formally endorsed the shift, and advocates hope schools, governments and international organizations will follow. That gives the issue practical stakes. If a UN resolution advances, it could influence educational publishing, diplomacy, communication materials and perhaps even the defaults used by some digital platforms.

Overall, the article is a reminder that maps are never just containers of geographic fact. Projection choices influence who appears central, who appears marginal and how scale itself is understood. Here, cartography becomes a site of political negotiation, where demands for more accurate representation are tied to questions of dignity, narrative power and epistemic fairness.
