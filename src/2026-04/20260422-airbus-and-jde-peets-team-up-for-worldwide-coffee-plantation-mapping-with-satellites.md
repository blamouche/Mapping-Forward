# Airbus and JDE Peet’s team up for worldwide coffee plantation mapping with satellites
**Source**: https://www.airbus.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-04-airbus-and-jde-peets-team-up-for-worldwide-coffee-plantation-mapping-with-satellites
**Date**: April 22, 2026
**Author**: Airbus Defence and Space
**Keywords**: Airbus, coffee plantations, satellite mapping, deforestation, supply chains, AI

## Elevator pitch
Airbus and JDE Peet’s are building an open global map of coffee plantations with very high resolution satellite imagery and AI to support deforestation monitoring and more resilient coffee supply chains.

## Takeaways
- The Coffee Canopy Partnership aims to create an open worldwide map of coffee plantations.
- Airbus is combining Pléiades and Pléiades Neo imagery with AI models to detect coffee plantations from space.
- Initial deployments cover more than 1.2 million square kilometers across several East African countries.
- The project is designed to help identify deforestation risk, support restoration, and improve supply-chain transparency.
- The partnership plans to scale to worldwide coffee-growing regions in 2027.

## Synthesis
Airbus’s announcement describes a geospatial project with unusually broad implications for agricultural supply chains. By combining high-resolution satellite imagery with AI models trained to detect coffee plantations, the Coffee Canopy Partnership is trying to create what it calls the first comprehensive open map of global coffee cultivation. The core goal is practical: give governments, communities, traders, and brands a shared spatial baseline for identifying deforestation risk and managing resilience.

The mapping challenge is non-trivial. Coffee is often grown in complex landscapes, including shade-grown and agroforestry systems where plantation boundaries are harder to identify than in more uniform industrial crops. Airbus argues that the combination of 50 cm and 30 cm imagery with specialized AI models now makes it possible to map these landscapes at a scale and level of detail that was previously out of reach. That matters because without credible baseline maps, sustainability claims and deforestation commitments remain difficult to verify.

The project also reflects a shift away from fragmented company-led monitoring. Rather than building isolated proprietary maps for individual supply chains, the partnership is framed as shared infrastructure for an entire commodity ecosystem. That is strategically important. A common geospatial layer can reduce duplication, improve comparability across actors, and make landscape-scale interventions easier to coordinate.

The article emphasizes East Africa as the first deployment zone, covering more than 1.2 million square kilometers. Early results are presented as proof that large-scale plantation mapping can now be precise enough to guide policy and operational decisions. If the partnership succeeds in expanding globally, it could become a template for how remote sensing and AI support due diligence for other deforestation-linked commodities.

From a mapping perspective, the initiative shows how satellite cartography is moving deeper into supply-chain governance. The value of the map is not simply descriptive. It is intended to trigger action: identify risk, prioritize restoration, and give producers and buyers a more transparent view of where coffee is grown and under what environmental pressures.

Overall, the announcement illustrates a growing expectation that commodity systems should be legible from space. Mapping is being used here as a governance tool, tying together environmental compliance, farmer livelihoods, and corporate accountability through a shared spatial dataset.
