# Google Street View devient un outil de création de décors : les studios de cinéma vont pouvoir visualiser leurs scènes depuis un bureau
**Source**: https://www.01net.com/actualites/google-maps-street-view-ia-decors-cinema-maps-imagery-grounding.html
**Date**: April 22, 2026
**Author**: Unknown
**Keywords**: Google Street View, Maps Imagery Grounding, Gemini, Veo, film production, satellite imagery

## Elevator pitch
Google is turning Street View into a prompt-based previsualization tool, letting studios and other enterprise users test virtual scenes inside real locations while expanding AI analysis of aerial and satellite imagery.

## Takeaways
- Google announced Maps Imagery Grounding as a way to place generated visual elements into Street View scenes.
- The feature is aimed at enterprise and creative workflows, including film preproduction and advertising.
- Gemini creates the scene and Veo can animate it, reducing the need for early location scouting.
- Google also launched Aerial and Satellite Insights for faster image analysis in BigQuery.
- New Earth AI Imagery models are designed to detect objects like roads, bridges, and power lines automatically.

## Synthesis
01net’s article translates Google’s Cloud Next geospatial announcements into a concrete use case: film and creative teams using Street View as a rapid previsualization environment. That framing helps clarify what is changing. Google is no longer treating Street View as only a photographic archive for navigation or local search. It is becoming an AI-assisted scene-building surface tied to real places.

The feature at the center of the article, Maps Imagery Grounding, allows users to describe a scene in text and see generated visual elements inserted into a real Street View setting. For studios, agencies, or production teams, this reduces one of the most cumbersome parts of location-based creative work: the time and travel required to test visual ideas on site. If a team can sketch a spaceship over a New York plaza or preview another staging concept before sending people into the field, early decision-making becomes faster and cheaper.

That matters beyond cinema. The same capability could be useful for urban design, event staging, retail planning, or any workflow where stakeholders need to imagine how a real place might look after intervention. The article points out that the output remains indicative, which is an important limitation, but even an approximate visualization can accelerate discussion and narrow options before more rigorous design work begins.

The piece also connects this feature to Google’s broader geospatial AI push. Aerial and Satellite Insights aims to shrink manual imagery analysis from weeks to minutes by connecting satellite interpretation to BigQuery, while the new Earth AI Imagery models standardize detection of infrastructure objects such as roads or power lines. Together, these features show a consistent strategy: maps and imagery are being turned into AI-ready operational layers rather than static visual products.

From a mapping perspective, the interesting shift is conceptual. Real-world imagery is not just being searched or viewed, it is being computationally edited, analyzed, and repurposed through prompts. That opens new workflows, but it also changes what users expect from mapping platforms: less passive reference material, more active co-creation and machine interpretation.

Overall, the article captures a simple but important move. Google is trying to make geospatial imagery useful earlier in the creative and planning process, while also making remote-sensing analysis easier to operationalize at scale.
