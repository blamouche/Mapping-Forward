# How the Florida Wildlife Corridor Foundation Uses GIS to Save Florida's Wild Lands
**Source**: https://www.geoweeknews.com/news/how-the-florida-wildlife-corridor-foundation-uses-gis-to-save-florida-s-wild-lands
**Date**: April 22, 2026
**Author**: Abigail Hart
**Keywords**: GIS, conservation, Florida Wildlife Corridor, land use, participatory mapping

## Elevator pitch
The Florida Wildlife Corridor Foundation uses GIS as a decision and coordination layer to identify conservation pinch points, translate development risk, and mobilize landowner action before habitat links are lost.

## Takeaways
- Florida’s rapid population growth is increasing pressure on remaining habitat corridors and working lands.
- The Foundation uses GIS daily to identify vulnerable corridor gaps, conservation opportunity areas, and parcel-level ownership patterns.
- Spatial analysis helps translate abstract development pressure into concrete maps that stakeholders can act on.
- Participatory mapping workshops bring local institutions and landowners into the process, turning map review into relationship-based conservation work.
- The organization sees future value in more automation and interactive web tools to broaden collaboration and speed decision-making.

## Synthesis
This article offers a concrete example of GIS functioning as operational infrastructure for conservation rather than as a supporting visualization tool. The Florida Wildlife Corridor Foundation is dealing with a strategic problem that is both spatial and time-sensitive: how to protect enough connected land to preserve ecological movement across a rapidly developing state. GIS sits at the center of that effort because it allows the team to move between statewide corridor strategy and parcel-level intervention.

The scale described in the article is important. The corridor spans 18 million acres, of which 10 million are already protected and 8 million remain opportunity areas. Within that remaining area, the Foundation has identified 900,000 acres facing especially urgent development pressure. GIS makes those priorities visible and manageable. It allows staff to pinpoint narrow corridor sections where limited development could sever connectivity between larger ecological hubs, which is exactly the kind of problem that is difficult to understand without spatial analysis.

The article also shows how GIS supports action beyond analysis. Parcel-level ownership data helps the Foundation identify who controls critical lands, whether those owners have ties to trusted intermediaries, and what kind of conservation pathway might be realistic. That is a reminder that spatial workflows in conservation often connect technical data, institutional coordination, and human relationships. The Tallahassee example illustrates this well: a key gap was identified through analysis, but progress depended on matching the map insight with relationship-building that could move a landowner toward an easement application.

A second strong theme is communication. Development pressure can be modeled statistically, but models alone are rarely persuasive for local decision-makers. The article argues that maps make those dynamics tangible. In the Foundation’s “Mind the Gaps” workshops, large printed maps become shared decision objects around which governments, nonprofits, academics, and landowners can exchange local knowledge. That participatory layer matters because statewide datasets do not capture everything, especially who knows whom and where trust already exists.

Finally, the piece hints at the next step in conservation GIS: more automation and more collaborative digital interfaces. The Foundation wants faster map production and interactive tools that can capture local knowledge without waiting for in-person events. Overall, the article demonstrates how GIS can serve as a bridge between ecological strategy, land economics, stakeholder coordination, and practical conservation outcomes.
