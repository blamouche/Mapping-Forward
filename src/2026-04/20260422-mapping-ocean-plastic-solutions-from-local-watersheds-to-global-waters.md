# Mapping Ocean Plastic Solutions, from Local Watersheds to Global Waters
**Source**: https://www.esri.com/about/newsroom/blog/mapping-ocean-plastic-solutions
**Date**: Unknown
**Author**: Dawn Wright
**Keywords**: ocean plastic, GIS, pollution mapping, watersheds, marine conservation, citizen science

## Elevator pitch
An Esri profile shows how OpenOceans Global is using GIS, citizen data, and watershed-to-ocean mapping to locate plastic pollution at its source and turn local intervention into a scalable environmental strategy.

## Takeaways
- OpenOceans Global frames ocean plastic as an upstream mapping problem, not just a downstream cleanup challenge.
- Its GIS work links local watersheds, rivers, beaches, and ocean currents to reveal where plastic enters marine systems.
- The Ecuador pilot is testing a replicable dashboard model that combines data, predictive tools, and local expertise.
- The initiative aims to support practical interventions such as barriers, monitoring, and policy targeting before debris disperses.
- The broader goal is to create a global hub that matches locations with proven anti-plastic solutions and local ownership.

## Synthesis
This article profiles Carl Nettleton and OpenOceans Global, a nonprofit effort using geographic information systems to understand how plastic moves from land into the sea. Its central claim is simple but powerful: if communities can map where plastic enters waterways, they can intervene before that waste disperses across coasts and oceans. That shifts the problem from expensive cleanup to targeted prevention.

The piece emphasizes that most ocean plastic originates upstream, often through rivers and weak waste-management systems rather than through diffuse marine dumping alone. In that context, maps become more than awareness tools. They act as operational infrastructure for tracing sources, identifying hot spots, and prioritizing where funding, policy, and technical help should go. Nettleton explicitly compares the effort to outbreak tracking, which is a useful framing because it treats pollution as something that moves through networks and can be interrupted at key points.

OpenOceans Global has assembled this approach by combining fragmented public datasets, original research, and citizen-contributed observations into GIS-based applications. One example is the Ocean Plastic Map, which lets people submit field evidence of coastal pollution. That matters because the project is not built only around expert remote sensing or institutional data. It also depends on local knowledge being translated into spatial evidence. The article argues that people closest to the problem often already understand where it is concentrated, but they need tools that can make that knowledge actionable for decision-makers.

The most concrete development described here is the Ocean Plastic Pathways initiative, now piloted in Ecuador with support from geospatial institutions, universities, NGOs, and Esri partners. The pilot combines watershed maps, current patterns, and regional emissions data into a dashboard intended to guide intervention. The Ecuador example matters because it tests whether a mapping workflow can be standardized without becoming detached from local realities. The article suggests the answer is yes, provided capacity-building and local ownership remain central.

There is also a wider governance point running through the piece. International treaty discussions remain slow, while plastic production and waste continue to grow. In that setting, spatial intelligence offers a faster path to practical action. The map is not presented as a substitute for policy, but as a way to shorten the distance between evidence and intervention. Communities can use it to justify investments, choose where to deploy cleanup or interception tools, and build public consensus around visible problem areas.

The article is strongest when it shows mapping as a coordination layer. OpenOceans Global is not merely publishing a visualization of pollution. It is trying to connect data collection, local participation, predictive modeling, and solution design in one system. That makes the work relevant beyond marine conservation. It reflects a broader trend in mapping, where spatial products are increasingly expected to support intervention, not just description.

Overall, this is a good example of environmental GIS moving from narrative awareness to operational planning. The real promise is not a global dashboard on its own, but a repeatable method for helping local actors see upstream plastic pathways clearly enough to act before the damage spreads.
