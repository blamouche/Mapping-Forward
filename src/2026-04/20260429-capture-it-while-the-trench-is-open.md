# Capture It While the Trench Is Open

**Keywords**: [continuous capture, infrastructure construction, digital twins, reality capture, utilities, as-built documentation]

## Elevator pitch
Continuous site capture turns infrastructure construction into a time-indexed digital record that reduces rework, speeds remote verification, improves safety, and preserves higher-value as-built data.

## Takeaways
- The article argues that continuous capture closes documentation gaps that regularly lead to rework, delays, and disputes on infrastructure projects.
- Teams can use 360° cameras, drones, lidar, and connected sensors to build a time-stamped visual record of every project phase.
- The record becomes especially valuable when trenches are open because it preserves otherwise hidden underground conditions before they are covered again.
- Owners and project managers can verify work remotely across dispersed sites instead of relying on constant in-person surveys or incomplete field sketches.
- Captured data supports safety, compliance, and long-term as-built documentation that can feed maintenance workflows and digital twins.

## Synthesis
The article positions continuous capture as a practical shift in how infrastructure projects are documented and managed. Rather than depending on occasional site photos, paper sketches, and intermittent surveys, contractors and owners can build an ongoing visual record of how a project changes day by day. In sectors like utilities, roadworks, pipelines, and other buried infrastructure, that record matters because critical conditions often disappear as soon as trenches are closed, surfaces are restored, or the next subcontractor takes over.

A core argument is that continuous capture reduces the cost of missing information. When a team later needs to confirm where an asset was placed, how a condition looked before backfilling, or who completed a specific installation step, the traditional fallback is often expensive: call the construction team, return to the field, reopen a site, or accept uncertainty. The article frames 360-degree cameras, drones, lidar, and other reality-capture tools as a way to replace that uncertainty with a time-stamped, searchable record. That shifts documentation from a compliance afterthought into an operational asset.

The piece also stresses accountability. Construction disputes frequently hinge on incomplete evidence, especially when multiple contractors, inspectors, and owners are involved. A continuous visual history makes it easier to reconstruct what happened, when it happened, and what changed between visits. That can reduce arguments, support insurance or legal claims, and limit the reliance on memory or partial field notes. For infrastructure owners, the value is not only defensive. Better records also make project oversight more scalable.

Remote verification is another major theme. Large infrastructure programs can stretch across wide geographies, and the article notes that it is unrealistic to put a surveyor on every active site every day. If teams can capture conditions continuously with lightweight devices, project managers and owners gain a way to monitor progress without adding equivalent headcount. That matters for schedule control, but also for coordinating multiple parallel workstreams in utilities and transportation networks.

Finally, the article links continuous capture to long-term asset value. When construction ends, the accumulated record can feed accurate as-built documentation and digital twins, which then support maintenance, upgrades, and ownership handoffs. The broader message is that reality capture is moving from a specialized surveying tool to foundational infrastructure intelligence. The best moment to capture the hidden state of a project is while it is visible, because once the trench is closed, the cost of reconstructing that knowledge rises sharply.
