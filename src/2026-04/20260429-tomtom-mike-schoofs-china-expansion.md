# 对话TomTom首席执行官Mike Schoofs：当一家“海外版高德”要进入中国
**Source**: [https://www.eet-china.com/mp/a491215.html](https://www.eet-china.com/mp/a491215.html)
**Date**: [2026-04-29]
**Author**: [苏鹏]
**Keywords**: [TomTom, China automakers, Europe, ADAS, ISA compliance, Orbis maps]

## Elevator pitch
TomTom is positioning itself as the compliance-ready mapping and navigation infrastructure partner that can help Chinese automakers scale from exporting cars to operating competitively in Europe and other regulated global markets.

## Takeaways
- TomTom says vehicles equipped for Chinese automakers' overseas programs exceeded 100,000 units in the first quarter of 2025.
- The company claims orders tied to Chinese business grew tenfold from 2024 to 2025.
- TomTom frames EU intelligent speed assistance rules as a major entry barrier that requires precise, continuously updated speed-limit data.
- Its Orbis map platform is presented as the next-generation base layer for both lane-level ADAS functions and smarter navigation experiences.
- TomTom stresses flexible partnership models with Chinese ecosystem players such as Tencent, Huawei Petal Maps, and system integrators rather than a direct go-it-alone approach.

## Synthesis
The article presents TomTom as an increasingly important enabler for Chinese automakers expanding abroad, especially into Europe. It argues that the challenge has shifted from simply exporting vehicles to operating competitively in markets where regulation, localization, and software quality matter more. In that context, mapping and positioning infrastructure becomes a strategic dependency rather than a commodity feature.

A central theme is regulation. The piece highlights the European Union's requirement that new cars include intelligent speed assistance, which raises the bar for speed-limit data quality, freshness, and reliability. TomTom uses this point to position its unified speed restriction service as a practical compliance layer for carmakers. By combining static and real-time sources into a single high-precision output, the service is framed not only as a way to meet legal requirements but also as an input for smoother and more predictive assisted-driving behavior.

The article also emphasizes TomTom's Orbis platform as the long-term technical foundation behind this proposition. Orbis is described as a globally scaled map stack with road-to-lane coverage, continuous updates, and separate value propositions for ADAS and navigation. For ADAS, the promise is more precise lane-level positioning and current road intelligence. For navigation, the focus shifts to smarter routing, better ETA accuracy, and customizable private map layers and points of interest. Mentioning its role in Volkswagen Group's CARIAD stack is meant to signal industrial credibility for Chinese brands entering European markets.

Another notable point is commercial flexibility. Mike Schoofs accepts the comparison of TomTom to an "overseas AutoNavi" but argues that TomTom's advantage lies in being able to sell individual map data, standalone services, or complete integrated solutions. That flexibility matters because Chinese automakers do not share a single software architecture or partnership model. Some want only foundational map data, others prefer a turnkey package, and many already depend on domestic ecosystem partners.

The article therefore frames cooperation, not competition, as TomTom's preferred China strategy. Partnerships with Tencent, Huawei's Petal Maps, and local integrators are presented as ways to combine TomTom's global map base, regulatory knowledge, and ADAS expertise with local software adaptation and commercial relationships. The broader message is that global map data, compliance experience, and AI-assisted navigation are becoming part of the infrastructure Chinese carmakers need if they want to compete seriously with incumbent European brands in user experience as well as hardware.
