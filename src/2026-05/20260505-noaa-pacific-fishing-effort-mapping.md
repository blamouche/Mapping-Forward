# New System Maps and Charts West Coast Fisheries Data to Inform Decisions on Ocean Uses
**Source**: https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/feature-story/new-system-maps-and-charts-west-coast-fisheries-data-inform-decisions-ocean-uses
**Date**: May 5, 2026
**Author**: NOAA Fisheries (Northwest Fisheries Science Center)
**Keywords**: fisheries mapping, NOAA, West Coast, ocean planning, marine spatial planning, offshore wind, fisheries data, Pacific Fishing Effort Mapping Project

## Elevator pitch
NOAA, state agencies, and the Pacific States Marine Fisheries Commission have built a mapping system that consolidates decades of West Coast fisheries data into interactive dashboards, helping planners assess how offshore wind and other ocean uses may impact commercial fishing.

## Takeaways
- The Pacific Fishing Effort Mapping Project integrates observer data, logbooks, landings, vessel monitoring, and economic figures into unified dashboards at ~1 nautical mile resolution
- The system protects fishing confidentiality by displaying locations only when occupied by three or more vessels
- California Department of Fish and Wildlife will use the tool to review offshore wind proposals for fisheries impacts
- The tool helped U.S. and Canadian managers understand shifts in Pacific hake/whiting distribution under their international treaty
- Funded by NOAA and Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, supporting the National Seafood Strategy and Executive Order on Restoring American Seafood Competitiveness

## Synthesis
The Pacific Fishing Effort Mapping Project addresses a fundamental challenge in marine spatial planning: fisheries data typically lives scattered across state and federal agencies, in incompatible formats, making it nearly impossible to assess how proposed ocean uses like offshore wind might affect fishing communities. By consolidating vessel location data from automated beacons, catch information from observers and logbooks, and economic figures from landings, the system creates what lead researcher Lisa Pfeiffer calls "an efficient way to bring together data from different sources and make it more useful to managers and decisionmakers."

The dashboards display geographic concentrations of fishing for specific species across the West Coast, showing volume landed, economic value, and how these patterns change over time — all charted by year. Users can focus on particular ocean areas, such as those proposed for wind energy development. A geographic grid covering West Coast waters provides resolution down to approximately one nautical mile, though resolution is coarser where data confidentiality requires aggregation (the system requires three or more vessels to display a location).

A concrete success case: researcher Aaron Berger's team used early versions of the displays to help U.S. and Canadian managers understand shifting distributions of Pacific hake (whiting), during a period when Canadian catches were falling short of treaty expectations. The tool provided the spatial evidence needed for productive international dialogue.

The system comes at a critical moment. California's Statewide Offshore Renewable Energy Project will use it to review wind energy proposals for fisheries effects. By identifying connections between coastal communities and fisheries — showing where vessels land catches, creating demand for processing — the tool adds an economic and social dimension to what might otherwise be purely environmental assessments. Funded by NOAA and the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management, it represents a pragmatic approach to the unavoidable tradeoffs between clean energy development and existing ocean economies.
