# Giant Map Reveals Thousands of Cities Worldwide with Successful Green Policies
**Source**: https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-01491-y
**Date**: May 11, 2026
**Author**: Chris Simms
**Keywords**: urban sustainability, NO2 emissions, satellite mapping, green cities, decoupling, GDP, Sentinel-5P, Nature Cities

## Elevator pitch
A study of 2,475 of the world's largest cities using Sentinel-5P satellite data finds that 80% have decoupled economic growth from fossil-fuel emissions, revealing that green urban policies are succeeding at a scale far beyond isolated case studies.

## Takeaways
- Researchers used Copernicus Sentinel-5P satellite measurements of tropospheric NO2 (a fossil-fuel combustion byproduct) combined with local GDP data from 2019-2024
- Of 2,475 cities with significant trends, 80% showed rising GDP per capita alongside falling NO2 levels — labeled "green" cities
- The study, published in Nature Cities, mapped 5,435 cities initially, removing those with no significant NO2 change (2,919) or unreliable GDP data (41)
- Guangzhou, China is highlighted as an example where economic growth coincided with falling fossil-fuel emissions
- External economist Michail Fragkias called the results "highly encouraging" but noted the definition of "greenness" is broad

## Synthesis
The study by Daniel Moran and colleagues at the Norwegian Institute for Air Research represents one of the most comprehensive satellite-based assessments of urban sustainability ever conducted. Using data from the European Union's Copernicus Sentinel-5P satellite, the researchers measured tropospheric nitrogen dioxide (NO2) — a direct byproduct of burning fossil fuels in vehicles, power plants, and industrial facilities — above 5,435 cities worldwide over a five-year period (January 2019 to December 2024).

The headline finding is striking: of the 2,475 cities that showed statistically significant trends, 1,980 (80%) demonstrated what the researchers term a "green" trajectory — increasing GDP per capita while simultaneously reducing NO2 emissions. This suggests that the long-assumed coupling between economic growth and fossil fuel consumption is breaking down at the urban scale, and not just in wealthy Western cities but across diverse geographies including Guangzhou, China.

The methodology merits attention. Rather than relying on self-reported emissions inventories (which vary in quality and completeness across countries), the study uses a single, consistent satellite-based measurement system. NO2 serves as a proxy for fossil fuel combustion broadly, not just transport emissions. By pairing this with local GDP per capita data, the researchers created a standardized indicator that allowed direct comparison across very different urban contexts.

However, the study's limitations are significant. A broad definition of "greenness" means a city could qualify by reducing NO2 slightly while growing modestly — this doesn't necessarily indicate deep decarbonization. The 2,919 cities excluded for showing no significant NO2 change may include cities where emissions are stubbornly high. And the study doesn't distinguish between emissions reductions from deliberate policy versus economic shifts (deindustrialization, for instance). Still, as Boise State economist Michail Fragkias notes, the finding that four out of five cities with significant trends are decoupling is genuinely encouraging. It provides empirical evidence that the urban scale may be the most promising level for climate action.
