# USGS, NASA Map Critical Minerals from 65,000 Feet
**Source**: https://www.usgs.gov/news/national-news-release/usgs-nasa-map-critical-minerals-65000-feet
**Date**: May 12, 2026
**Author**: USGS Communications and Publishing
**Keywords**: USGS, NASA, hyperspectral survey, critical minerals, Earth MRI, GEMx, ER-2, mineral mapping, remote sensing

## Elevator pitch
The USGS and NASA are conducting the world's largest airborne hyperspectral survey from a NASA ER-2 aircraft flying at 65,000 feet, mapping critical minerals across 400,000 square miles of the American West to support U.S. mineral independence and national security.

## Takeaways
- The survey has covered about 400,000 square miles, capturing "spectral fingerprints" from ultraviolet through thermal infrared to identify minerals from 12 miles up.
- The ER-2 aircraft itself is built from aluminum, nickel, and titanium — all listed on the 2025 USGS List of Critical Minerals, underscoring the strategic importance of the mission.
- The survey is part of the USGS Earth Mapping Resources Initiative (Earth MRI) and the NASA-USGS Geological Earth Mapping Experiment (GEMx), a 60-year partnership.
- Ground-truthing with backpack-mounted spectrometers validates airborne data, with recent field work investigating a potential porphyry copper deposit anomaly.
- All Earth MRI data is made publicly available under USGS Fundamental Science Practices, supporting applications beyond minerals including water availability and landslide hazard assessment.

## Synthesis
In May 2026, the U.S. Geological Survey and NASA announced a major expansion of their collaborative hyperspectral survey program, creating what they describe as a "digital portrait" of critical minerals and geology across the American West. Flying at 65,000 feet — roughly 12 miles above the Earth's surface — NASA's ER-2 high-altitude aircraft carries instruments that measure light across dozens to hundreds of wavelengths, from ultraviolet through short-wave infrared to thermal infrared. These measurements capture distinctive spectral signatures that USGS scientists analyze to identify specific minerals with a precision that would be impossible from conventional aerial or satellite imagery.

The survey is a cornerstone of the USGS Earth Mapping Resources Initiative (Earth MRI), operating in partnership with 45 state geological surveys, educational institutions, and industry. The flights, which began April 1 and continue through May 20, 2026 (weather permitting), have already covered approximately 400,000 square miles. The program's scope extends beyond mineral identification: the same data supports assessments of water availability and geologic hazards such as landslides, making it a multi-purpose national mapping asset. The initiative operates under the Geological Earth Mapping Experiment (GEMx), continuing a 60-year USGS-NASA partnership that has previously mapped the Moon for Apollo and Artemis missions and delivered the Landsat program.

The strategic framing is explicit. Assistant Secretary Andrea Travnicek linked the survey to "regaining America's mineral independence," while USGS Director Ned Mamula noted the irony that the ER-2 aircraft itself is constructed primarily from critical minerals — aluminum, nickel, and titanium — for which the United States is heavily import-dependent (100% for titanium, 75% for aluminum, 41% for nickel in 2025).

The scientific process doesn't end with the flight data. Ground-truthing teams use backpack-mounted spectrometers to validate airborne measurements against field conditions, accounting for atmospheric interference and confirming mineral identifications. USGS geologist Erik Tharalson recently investigated an anomaly that could indicate a porphyry copper deposit — an example of how the survey translates spectral data into actionable mineral exploration leads. All data is publicly released under USGS Fundamental Science Practices and Executive Order 14303, emphasizing peer review and open communication. The initiative represents a significant investment in modern geoscience data collection, with Colorado's complex Rocky Mountain geology serving as an ideal proving ground for advanced airborne mapping techniques.
