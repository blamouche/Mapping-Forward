## Elevator pitch
A practical guide to three free, tracking-free GPS alternatives to Google Maps on Android — CoMaps (the community fork replacing Organic Maps), OsmAnd (the power user's Swiss Army knife), and Magic Earth (the surprising Waze replacement with 250% user growth in 2025).

## Takeaways
- Google's location tracking is systematic: a month of daily commutes reveals employer, neighborhood, daycare, doctor, and shopping habits. Timeline is activated by default and retains 36 months of history.
- CoMaps is a May 2025 community fork of Organic Maps after a governance crisis where 200 contributors accused the Estonian company of injecting proprietary binary components — now a clean, fast, no-account-needed OSM-based app.
- OsmAnd (since 2010, GPL-licensed) offers unparalleled depth: topographic maps, contour lines, GPX import, nautical charts, Wikipedia integration — but with an engineer-designed interface.
- Magic Earth saw 250% user growth in 2025, driven by privacy backlash against Google; it's free, proprietary, with real-time traffic, CarPlay/Android Auto support, and a no-tracking privacy policy.
- The recommended strategy: combine all three — CoMaps for hiking and daily use, Magic Earth for road trips with traffic, OsmAnd as backup for advanced functions.

## Synthesis
Alexandre Nardo's article for Les Numériques addresses the growing tension between navigation convenience and privacy, presenting a curated selection of tracking-free GPS alternatives for Android users who want to escape Google's data collection ecosystem. The piece opens with a stark reminder: Google's location tracking is granular enough to reconstruct a user's entire life — employer, neighborhood, childcare, medical providers, shopping patterns — from as little as a month of commute data, with Timeline retaining 36 months of history by default.

The article's most significant revelation concerns the shifting landscape of open-source navigation. Organic Maps, long the go-to recommendation for privacy-conscious users, is no longer reliable following a May 2025 governance crisis where its Estonian parent company was accused by 200 contributors of unilateral decision-making and injecting proprietary binary code into store-distributed versions. The community response was CoMaps, a clean fork that preserves the lightweight, no-tracking, no-account-required experience while restoring transparent governance. The migration for existing Organic Maps users takes "five minutes."

OsmAnd remains the reference for users who want total control. Licensed under GPL and based on OpenStreetMap since 2010, it provides functional depth that no alternative matches: topographic and nautical maps, contour lines, multiple activity profiles (car, bike, hiking, skiing), GPX import, and geolocated Wikipedia entries. Its interface, however, is "designed by engineers for engineers" — a trade-off that the free version's limit of seven map downloads and the €35 lifetime unlock can mitigate for committed users.

Magic Earth represents the pragmatic middle ground. Published by Romanian company General Magic, it is the only non-open-source option recommended but compensates with CarPlay/Android Auto integration, real-time traffic, offline country maps, and community-reported hazard alerts — essentially a privacy-respecting Waze replacement. Its 250% user growth in 2025 signals a broader market shift as privacy concerns drive adoption of alternatives.

Nardo's practical conclusion is to combine all three: CoMaps for hiking and daily navigation, Magic Earth for traffic-aware road trips, OsmAnd for advanced backup functions. The total storage cost is "a few hundred megabytes" — a negligible price for zero data sent to advertising networks.
