# In Utah, Geospatial Is a Community Sport
**Source**: https://www.geoweeknews.com/blogs/in-utah-geospatial-is-a-community-sport
**Date**: May 13, 2026
**Author**: Carla Lauter
**Keywords**: UGIC, Utah, GIS, regional conference, geospatial community, conservation, career pathways, UGRC

## Elevator pitch
The Utah Geographic Information Council (UGIC) annual conference reveals how a regional geospatial community thrives through collaboration on tough problems, conservation efforts, and intentional career development — offering a model that larger professional gatherings can learn from.

## Takeaways
- Utah's geospatial challenges are genuinely hard: patchwork governance, limited-capacity rural counties, and significant private land holdings complicate statewide data coherence.
- UGRC (Utah Geospatial Resource Center) serves as the state's coordination hub, aggregating datasets and building tools for all levels of government.
- Conservation acts as a unifying thread, with organizations sharing data across jurisdictional lines — exemplified by the Greater Salt Lake Municipal Services District's GIS and drone program.
- Career pathways and professional development were front and center, with sessions dedicated to interns, early-career hiring, and talent development for often-isolated GIS practitioners.
- The community's collaborative, tight-knit culture — where "everyone knows everyone" — creates a sense of belonging that larger conferences struggle to replicate.

## Synthesis
Carla Lauter's report from the 2026 UGIC conference in Kanab, Utah, paints a portrait of a geospatial community that has figured out how to make a regional gathering genuinely work. Unlike sprawling national events, UGIC succeeds because it leans into local specificity: the challenges of managing geospatial data across a state where rural counties operate with aging data and limited capacity, where private land ownership creates gaps in the statewide picture, and where the physical landscape itself — vast, remote, and contested — demands practical solutions.

The conference highlighted concrete applications of GIS technology to these challenges. UGRC, the Utah Geospatial Resource Center, emerged as a central player, coordinating statewide datasets, advising on best practices, and building applications for government at every level. Sessions covered digitizing parcel fabric from text-based legal descriptions, tracking invasive species, and using GIS to evaluate wildlife protection zones amid booming recreational tourism. These are not theoretical exercises; they reflect the day-to-day work of a community that treats spatial data as essential infrastructure.

Conservation proved to be a surprisingly effective unifying force. Organizations that might otherwise operate in silos were actively sharing data and collaborating on cross-jurisdictional problems. The Greater Salt Lake Municipal Services District exemplifies this approach: serving unincorporated communities that lack the resources of Salt Lake City proper, MSD has built a GIS and drone program to manage infrastructure and spatial data for tens of thousands of residents. This kind of work depends on the ecosystem of connection and collaboration that UGIC actively cultivates.

Perhaps the most distinctive feature of UGIC was its investment in people. Beyond technical content, the program carved out space for sessions on career pathways: advice for interns entering the field, strategies for hiring early-career professionals, and panels of GIS managers discussing talent development. In a community where many attendees are the only GIS person at their organization, this focus on professional growth is not a luxury — it is a survival strategy. The conference's informal network, where career histories are woven into everyday conversation, serves as an invisible support structure for practitioners who might otherwise work in isolation.

Lauter's conclusion is clear: regional conferences like UGIC deserve more attention. They have figured out what makes people feel like they belong, combining strong technical content with genuine collaborative instincts. As Geo Week prepares to move to Salt Lake City, the Utah geospatial community appears ready to contribute to a broader national conversation while maintaining the local texture that makes it work.
