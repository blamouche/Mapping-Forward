# MAPS Dialogues – A Shared Vocabulary for Military AI: Bridging Technical Understanding and Policy Needs
**Source**: https://disarmament.unoda.org/en/updates/maps-dialogues-shared-vocabulary-military-ai-bridging-technical-understanding-and-policy-needs
**Date**: May 14, 2026
**Author**: UN Office for Disarmament Affairs
**Keywords**: military AI, MAPS Dialogues, shared vocabulary, disarmament, AI terminology, human control, trust, accountability, autonomous weapons, UN ODA

## Elevator pitch
The UN Office for Disarmament Affairs launches the 2026 MAPS Dialogues, convening 160+ participants to address the dangerous gap between technical definitions and policy use of core AI terminology — a misalignment with direct operational consequences for military decision-making and international governance.

## Takeaways
- The MAPS Dialogues' first 2026 webinar focused on "shared vocabulary" — how terms like trust, reliability, accuracy, predictability, and autonomy carry divergent meanings between engineers and policymakers.
- Ambassador Jongin Bae (Republic of Korea) warned that without shared vocabulary, "even well-intentioned discussions risk talking past each other," directly affecting risk assessment, responsibility, and safeguards design.
- Panelists identified an accountability gap: commercial AI vendors claim IHL compliance is not their responsibility, while policymakers assume models were built with legal frameworks in mind.
- "Fabricated urgency" from AI arms race narratives drives accelerated procurement that skips necessary evaluation and validation practices.
- The panel challenged the premise that AI integration in military systems is inevitable, arguing policymakers should preserve the option of declining specific applications.
- A call for technically informed taxonomy, shared policy frameworks, and connecting existing multilateral conversations rather than starting parallel initiatives.

## Synthesis
The UN Office for Disarmament Affairs convened the first of three 2026 MAPS (Military AI, Peace & Security) Dialogues webinars on May 7, with a summary published May 14. The session's focus — building a shared vocabulary for military AI — addressed what panelists described as a foundational problem undermining international governance efforts.

The core tension is both simple and consequential: engineers define terms like accuracy, reliability, and autonomy with narrow, objective precision, while diplomats, lawyers, and military doctrine writers use the same words with inherent subjectivity. Zena Assaad (ANU) illustrated the operational danger: a policymaker hearing that an AI targeting system has "high accuracy" may interpret it in the broader, everyday sense, leading to over-trust and compressed human decision timeframes — including in current targeting operations.

Paul Lushenko (Cornell) added nuance by describing trust not as binary but as a spectrum: over-trust, under-trust, and "trust by proxy" where junior personnel rely on observation of peers rather than independent system assessment. This granularity matters because military hierarchies amplify the proxy effect.

The accountability problem surfaced forcefully. Heidi Khlaaf (AI Now Institute) described a circular abdication: commercial AI vendors disclaim IHL responsibility, while policymakers assume it was engineered in. No one owns the gap, and weaponized systems deploy anyway.

Several interventions pushed back against inevitability narratives. Assaad called out "fabricated urgency" — AI arms race rhetoric that justifies skipping evaluation. Khlaaf questioned whether multilateral discussions start from a false premise that AI is more capable than it actually is, and that integration in all military systems is unavoidable. Preserving the option to decline specific applications, she argued, should remain on the table.

For the mapping and geospatial community, the implications run deep. Military AI systems increasingly rely on geospatial data, satellite imagery analysis, and location intelligence. The terms under debate — accuracy, reliability, autonomy — are the same ones used in geospatial product specifications. When a satellite imagery classifier claims "95% accuracy" in detecting military equipment, is that the engineer's narrow definition or the diplomat's broader interpretation? The answer shapes whether targeting decisions are delegated to machines.

The MAPS Dialogues feed into UN informal exchanges on AI in the military domain scheduled for June 15-17 in Geneva. The vocabulary problem identified in this session will shape everything that follows.
