# Whose Map Counts in Conservation?
**Source**: https://news.mongabay.com/2026/05/whose-map-counts-in-conservation/
**Date**: May 14, 2026
**Author**: Rhett Ayers Butler
**Keywords**: participatory mapping, conservation, GIS, community knowledge, PPGIS, indigenous mapping, spatial planning, Massaha, precision conservation, data ownership

## Elevator pitch
A comprehensive review of 398 studies reveals that participatory mapping has grown rapidly in conservation but still lacks consistent standards for methods, ethics, and data governance — while cases like Massaha in Gabon demonstrate how community maps can challenge official datasets that render inhabited forests invisible.

## Takeaways
- Participatory mapping bridges two conservation needs: better spatial information about human-ecological overlaps, and greater legitimacy for decisions affecting land, livelihoods, and rights.
- The field grew steadily from 2012 to a 2019 peak, covering 102 countries and 13 domains — but English-language publication bias leaves significant gaps, especially in Africa, Asia, and South America.
- Robert Chambers' founding question — "whose map, who is empowered, who is disempowered?" — remains central: participatory maps reflect choices about who was invited, who spoke, and who controlled the output.
- The Massaha community in Gabon used participatory GIS to document sacred sites and ancestral villages within a logging concession, transforming a forest that appeared "empty" on satellite imagery into a visible, governed territory.
- Despite two decades of practice, evidence that participatory maps actually change land-use decisions remains thin — agencies may lack incentives, distrust lay knowledge, or face pressure from powerful interests.
- Practitioners should tie participatory mapping to specific decisions upfront, define data ownership and confidentiality, and treat the process as governance, not merely data collection.

## Synthesis
Mongabay founder Rhett Ayers Butler presents an incisive analysis of participatory mapping in conservation, anchored by a new 398-study review published in Conservation Science and Practice by Kowalski and colleagues. The piece operates at multiple levels: a field stocktake, a philosophical inquiry into cartographic power, and a practical guide for practitioners.

The central tension is straightforward. Conservation maps have traditionally been drawn from above — satellite imagery, species-distribution models, government zoning. These tools reveal patterns at scale but systematically miss how people use and value landscapes. A forest that appears as "intact canopy" to a satellite may be, to local residents, a burial site, a hunting ground, a medicine cabinet, or a contested space where patrols have become threatening. Participatory mapping inserts human meaning into spatial data.

The Kowalski review reveals impressive scope — 13 domains from ecosystem services to human-wildlife conflict, mapping in 102 countries — but also a discipline without guardrails. Terminology is chaotic (PPGIS, PGIS, VGI, sketch mapping used interchangeably), methods are inconsistently reported, and ethical standards for data ownership, confidentiality, and community benefit remain underdeveloped. The field has maps; it lacks standards.

Butler's most compelling case study is Massaha, Gabon, where the community mapped Ibola Dja Bana Ba Massaha — "the reserve of all Massaha's children." On global datasets, this forest appeared as intact canopy within a logging concession. Through oral histories, participatory GIS, GPS fieldwork, and community review, residents documented ancestral villages, sacred sites, sacred lakes, and encroaching logging roads. Their map became political evidence: a demand to remove the forest from the concession and recognize it as a community conserved area. The contrast with colonial, post-colonial, and global land-use maps was not merely technical but ontological — one cartographic tradition made the forest legible as a commodity; the other made visible centuries of human presence and stewardship.

The sobering finding is that despite decades of practice, participatory maps rarely change land-use decisions. Brown and colleagues' reviews found strong potential for mapping cultural and provisioning services, but little evidence of influence on actual planning. Agencies may lack incentives to integrate lay knowledge; officials may distrust non-expert data; powerful interests may override mapped community claims regardless.

For the geospatial community, this analysis carries urgent implications. As "precision conservation" gains traction, participatory mapping provides the missing layer: the model may find the best restoration site based on habitat connectivity, but local mapping reveals tenure disputes, failed past projects, or community-protected sacred groves. The most efficient intervention on paper may be unworkable on the ground. The challenge is not better software — it is better governance practices that connect mapped evidence to legitimate decisions before the concession is signed.
