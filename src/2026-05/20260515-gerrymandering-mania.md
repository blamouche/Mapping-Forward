# Gerrymandering mania
**Source**: https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/2026-election/gerrymandering-mania-rcna345380
**Date**: May 15, 2026
**Author**: Yasmin Vossoughian, Emily Ngo
**Keywords**: gerrymandering, redistricting, Voting Rights Act, Supreme Court, Republicans, congressional maps

## Elevator pitch
Following the Supreme Court's narrowing of the Voting Rights Act, Republicans in multiple states have launched a sweeping effort to redraw congressional lines to maximize GOP-leaning seats.

## Takeaways
- The Supreme Court's decision weakening the Voting Rights Act has triggered a redistricting bonanza across Republican-controlled states
- Multiple states are moving to redraw congressional boundaries to create more Republican-leaning districts
- The wave of redistricting follows President Trump's urging for GOP states to redraw their maps
- The mid-decade redistricting push is unprecedented in its scale and speed
- The outcome could significantly reshape the balance of power in the U.S. House of Representatives

## Synthesis
A nationwide redistricting frenzy has been unleashed following the U.S. Supreme Court's decision to significantly narrow the Voting Rights Act. The ruling, combined with President Trump's explicit encouragement for Republican-controlled states to redraw their congressional maps, has triggered what NBC News describes as "gerrymandering mania" — a coordinated, multi-state effort by Republican lawmakers to reshape electoral districts in their favor.

The push represents an unprecedented mid-decade redistricting wave. Historically, congressional maps are redrawn once per decade following the census, but the current political and legal environment has shattered that norm. States including Texas, Missouri, North Carolina, Ohio, and Florida have already enacted new maps, while Alabama, Louisiana, Tennessee, and others are actively pursuing redraws. The Virginia case — where a voter-approved Democratic map was struck down by the state Supreme Court and the U.S. Supreme Court declined to intervene — represents the flip side of this dynamic.

The scale of the effort is extraordinary. The Voting Rights Act, since its passage in 1965, has served as the primary federal safeguard against discriminatory redistricting, particularly protecting majority-Black and Hispanic districts. The Supreme Court's April ruling in the Louisiana case significantly weakened those protections, effectively opening the door for states to dismantle long-established minority-opportunity districts. Republican legislators have moved swiftly to capitalize on the opening, redrawing maps that eliminate or reconfigure districts that had reliably elected representatives of color. The result is a redistricting cycle that could determine control of the House for the remainder of the decade, conducted not through the traditional decennial process but through court rulings, emergency applications, and legislative special sessions.
