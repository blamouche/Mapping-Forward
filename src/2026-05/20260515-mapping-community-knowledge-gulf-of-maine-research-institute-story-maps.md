# Mapping Community Knowledge: How the Gulf of Maine Research Institute Uses Story Maps
**Source**: https://www.geoweeknews.com/news/mapping-community-knowledge-how-the-gulf-of-maine-research-institute-uses-story-maps
**Date**: May 15, 2026
**Author**: Abigail Hart
**Keywords**: story maps, GIS, climate communication, community knowledge, coastal resilience, ArcGIS, Gulf of Maine

## Elevator pitch
The Gulf of Maine Research Institute uses ArcGIS Story Maps to bridge the gap between dense climate data and coastal communities, validating local knowledge alongside official flood data and empowering residents to understand climate risks on their own terms.

## Takeaways
- Story maps serve as a major communications tool to make dense climate vulnerability data approachable for non-technical audiences.
- GMRI places community-observed flooding data alongside official FEMA maps, creating a powerful validation of local experience and a unique learning opportunity.
- The team carefully decides what to visualize, limiting zoom levels on maps to prevent misinterpretation of flood extent data at the individual property level.
- Story maps are designed as part of a broader communications strategy, tied to vulnerability assessments, community science initiatives, and multi-year studies.
- The interactive format has proven effective in classrooms, where students engage more naturally with spatial data than with static reports.

## Synthesis
The Gulf of Maine Research Institute (GMRI), through its Community Climate Action Team (CCAT), has embraced ArcGIS Story Maps as a central tool for making complex climate data accessible to coastal communities in Maine. In a conversation with Geo Week News, program manager Stephanie Sun detailed how the institute's geospatial work spans three interconnected areas: climate literacy, capacity support, and technical assistance.

The most distinctive aspect of GMRI's approach is its deliberate elevation of community knowledge to the same level as official scientific data. When residents report where they observed flooding during a major storm, GMRI maps those observations alongside FEMA flood maps and other authoritative datasets. This side-by-side comparison often reveals discrepancies between modeled risks and lived experience, and the act of seeing their knowledge reflected geospatially has proven deeply validating for community members. Sun describes this convergence of scientific data and local knowledge as "the magic of geospatial work."

A significant part of the team's craft lies in deciding what to visualize, a process Sun describes as a constant balancing act. A flood extent map might show a centimeter of water or a foot of water, two scenarios that require entirely different communication strategies. The team must be conservative without understating the problem, and transparent about data limitations. One practical technique they've developed is limiting how far users can zoom in on maps within the story map interface, preventing residents from trying to identify flood risk at the individual property level when the data resolution doesn't support that kind of precision.

A standout example is the Union Wharf Asset and Water Level Data Viewer in Portland, an area notorious for flooding. Built over three years of close collaboration with property managers, the tool is designed for quick decision-making when a storm is approaching: managers can look up a specific water level and immediately access all the critical information they need. The interactive format also extends into educational settings, where GMRI partners with an in-house education team to support curriculum development around coastal flooding. Students respond well to the exploratory nature of story maps, which are "so much less prescriptive than a report" and allow for conversation and context rather than static consumption of information.

Looking ahead, Sun hopes the tools and knowledge spread beyond GMRI itself, envisioning communities equipped to take the same data and create their own story maps. The work exemplifies a broader trend in geospatial practice: maps are no longer just outputs of expert analysis, but platforms for dialogue between technical data and the people whose lives it describes.
