# Supreme Court is death knell for Virginia's Democratic-friendly congressional map
**Source**: https://www.npr.org/2026/05/15/nx-s1-5823911/supreme-court-virginia-redistricting
**Date**: May 15, 2026
**Author**: Nina Totenberg, Zoe Sobel
**Keywords**: redistricting, Supreme Court, Virginia, gerrymandering, congressional maps, elections

## Elevator pitch
The U.S. Supreme Court declined to intervene after Virginia's state Supreme Court nullified a voter-approved Democratic-friendly congressional map, effectively killing it and leaving the old districts in place.

## Takeaways
- Virginia Democrats drew a new congressional map approved by voters in an April referendum that would have yielded four more likely Democratic seats
- The Virginia Supreme Court ruled 4-3 that lawmakers failed to follow proper constitutional procedure to get the issue on the ballot, voiding both the referendum and the new map
- Democrats appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court arguing the state court overrode the will of the people on federal law grounds
- The U.S. Supreme Court sided with Republicans without explanation, leaving the state court ruling intact
- This is the latest in a series of emergency redistricting interventions — the Court previously allowed Texas's gerrymander, permitted California's voter-approved map, blocked New York's redraw, and ruled Louisiana's map was a racial gerrymander

## Synthesis
The U.S. Supreme Court delivered a decisive blow to Virginia Democrats' redistricting strategy by refusing to reinstate a congressional map that would have tilted the state's House delegation heavily toward Democrats. The voter-approved map, designed to counter the Republican redistricting wave following President Trump's election, was voided by the Virginia Supreme Court on procedural grounds — specifically that lawmakers had violated the state constitution by failing to follow proper procedures for placing the issue on the ballot.

The Virginia Supreme Court's 4-3 ruling on May 8 declared both the April referendum and the resulting map null and void. Democrats and the state attorney general filed an emergency application with the U.S. Supreme Court arguing that the state court was "deeply mistaken" on issues of federal law and had "overrode the will of the people." Republican legislators countered that the case was purely a state law dispute in which the federal courts should not intervene — particularly since no federal claims had been raised in the lower court.

The Supreme Court's decision not to intervene, delivered without explanation, continues a pattern of high-stakes redistricting rulings this term. In December, the Court allowed Texas to use a gerrymandered map potentially giving Republicans five additional House seats. In February, it permitted California to use a voter-approved Democratic-friendly map. In March, it blocked the redrawing of a New York map that would have flipped a Republican seat. And in April, it ruled that Louisiana's congressional map constituted a racial gerrymander requiring redrawing — a decision that set off a flurry of redistricting efforts across the South, where Republican legislators immediately began eliminating long-established majority Black and Hispanic districts.

The cumulative effect of these rulings reveals a Court deeply enmeshed in the partisan battle over electoral maps, but applying no consistent federal doctrine. Instead, each emergency application appears to be handled in isolation, leaving state courts and political actors to navigate an increasingly fragmented landscape of redistricting law. For Virginia, the practical consequence is clear: the state will conduct its upcoming elections using the congressional districts that voters rejected, a result that reinforces the decisive role courts play in shaping American electoral geography.
