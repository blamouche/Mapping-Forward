# The DMA's Google Maps Experiment Shows That Competition Is Not One Click Away
**Source**: https://www.promarket.org/2026/05/18/the-dmas-google-maps-experiment-shows-that-competition-is-not-one-click-away/
**Date**: May 18, 2026
**Author**: Louis Pape (Télécom Paris/CREST) and Michelangelo Rossi (HEC Paris)
**Keywords**: DMA, Digital Markets Act, Google Maps, self-preferencing, competition policy, antitrust, search defaults, platform regulation, EU

## Elevator pitch
New research shows that the EU's DMA-mandated removal of Google Maps shortcuts from search results had virtually no effect on competition — users simply typed "google maps" instead, with rivals like Bing Maps, Mappy, and OpenStreetMap seeing no meaningful traffic gains.

## Takeaways
- After the DMA forced Google to remove clickable Maps shortcuts from search results in January 2024, EU searches for "maps" and "google maps" both rose by over 21% — users compensated by typing the brand name.
- Bing Maps, Mappy, and OpenStreetMap showed no robust increase in search volume; Apple Maps showed a small but statistically fragile increase from a near-zero base.
- France is the striking case: Mappy had higher pre-DMA search volume than Google Maps there, yet after the redesign Mappy searches declined and Google Maps searches overtook them.
- Organic search traffic to Google Maps increased by 33-40%, while direct traffic was flat — users rerouted rather than reallocated.
- Average visit duration fell 5% and bounce rate rose 16%, indicating a small user experience deterioration from the "Directions"-only routing.
- The authors argue the DMA achieved formal compliance but failed on contestability — the actual market outcome didn't change.
- A mapping service choice screen (like Google's browser search engine screen) is proposed as a more effective remedy.
- Self-preferencing removal only works when viable alternatives exist; in mapping, data coverage and feature quality gaps mean removing defaults doesn't redirect demand.

## Synthesis
Two economists from Télécom Paris and HEC Paris have published what may be the most rigorous empirical test of the EU Digital Markets Act's core premise: that removing self-preferencing by dominant platforms will create space for competitors. Their answer, based on a natural experiment created by Google's DMA-mandated search interface redesign in January 2024, is sobering for competition regulators.

Before the DMA, searching for a location on Google returned a clickable map that defaulted to Google Maps. After compliance, the map became non-clickable and the direct link disappeared. Users could still reach Google Maps, but only by manually typing a query or clicking a narrow "Directions" button. The researchers used a difference-in-differences design comparing EU and non-EU countries, where the interface remained unchanged.

The results are striking in their clarity. Searches for both "maps" and "google maps" jumped over 21% in the EU relative to non-EU countries — tracking each other almost perfectly. Users didn't explore alternatives; they simply typed the brand name they already knew. Organic search traffic to Google Maps surged 33-40% as users compensated for the lost click with a new one, while direct traffic stayed flat.

Competitors saw nothing meaningful. Bing Maps, Mappy, and OpenStreetMap showed no robust increases. Apple Maps showed a detectable but tiny increase (0.2% of Google Maps volume) that wasn't statistically robust. France provides the most damning counterfactual: Mappy, a credible local competitor with higher pre-DMA search volume than Google Maps, actually declined after the redesign while Google Maps searches overtook it. If any market was positioned for a DMA-driven shift, it was France. It didn't happen.

The authors frame their conclusion carefully: this isn't an indictment of the DMA, but evidence that formal compliance doesn't equal competitive effect. Removing a self-preferencing shortcut didn't redirect demand because mapping services aren't interchangeable. Data coverage, interface familiarity, and feature quality matter more than one click. The policy implication is direct: choice screens that actively present alternatives — not passive removal of defaults — are the remedy more likely to achieve the DMA's contestability mandate.
