# Precipitation-based Flood-Inundation Maps for the East Fork Little Blue River and Tributaries at Lee's Summit, Missouri, 2024
**Source**: https://www.usgs.gov/publications/precipitation-based-flood-inundation-maps-east-fork-little-blue-river-and-tributaries
**Date**: May 19, 2026
**Author**: Allison A. Atkinson
**Keywords**: USGS, flood inundation, HEC-RAS, precipitation modeling, Missouri, flood mapping, emergency management, climate change

## Elevator pitch
The USGS developed a library of flood-inundation maps from 240 precipitation scenarios using a calibrated 2D HEC-RAS rain-on-grid model including climate-change projections, delivered via a real-time web mapping application tied to live airport weather data for emergency evacuations and road closures.

## Takeaways
- The study covers a 2.95-mile reach of the East Fork Little Blue River and tributaries in Lee's Summit, Missouri.
- A two-dimensional HEC-RAS rain-on-grid model was calibrated to selected runoff events representing a range of antecedent conditions.
- 240 scenarios were developed from design rainfall events (15-minute to 24-hour durations) with 3 antecedent runoff conditions each.
- Additional scenarios simulate projected precipitation effects on the 100-year recurrence interval under climate change.
- Lowest adjacent grades for structures were incorporated into terrain, and depth/water-surface elevation grids were developed for the study area.
- The flood-inundation maps are accessible via a public web mapping application tied to real-time precipitation data from Lee's Summit Municipal Airport.

## Synthesis
This USGS Scientific Investigations Report, produced in cooperation with the City of Lee's Summit, Missouri, represents a sophisticated marriage of hydrologic modeling and web-based geospatial delivery. The core innovation is the "rain-on-grid" approach using the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers' HEC-RAS 6.5 two-dimensional model, which simulates precipitation falling directly onto the terrain mesh. This captures both pluvial flooding (from intense rainfall) and fluvial flooding (from river overflow), making it particularly relevant for urban flash-flood scenarios where stormwater infrastructure can be quickly overwhelmed.

The modeling effort is exhaustive: 240 scenarios spanning 15-minute to 24-hour rainfall durations and recurrence intervals from 1-year to 1,000-year events, each run under three antecedent runoff conditions. Crucially, the study goes beyond historical data to incorporate projected climate-change precipitation scenarios for the 100-year storm at both 6-hour and 24-hour durations. The model was calibrated using water-surface elevation data from pressure transducers, streamflow measurements, and reference-point observations during actual runoff events.

What elevates this beyond a technical report is the delivery mechanism. Rather than publishing static PDF maps, the USGS ties the pre-computed flood-inundation library to real-time precipitation data from the Automated Surface Observing System at Lee's Summit Municipal Airport. This means emergency managers and residents can see flood extents that correspond to current rainfall conditions — not a theoretical design storm. The maps support operational decisions including evacuations and road closures, bridging the gap between academic hydrology and life-saving emergency response.
