# How Map Scales Impact Coastal Communities
**Source**: https://india.mongabay.com/2026/05/how-map-scales-impact-coastal-communities/
**Date**: May 19, 2026
**Author**: Shreya Raman
**Keywords**: coastal regulation, map scale, India, CRZ, community mapping, Puducherry, coastal zone management

## Elevator pitch
India's coastal regulation policy relies on 1:25,000 scale maps that are too coarse to capture the commons and eco-sensitive areas coastal fishing communities depend on, undermining meaningful public consultation and enabling business-friendly coastal development.

## Takeaways
- India's Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) notification mandates public consultation on 1:25,000 scale maps where coastal commons like fish-drying centres are nearly invisible.
- Smaller 1:4,000 scale maps exist but are not subject to public consultation, creating an accountability gap.
- In Puducherry and Karaikal, communities successfully protested draft maps in 2023-2024 that excluded fishing villages and eco-sensitive areas.
- Government satellite-based mapping (NCSCM) missed 70-90% of eco-sensitive areas according to community-led ground truthing with GIS experts.
- The coastline paradox means closer inspection reveals more detail — India's coastline estimate grew from 7,500 to 11,100 km when mapped at finer scale.

## Synthesis
This Mongabay-India investigation reveals how cartographic scale choices directly shape environmental governance outcomes for India's coastal communities. The article centres on the CRZ notification's requirement for Coastal Zone Management Plans (CZMPs) at 1:25,000 scale — a resolution where one grid cell covers roughly 196 square kilometres. At this scale, the fish-drying centres, boat-parking areas, and mangrove patches that constitute coastal communities' livelihood infrastructure effectively disappear from view.

The human impact is illustrated through Kannachiamma, a 75-year-old woman whose survival depends on a small 300-square-metre fish-drying centre near Karaikal. These commons are not marked on the large-scale maps used for public consultation, yet the finer 1:4,000 maps that could show them are prepared without community input. This asymmetric information structure systematically excludes the very people whose consent is nominally being sought.

The Puducherry case study demonstrates both the problem and a partial solution. When draft maps released in March 2023 classified fishing beaches as tourism zones without naming the villages themselves, communities boycotted the public hearing. This protest, combined with political pressure from fisher organisations, forced the government to withdraw the plans and form a joint mapping team. Over two months, traditional fisher panchayats worked with mapping experts and government officials to document fishing villages and commons — producing maps that were formally submitted to the Department of Fisheries.

However, even after this participatory mapping exercise, the April 2024 revised draft still excluded many seine fishing areas, mangroves, sand dunes, and turtle nesting sites. Community-led ground truthing by ecologists and GIS experts found that NCSCM's satellite-derived data missed 70-90% of eco-sensitive areas. The National Green Tribunal has twice been approached, and while authorities promised to create another draft incorporating community concerns, six months have passed without action.

The article exposes a structural tension in environmental governance: the 2019 CRZ notification, which opened coastal areas to real estate, tourism, and ports, relies on map scales that systematically obscure what communities need to protect. The coastline paradox — where measuring at finer scales reveals more detail — becomes a governance paradox: the tools designed to enable democratic consultation are calibrated at a resolution that precludes it. Until maps reflect what communities actually see and use, coastal regulation will remain tilted toward development interests.
