# Evaluating routing engine accuracy in replicating children's school routes: Google, Mapbox, and OSRM compared
**Source**: https://m.ebiotrade.com/newsf/2026-5/20260520200711597.htm
**Date**: May 21, 2026
**Author**: Unknown (International Journal of Health Geographics)
**Keywords**: routing engines, GPS, Google Maps, Mapbox, OSRM, active school travel, children mobility, navigation accuracy

## Elevator pitch
A study comparing Google Maps, Mapbox, and OSRM found that navigation engines can replicate about two-thirds of children's walking routes to school, offering a low-cost alternative to GPS tracking for mobility research.

## Takeaways
- OSRM achieved 67.6% overlap with actual GPS tracks, Mapbox 66.8%, and Google 62.6%
- All three engines significantly outperformed the shortest-path baseline of 45%
- Route distance was negatively correlated with accuracy (longer routes = less overlap)
- Gender, poverty level, and urban/rural setting had no significant effect on accuracy
- The study involved 233 Scottish children aged 10-11, suggesting navigation engines can supplement GPS in mobility research

## Synthesis
Published in the International Journal of Health Geographics, this study evaluated whether commercially available navigation engines can serve as low-cost alternatives to GPS devices for tracking children's active school travel (AST). The researchers compared three routing engines — Google Maps, Mapbox, and the open-source OSRM — against actual GPS-measured walking trajectories from 233 Scottish children aged 10-11, across both urban and rural settings.

The results showed that all three engines captured approximately two-thirds of the actual routes. OSRM performed best with 67.6% overlap accuracy, followed by Mapbox at 66.8% and Google at 62.6%. All three significantly outperformed a baseline shortest-path calculation based on Ordnance Survey road networks (45.0%). Notably, demographic factors such as gender, poverty level, or urban/rural environment did not systematically affect accuracy, though longer routes showed a slight negative correlation with overlap precision.

The study's significance lies in its practical implications: if navigation engines can reasonably approximate children's walking routes, researchers could reduce reliance on expensive GPS devices that often introduce recruitment bias. This could democratize AST research, particularly in resource-constrained settings, while providing an empirical benchmark for future mobility studies.
