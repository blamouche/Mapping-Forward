# These new Martian maps could change everything we know about living on Mars
**Source**: https://www.futura-sciences.com/en/these-new-martian-maps-could-change-everything-we-know-about-living-on-mars_32440/
**Date**: May 21, 2026
**Author**: Morgane Gillard
**Keywords**: Mars, thermal inertia mapping, AI, THEMIS, CRISM, orbital data, Martian exploration, water ice

## Elevator pitch
Scientists used AI to merge old orbital datasets from NASA's THEMIS and CRISM instruments, producing high-resolution thermal inertia maps of Mars that reveal surface composition and potential water ice locations without launching new missions.

## Takeaways
- AI model trained on Gale Crater data from Curiosity rover to match spectral signatures with surface temperatures
- THEMIS provides wide-area thermal data at moderate resolution; CRISM offers high-res mineral composition but limited thermal info
- Combined approach produces detailed thermal inertia maps distinguishing rocky terrain, sandy expanses, and ice-rich regions
- Results presented at the International Astronautical Congress and available on arXiv
- Demonstrates that existing data can yield breakthrough results through intelligent recombination

## Synthesis
A research team has demonstrated that artificial intelligence can extract significantly more value from existing Martian orbital data than previously possible, producing high-resolution thermal inertia maps without requiring new spacecraft missions. The work addresses a fundamental challenge in Mars exploration: identifying surface locations with exploitable resources — particularly water ice — for potential human outposts.

The approach combines two complementary but imperfect datasets. NASA's THEMIS instrument aboard Mars Odyssey provides surface temperature readings over large areas but at moderate resolution. The CRISM instrument on the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter delivers highly detailed mineral composition maps but limited thermal information. Neither alone provides the complete picture needed for precise surface characterization.

The researchers trained a statistical model on Gale Crater, where the Curiosity rover provides ground-truth data, teaching the AI to correlate spectral signatures with surface temperatures. Once trained, the model was applied across all available orbital data, generating thermal inertia maps that distinguish between rocky areas, sandy expanses, and regions potentially rich in subsurface ice with far greater precision than either instrument could achieve independently.

The significance extends beyond the specific Martian results. The project demonstrates a broader principle: that AI-driven data fusion can unlock insights from archival datasets that were collected for different purposes, potentially reducing the need for costly new missions. For Mars exploration planning, these maps provide crucial intelligence for selecting landing sites and identifying resource-rich areas for future human presence.

The results are available on arXiv and were presented at the International Astronautical Congress.
