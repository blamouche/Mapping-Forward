# De Marioupol à Gaza : Google Maps et la mémoire des lieux meurtris par la guerre
**Source**: https://blogs.mediapart.fr/romain-mejean/blog/220526/de-marioupol-gaza-google-maps-et-la-memoire-des-lieux-meurtris-par-la-guerre
**Date**: May 22, 2026
**Author**: Romain Méjean
**Keywords**: Google Maps, war, memory, geonumerization, Marioupol, Gaza, digital sovereignty, cartography

## Elevator pitch
Google Maps preserves fragments of daily life in war-torn cities like Marioupol and Gaza, raising urgent questions about who controls the digital memory of destroyed places and the geopolitics of geonumerization.

## Takeaways
- Google Maps still shows restaurants and POIs in Gaza and Marioupol that no longer physically exist, creating "ghost POIs"
- Satellite imagery archives in Google Earth allow diachronic comparison of destruction, making war visible to anyone
- The map is not the territory: Google's interface becomes absurd in contexts of war, where cities are spaces of survival not consumption
- Digital memory of destroyed places is held by private corporations, creating a democratic and ethical sovereignty problem
- The author argues for collective awareness and alternative geospatial platforms to counter corporate control of spatial memory

## Synthesis
Romain Méjean explores the concept of "geonumerization" — the process of transcribing the physical world into geolocated digital data — through the lens of war. Using Google Maps and Google Earth to examine Marioupol (Ukraine) and Gaza (Palestine), two cities devastated by recent bombing campaigns, the article reveals striking dissonances between digital representations and physical reality.

The core observation is haunting: Google Maps in Gaza still displays restaurant recommendations, hotel listings, and "things to do" suggestions — commercial interface elements that become absurd against a backdrop of survival and destruction. Meanwhile, many physical locations that were destroyed have been removed from the map, but some persist as "ghost POIs," their deletion governed by opaque corporate processes rather than any coherent memorial logic.

Google Earth's historical imagery feature proves particularly powerful. By toggling between pre-war and post-war satellite views, the destruction becomes visible to anyone with an internet connection. Méjean uses the Marioupol theatre bombing as a case study, showing how the word "Дети" (children) was painted on the ground to warn Russian pilots, and how satellite archives preserve this evidence.

The article raises fundamental questions about geonumerical sovereignty. When Google controls the most visible digital representation of the world, the memory of war-torn places — the restaurants where families gathered, the parks where children played, the streets where daily life unfolded — belongs to a private corporation. This is not just an abstract concern: both Marioupol and Gaza are subjects of reconstruction projects driven by foreign powers (Russia's "New Russia" vision and Trump's "Middle East Riviera" plan), making the question of who controls spatial memory deeply political.

Méjean reframes the cartographic truism "the map is not the territory" for the digital age, arguing that the disconnect between Google's commercial cartography and the reality of war is not just a technical glitch but a structural feature of corporate-controlled geonumerization. The article calls for democratic engagement with geospatial technologies — not as passive consumers but as citizens who understand that the power to draw the map is the power to shape memory itself.
