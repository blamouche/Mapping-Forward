## Elevator pitch
The FAO/IAEA SoilFER programme is scaling up soil fertility monitoring across Africa and beyond using Mid-Infrared Spectroscopy — a faster, cheaper alternative to conventional soil analysis that enables data-driven agricultural decision-making.

## Takeaways
- MIR spectroscopy enables rapid, cost-effective soil analysis compared to conventional wet-chemistry methods
- Training brings together soil scientists from Ghana, Guatemala, Honduras, Kenya and Zambia
- The programme is funded by the US Department of State and Government of Japan, led by FAO's Land and Water Division
- Goal is to strengthen national soil information systems and laboratory capacities
- Knowledge gained supports evidence-based policies on fertilizer use, land management and crop suitability

## Synthesis
The FAO, in partnership with the IAEA, has launched a three-week training programme at their joint laboratory in Seibersdorf, Austria, focused on Mid-Infrared (MIR) spectroscopy for soil analysis. The training is part of the broader Soil Mapping for Resilient Agrifood Systems (SoilFER) initiative and involves soil scientists and laboratory specialists from five countries: Ghana, Guatemala, Honduras, Kenya, and Zambia.

The core challenge SoilFER addresses is the persistent lack of accessible, accurate soil data in many developing countries. Traditional soil testing methods rely on wet chemistry, which is expensive, slow, and dependent on chemical reagents that may be difficult to source. MIR spectroscopy changes this equation dramatically — it requires only drying, grinding, and milling samples before spectral analysis, making large-scale soil monitoring both technically feasible and economically viable. The technique can assess organic carbon, soil texture, and multiple nutrient indicators in a single rapid scan.

The programme sits at the intersection of nuclear science applications and agricultural development. The Joint FAO/IAEA Centre brings specialized expertise in nuclear and related techniques, while FAO's Land and Water Division provides the institutional framework for translating laboratory science into national policy tools. Funding from the United States and Japan underscores the geopolitical importance placed on food system resilience and soil health as strategic assets.

Nora Berrahmouni, Deputy Director of FAO's Land and Water Division, framed the initiative as foundational for countries to make "sound, science-based decisions" about where, what, and how to plant. Dongxin Feng, Director of the Joint FAO/IAEA Centre, emphasized the training as a step toward building lasting national capacities rather than creating dependency on external laboratories.

The practical impact is meant to cascade from laboratory benches to farmers' fields: better soil data informs fertilizer recommendations, land-use planning, and crop selection, ultimately contributing to food security and farmer livelihoods. By equipping national institutions with both the technology and the skills to operate it independently, SoilFER aims to create self-sustaining soil information systems rather than one-off interventions.

This represents a significant scaling of spectroscopic soil mapping techniques from research settings into operational national programmes, potentially transforming how soil health is monitored and managed across large agricultural regions.
