## Elevator pitch
The inaugural Africa on the Mapping Festival brings video mapping and digital street art to Bujumbura's public spaces, transforming buildings into canvases for ephemeral futures and cultural dialogue in Burundi.

## Takeaways
- First edition of Africa on the Mapping Festival runs June 1-6, 2026 in Bujumbura, organized by collectives La Symphonie du Souffle and Lumartis.
- Participating artists include video artists, photographers, motion designers, and mappers: Albert Morisseau Leroy, Jonathan Nkubito Nindorera, Daniela Pinto, Fred Ebami, and Achref Guesmi.
- Two landmark buildings selected for projections: the Greek Orthodox Church and the Old East building on Independence Square — both iconic, free, and accessible to all residents.
- The festival emerged from a video mapping training led by Albert Morisseau Leroy under the "Burundi en création" program, with talented artists from Congo, Rwanda, and Congo.
- A dance-sound-image performance titled "Noyau de mangue" featuring music by Blick Bassy will explore life's scars at the Institut français on June 4.

## Synthesis

In June 2026, the streets of Bujumbura will become a luminous gallery as the first Africa on the Mapping Festival transforms buildings into canvases for digital projection art. Organized by two collectives — La Symphonie du Souffle and Lumartis — the festival brings together approximately ten video artists, photographers, motion designers, and mapping specialists to project ephemeral works onto the facades of the Greek Orthodox Church and the Old East building on Independence Square, two landmarks familiar to every resident of the Burundian capital.

The festival's origin story is rooted in a video mapping training workshop conducted by Albert Morisseau Leroy as part of the "Burundi en création" program. The training brought together promising artists from the region, including Junior François Adasopi (Congo), Jonathan Nkubito Nindorera (Rwanda), and Françoise Christelle Faila (Congo). Morisseau Leroy describes the collective drive as emerging from their talent and motivation: "What I like is the idea of bringing art out of expected places, bringing smiles by making facades come alive; and letting everyone express themselves, reappropriate their own narrative."

The festival's ambition extends beyond artistic expression to address structural challenges. Burundi, a small landlocked country in the Great Lakes region bordered by Rwanda, Tanzania, and the Democratic Republic of Congo, faces frequent power outages and an economy still anchored in agriculture. The organizers see the festival as a vehicle to raise Burundi's international profile, equip local artists with projection equipment through contributions from African partners, and build bridges with countries including France, Guatemala, Nigeria, and Tunisia to catalyze more cultural and creative industries (ICC) projects.

Video mapping — conceived as digital, ephemeral street art — invites residents to see their city differently through immersive installations projected in open-air settings. The festival program includes roundtables on this still-emerging art form in Central Africa, and a distinctive performance titled "Noyau de mangue" (Mango Kernel) blending dance, sound (with music composed by Blick Bassy), and images, addressing life's scars, scheduled for June 4 at the Institut français. The article closes with an invocation of Gaël Faye's lyrics about Bujumbura, extending an open invitation to experience the city as a space where poetry, digital technology, and territorial sublimation converge on centuries-old walls.
