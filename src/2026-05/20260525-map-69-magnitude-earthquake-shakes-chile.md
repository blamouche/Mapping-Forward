# Map: 6.9-Magnitude Earthquake Shakes Chile
**Source**: https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/05/25/world/americas/quake-tracker-chile.html
**Date**: May 25, 2026
**Author**: William B. Davis, Madison Dong, Judson Jones, John Keefe, Joey K. Lee, Bea Malsky
**Keywords**: earthquake, Chile, USGS, shake intensity, mapping, seismic, aftershocks, Calama, data visualization

## Elevator pitch
An interactive NYT map tracks a 6.9-magnitude earthquake near Calama, Chile, combining USGS shake intensity data with population density overlays and real-time aftershock monitoring.

## Takeaways
- A 6.9-magnitude earthquake struck 19 miles east of Calama, Chile, at 5:52 PM local time on May 25, 2026.
- The interactive map layers shake intensity (Modified Mercalli scale) with LandScan population density data from Oak Ridge National Laboratory.
- Aftershocks are tracked within a 100-mile radius over a seven-day window, with real-time updates.
- The visualization combines earthquake epicenter data, shake-severity mapping, population exposure analysis, and temporal aftershock charts.
- Exemplifies modern disaster journalism where cartographic and data visualization tools convey complex seismic information to the public.

## Synthesis
This New York Times interactive feature maps a significant 6.9-magnitude earthquake that struck near Calama in northern Chile. The piece, produced by a team of six graphics and data journalists, exemplifies the evolution of disaster reporting where interactive cartography serves as the primary storytelling medium rather than supplementary illustration.

The visualization operates on multiple layers: a shake intensity map using the USGS Modified Mercalli Intensity scale shows where shaking was felt, overlaid with LandScan population density data to contextualize human exposure. An epicenter marker locates the quake 19 miles east of Calama, while concentric circles and color gradients communicate the geographic spread of seismic energy. The piece also incorporates an aftershock tracker monitoring subsequent seismic events within a 100-mile radius over a seven-day period, along with a temporal chart showing when quakes and aftershocks occurred.

This approach reflects a broader trend in news cartography where static maps are replaced by data-rich, updatable interactive visualizations. The combination of real-time USGS data feeds, population exposure analysis, and clear visual hierarchy makes complex geophysical information accessible to a general audience. For the mapping community, the piece demonstrates best practices in crisis mapping: layered information design, authoritative data sourcing, temporal awareness, and human-centric framing that prioritizes impact on populations over purely scientific metrics.
