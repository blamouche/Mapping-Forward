# Building the Geospatial Pipeline: Lessons from the Field
**Source**: https://www.geoweeknews.com/news/building-the-geospatial-pipeline-lessons-from-the-field
**Date**: May 26, 2026
**Author**: Abigail Hart
**Keywords**: geospatial workforce, mentorship, GIS internship, surveying outreach, talent pipeline, early-career development

## Elevator pitch
Two practitioners share practical strategies for developing the next generation of geospatial professionals: hire for character and train for skill, and get early-career talent hands-on with real equipment in the field.

## Takeaways
- Shawn Beecher's 20-year intern program at Spanish Fork City prioritizes character over technical knowledge: "I can teach GIS. I can't teach character."
- A rotating mentorship model where experienced interns train newcomers reduces staff burden while maintaining productivity.
- William Wing's Arizona Young Surveyors Network organized an underground mapping field day at a historic mine, producing a digital twin with GNSS, drones, and 3D laser scanning.
- Hands-on exposure with real tools — not lectures — creates inspiration and career awareness among students.
- Both speakers emphasize starting small with intention, finding 2-5 committed people, and building from there.

## Synthesis
The geospatial industry faces a persistent challenge: the talent pipeline does not build itself. At a recent Geo Week webinar, two practitioners from different corners of the profession — municipal GIS administration and land surveying — shared field-tested approaches to workforce development that go beyond traditional classroom recruitment.

Shawn Beecher, GIS Administrator for Spanish Fork City, Utah, has run a successful intern program for nearly two decades. His philosophy is straightforward but counterintuitive in a field often obsessed with software proficiency: hire for character, not technical knowledge. He recruits students early in their studies, often during introductory GIS courses, giving him one to four years to shape their professional development. Interview questions probe self-awareness and growth mindset rather than tool-specific skills. A rotating mentorship model ensures that newer interns learn from more experienced peers, reducing the training burden on full-time staff. Once competent, interns are given real challenges to solve independently — a strategy Beecher says produces his best performers and occasionally future full-time hires. His closing advice encapsulates the ethos: give credit generously, treat interns as team members, and invest in their success.

William Wing approaches pipeline-building through high-energy outreach. As a supporter of the Arizona Young Surveyors Network, he organized a field day that brought 25 participants underground at the historic Good Enough Mine in Tombstone, Arizona. The group conducted GNSS control surveys, drone flights, and 3D laser scanning to produce a detailed digital twin of the mine. The goal was not just technical exposure but inspiration — showing students that the same tools and techniques apply across surveying, GIS, mining, and geology. Wing's model uses stations, rotations, and real equipment rather than lectures, with vendors loaning gear and volunteers managing logistics.

Both speakers agree on fundamentals: start small with intention, find a small committed team, assign clear responsibilities, and build incrementally. The return on investment — in talent, industry reputation, and personal satisfaction — justifies the effort. For an industry grappling with an aging workforce and rapidly evolving technology, these practitioner-led approaches offer a replicable template for cultivating the next generation.
