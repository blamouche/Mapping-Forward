## Elevator pitch
After 18 years, the first continent-wide magnetotelluric survey has mapped the electrical conductivity of North America's crust and upper mantle at unprecedented resolution, revealing the continent's hidden architecture from ancient cratons to active volcanic plumbing.

## Takeaways
- The USMTArray collected over 1,800 magnetotelluric stations on a 70-km grid across the contiguous U.S., completed in 2024 after 18 years.
- Magnetotellurics uses natural electromagnetic signals (solar activity and lightning) to image subsurface conductivity from hundreds of meters to hundreds of kilometers depth.
- Applications span mineral and geothermal exploration, volcanic hazard assessment, tectonic studies, and space weather risk mapping for power grids.
- The dataset is fully public with no embargo, representing a unique uniform continental-scale geophysical resource.
- The method is uniquely sensitive to partial melt, clay, graphite, and metallic sulfides, making it ideal for resource exploration and volcanic system imaging.

## Synthesis
This Editors' Vox piece in Eos describes the completion of the United States Magnetotelluric Array (USMTArray), an 18-year project that produced the first high-resolution continent-wide map of electrical conductivity beneath North America. The authors explain magnetotellurics (MT) as a passive geophysical method that uses natural electromagnetic variations — from solar activity and global lightning — to measure how the Earth conducts electricity at different depths, essentially creating a 3D "electrical anatomy" of the continent.

The USMTArray deployed over 1,800 stations on a systematic 70-kilometer grid across the contiguous United States, funded successively by NSF, NASA, and USGS. Each station recorded electric and magnetic field variations for approximately three weeks in remote locations, far from infrastructure that could interfere with measurements. The resulting dataset and a new 3D conductivity model of the crust and upper mantle were published in Reviews of Geophysics.

The applications are remarkably diverse. MT studies reveal ancient orogens and rifts that shaped continental assembly, map hydration of mantle lithosphere to assess cratonic stability, image melt zones beneath active volcanoes and orogenic plateaus, and constrain water budgets in subduction zones. For the resource sector, MT's sensitivity to conductive phases like graphite, metallic sulfides, and clay makes it invaluable for mineral and geothermal exploration.

A particularly timely application concerns space weather hazards: MT impedance data are essential for estimating surface electric fields during geomagnetic storms that can damage power grids. These data inform both national geoelectric hazard maps and real-time risk estimates — a critical infrastructure protection tool as solar activity intensifies. The fully public, unrestricted nature of the USMTArray dataset represents a major contribution to open geophysical science.
