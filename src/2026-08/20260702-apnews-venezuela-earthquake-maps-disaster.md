## Synthesis
AP News produced a compelling visual story of the Venezuela earthquake disaster using three data-driven maps that combine seismic data, building footprints, and displacement tracking. Two earthquakes struck Venezuela on June 24, 2026 — just 39 seconds apart — and by July 2, more than 2,200 people had died. Thousands of displaced people were sleeping in the open, and the maps were designed to make the scale of the catastrophe comprehensible to readers and useful to humanitarian responders.

The mapping project drew on multiple open geospatial data sources. Building footprint data from the Overture Maps Foundation was used to assess structural exposure and damage across affected areas. Overture Maps provides machine-derived building footprints that fill gaps where OpenStreetMap data is sparse, particularly in regions with less active volunteer mapping communities. This combination of OSM and Overture data was published as a humanitarian dataset on the Humanitarian Data Exchange (HDX), providing responders with a comprehensive view of the built environment in the earthquake zone.

The three maps serve complementary purposes. One shows seismic intensity — the geographic spread of ground shaking and its severity. Another focuses on building damage, using Overture building footprints to estimate how many structures were exposed to destructive forces. The third visualizes displacement patterns, showing where affected populations have moved or are sheltering. Together, they tell a story that no single map could convey: the physics of the earthquake, its impact on the built environment, and the human consequences.

This project exemplifies how modern crisis cartography leverages open data ecosystems. The Overture Maps Foundation's machine-derived building footprints, combined with OSM's community-mapped infrastructure and humanitarian datasets, enable rapid production of situation-aware maps in the critical first days after a disaster. For the Venezuela earthquake, this meant that within days, responders and the public could access detailed maps of the affected areas — a capability that would have taken weeks or months to assemble using traditional methods. The AP's use of these data sources for journalistic storytelling also demonstrates how open geospatial data is transforming disaster reporting, making the invisible dimensions of catastrophe — structural exposure, displacement, seismic intensity — visible and actionable.
