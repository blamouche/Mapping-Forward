# How Creative Maps Make Air Pollution More Visible
**Source**: https://theconversation.com/how-creative-maps-make-air-pollution-more-visible-284849
**Date**: 2026-07-03
**Author**: The Conversation
**Keywords**: air pollution mapping, citizen science, York air map, bicycle monitoring, air quality visualization, creative data mapping, environmental data, pollution exposure, public health

## Elevator pitch
Researchers at the University of York combined bicycle-mounted air quality sensors, citizen science, and creative visualization to create the York Air Map — revealing how pollution exposure varies dramatically over just a few meters and making invisible environmental data tangible for the public.

- 16 small air quality monitors attached to bicycles collected data during daily cycling commutes across York over six-week blocks
- Citizen scientists could see pollution data change in real-time through sensors and phone apps, connecting statistics to lived experience

## Synthesis
Researchers from the University of York have demonstrated how creative mapping can make the invisible problem of air pollution tangible and meaningful for the public. Their project, the York Air Map, combines atmospheric science with creative design to transform complex environmental data into accessible visualizations that reveal how pollution shapes everyday life.

Traditional air quality monitoring relies on fixed stations positioned at specific locations around a city. While these provide highly accurate measurements, they can only represent conditions at a limited number of points. Air quality, however, varies significantly across environments and can change dramatically over short distances depending on traffic volume, street layout, weather conditions, and local emission sources. Two people traveling the same route at different times may experience very different levels of exposure.

The York Air Map project addresses this spatial gap using 16 small air quality monitors attached to bicycles, with citizen scientists collecting data during their daily cycling commutes across the city of 200,000 people. Each cyclist sees data change in real-time through a commercial sensor and phone app, so spikes in pollution can be attributed to what they are currently experiencing — road works, high-polluting vehicles, riding through a park or past construction sites. This approach captures data from spaces that fixed monitoring might miss.

The findings challenge common perceptions. While yearly averages might suggest pollution exposure in a city like York is low, people can be exposed to high concentrations over short timescales, and repeated exposure may cause long-term health effects. The creative component of the project extends beyond data collection: pollution data has been transformed into maps, visualizations, an informative zine, public exhibitions, and community workshops. One exhibition displayed smellable filters exposed to various pollution sources, letting visitors connect abstract statistics with recognizable sources like wood burning, BBQs, and perfumes.

Citizen scientists also kept pollution diaries, which showed that riders became more attuned to their surroundings over time, noticing smells and sources more clearly when app spikes appeared. This creative mapping approach demonstrates how combining rigorous environmental measurement with participatory design can make invisible environmental threats visible, personal, and actionable — a model that could be replicated in cities worldwide to engage communities with their local air quality.
