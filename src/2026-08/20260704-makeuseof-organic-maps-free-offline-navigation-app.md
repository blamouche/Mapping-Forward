# I Found a Free Map App That Works With No Signal — and No Subscription

## Elevator pitch
Organic Maps is a free, open-source offline navigation app built on OpenStreetMap data that delivers turn-by-turn routing, search, and voice guidance without cellular signal or subscription — with zero tracking or data collection verified by the Exodus Privacy Project.

## Takeaways
- Organic Maps is built on OpenStreetMap data, forked from MAPS.ME in late 2020 by original developers Alexander Borsuk and Viktar Havaka
- Users download regions ahead of time for fully offline search, routing, and turn-by-turn voice guidance for driving, cycling, or walking
- The app collects no location data or tracking history, verified by the Exodus Privacy Project's Android audit
- A 2025 community governance dispute led to a fork called CoMaps, created by contributors concerned about transparency and shareholder profit-taking
- As of August 2025, the map data is distributed under a custom license and the data-generation tooling is no longer publicly available
- Data quality depends entirely on OSM volunteer contributions, varying by region — cities and popular trails are detailed, rural areas can be sparse
- Supports GPX/KML import-export, bookmarks, contour lines, hiking and cycling layers, and search categories including ATMs, cafés, bus stops, and campgrounds
- Does not offer live traffic, real-time transit, or ride-hailing — it is an offline-first tool, not a Google Maps replacement

## Synthesis
Organic Maps is a free, open-source offline navigation app that excels at one unglamorous but critical task: providing reliable maps and routing when there is no cellular signal. Built on OpenStreetMap data, the app was forked from MAPS.ME in late 2020 by two of its original developers, Alexander Borsuk and Viktar Havaka, after MAPS.ME's new owners took the app closed-source and leaned into ads and monetization. Organic Maps had its first public release in June 2021, built around offline-first navigation as the core design principle rather than a fallback for bad reception.

In daily use, users download the regions they need — an entire country or a smaller area — and once saved, the app has little reason to check in with the internet. Search for addresses, street names, and points of interest, plan routes, and get turn-by-turn voice guidance for driving, cycling, or walking all work without cellular data. Privacy is a standout feature: Organic Maps says it does not collect location data or track history, a claim verified by the Exodus Privacy Project, which scanned the Android build for embedded trackers. This stands out in an app category where behavioral location data is a core business asset.

The project's open-source community credentials became more complicated in 2025, when a governance dispute led to a community fork called CoMaps. An open letter from community members to Organic Maps' Estonian-registered company shareholders went unresolved, and a group of contributors split to build CoMaps independently. Additionally, as of August 2025, the map data Organic Maps uses is distributed under a custom license, and the data-generation tooling is no longer publicly available — the app's code remains open, but the map files underneath are somewhat less open than before.

Organic Maps' data quality depends entirely on what OpenStreetMap volunteers have contributed, which varies by region. Cities, popular hiking trails, and well-mapped countries tend to be detailed and accurate, sometimes surfacing trailheads or footpaths that commercial apps skip. Less-traveled rural regions or fast-changing commercial districts can be thinner. The app does not offer live traffic, real-time transit, or ride-hailing integration. It is not a Google Maps replacement but a complementary tool for road trips through patchy coverage, hikes where battery conservation matters, and international travel where roaming data is expensive. With GPX/KML import-export, bookmarks, contour lines, and outdoor layers, it earns its place as the offline layer worth keeping installed.
