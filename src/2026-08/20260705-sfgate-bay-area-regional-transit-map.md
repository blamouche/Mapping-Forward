## Synthesis
The Metropolitan Transportation Commission (MTC) has introduced the latest version of its regional transit map, a product designed to make the Bay Area's notoriously complex transit network navigable at a single glance. Covering 140 miles from Cloverdale to Gilroy across nine counties and 27 distinct transit agencies, the map represents years of design work aimed at finding the "Goldilocks sweet spot" between comprehensiveness and readability.

Gordon Hansen, the MTC's Mapping and Wayfinding project manager, described the core challenge: deciding which of thousands of features to include on a single map, and how to use it in concert with more detailed maps for local-scale exploration. The design choices are deliberately reductive but strategic. Blue lines represent rail services such as BART and Caltrain; yellow lines show long-distance bus routes. Showing 27 individual agency colors or logos would have created an unreadable spaghetti plate. Instead, criteria were developed to identify the most critical bus routes — those reaching airports, tourist destinations, or making key inter-regional connections.

Local bus routes, such as those operated by Muni within San Francisco, are largely omitted, replaced by yellow splotches indicating where individual agencies operate. BART's Jumana Nabti explained this was a deliberate decision to highlight routes best suited for regional connections rather than local trips. The map also features a variety of navigational and whimsical icons — a pumpkin for Half Moon Bay, grapes for Wine Country vineyards, garlic for Gilroy — that draw the eye to regional attractions and landmarks.

The MTC collaborated with Hiking by Transit, an online guide for transit-accessible hikes, to highlight parks and public lands reachable by transit. The map's largely yellow and blue palette simplifies how routes are depicted while aligning with the MTC's Regional Mapping and Wayfinding initiative to standardize maps and signs across all 27 agencies. Transit map historian Mark Ovenden, author of "Transit Maps of the World," praised the diagram as a recognition of both riders' needs and how agencies can improve ridership by working together.

The map is being introduced to transit stops across the region, and Hansen emphasized it will never be truly finished — it will evolve as services change, making it a living document of the Bay Area's transit geography.
