## Synthesis
Published on July 9, 2026, by Insurance Journal (via Bloomberg), this article reports on the near-collapse of maritime traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — the world's most vital energy conduit — following US military strikes on Iran. Ship-tracking data, visualized using Mapbox and OpenStreetMap, reveals the dramatic slowdown in real time.

The article details how observable movements on July 10 were largely confined to an Iran-approved route near the waterway's northern side, while the US-supported Omani corridor remained quiet. Among larger vessels, only a US-sanctioned supertanker heading out of the Persian Gulf and an Iranian-flagged container ship were visible in the strait. Some vessels may have been crossing with transponders turned off, however.

The decline is stark: in the three weeks since the US-Iran interim deal to reopen Hormuz, average daily transits of commodity vessels were 34, peaking at 59 on June 24. On the day of the strikes, only 14 commodity carriers crossed — the lowest since the mid-June peace deal. During wartime, daily counts were below 20 on most days. Liquefied natural gas tanker traffic remained at a complete standstill, though two empty vessels were heading toward the eastern entrance.

The article also reports renewed electronic interference in the Gulf of Oman, with vessels near Limah, Oman appearing to travel at implausible speeds of 30+ knots — a telltale sign of defense system activation that can affect ship transponder signals and corrupt tracking data. This phenomenon, well-documented in maritime monitoring, underscores how geopolitical conflict can degrade the reliability of the very mapping and tracking systems that the shipping industry depends on. The combination of physical disruption and electronic interference illustrates the complex interplay between mapping technology, maritime safety, and global geopolitics.
