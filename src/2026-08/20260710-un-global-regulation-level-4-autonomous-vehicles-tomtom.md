# UN adopts the first global regulation allowing fully autonomous vehicles
**Source**: https://www.tomtom.com/newsroom/news/un-adopts-the-first-global-regulation-allowing-fully-autonomous-vehicles
**Date**: 2026-07-10
**Author**: TomTom Editorial Team (Matthew Beedham and Snigdha Bansal)
**Keywords**: UN, autonomous vehicles, Level 4 ADS, regulation, UNECE WP.29, mapping, location intelligence, safety framework, TomTom, automotive

## Elevator pitch
The UN's World Forum has adopted the first global regulatory framework for Level 4 autonomous driving systems, establishing performance-based safety standards that explicitly recognize the role of mapping and location intelligence in the automated driving ecosystem.

## Takeaways
- On June 24, 2026, UNECE WP.29 adopted the world's first regulatory framework for fully driverless Level 4 Automated Driving Systems (ADS)
- The regulation is performance-based and technology-neutral: manufacturers can use any sensor combination, but must prove their system meets safety objectives through structured safety cases
- The framework explicitly recognizes the automated driving ecosystem including mapping, positioning, software, and sensing — acknowledging maps as a complement to real-time perception
- Supported by major automotive markets: Canada, China, EU, Japan, UK, and US — covering motorway operations, urban environments, and automated parking
- Over $200 billion has been invested in L4/L5 technology without any official pathway to legal operation; this regulation clears one of the biggest hurdles to deployment

## Synthesis
Published on July 10, 2026, on the TomTom Newsroom blog, this article (co-authored by Matthew Beedham and Snigdha Bansal) reports on the United Nations' adoption of the world's first global technical regulation for Level 4 Automated Driving Systems (ADS). On June 24, 2026, the UNECE World Forum for Harmonization of Vehicle Regulations (WP.29) adopted the framework, creating a standardized approach for how self-driving systems are defined, assessed, and brought safely onto public roads.

The regulation is notable for its performance-based, technology-neutral approach. Rather than prescribing specific technical solutions, it sets performance requirements with validation, monitoring, and reporting provisions. Manufacturers must demonstrate through structured safety cases — similar to processes used in aerospace and nuclear industries — that their systems meet defined safety objectives across real-world scenarios. The system must perform the full Dynamic Driving Task (DDT): sensing the environment, perceiving and interpreting surroundings, planning and decision-making, and executing vehicle control.

A key feature for the mapping community is the framework's explicit recognition of the full automated driving ecosystem. Francois Guichard, Secretary to the UNECE's Working Party on Automated/Autonomous and Connected Vehicles, highlights that the regulation recognizes elements including mapping, positioning, software, and sensing as interconnected components contributing to safe system behavior. TomTom's Stephanie Leonard, Global Head of Government and Regulatory Affairs, notes that the regulation 'correctly focused on the full automated driving ecosystem to ensure safe system behavior, where mapping can play a key role.'

Maps provide contextual anticipation that complements real-time sensor data: knowing a crosswalk lies ahead helps identify pedestrians with greater confidence, and knowing a bike lane runs alongside a road shapes intersection approach even when visibility is obstructed. The regulation was developed with support from Canada, China, the EU, Japan, the UK, and the US, covering use cases from motorway operations to complex urban environments and automated parking. With over $200 billion invested in L4/L5 technology and no previous pathway to legal operation, this framework clears one of the industry's biggest hurdles — though widespread deployment still requires progress on infrastructure, road safety, and local legislative incorporation.
