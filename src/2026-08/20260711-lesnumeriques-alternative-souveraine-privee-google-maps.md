## Synthesis
Published on August 9, 2026, in Les Numériques by Corentin Béchade, this article opens with a candid admission: despite four years of effort to replace Google Maps with sovereign and privacy-respecting alternatives, the author still uses Maps faute de mieux. The article argues that the problem is fundamentally political rather than technical.

Google Maps, the author explains, has become far more than a navigation tool — it is effectively a social network where every user review, photo, and comment enriches its database and cements its market dominance. This crowdsourced data creates a network effect: since everyone uses Maps, nobody contributes to competing platforms, which in turn ensures everyone keeps using Maps. The author calls this a vicious (or virtuous, depending on perspective) circle that even Apple, with its vast resources, has failed to break with Plans.

The public clearly wants alternatives. A survey by Cartes.gouv found that 73% of respondents prefer a map produced by a French public institution over one from an international tech giant, and 56% are bothered by mapping apps recording and storing their movements. Yet despite this demand and the existence of interesting, free, privacy-respecting alternatives — including OpenStreetMap-based apps, Cartes.gouv, and HERE WeGo — none has achieved the critical mass of user contributions needed to match Maps' depth of business listings, reviews, and photos.

The article is the first in a series documenting the author's four-year journey to escape Google's ecosystem, promising a no-compromise assessment of what works, what doesn't, and why the mapping market remains so resistant to competition. The conclusion is sobering: quitting Google Maps requires not just individual effort but collective action, as the service's dominance is sustained by the very users who might wish to leave it.
