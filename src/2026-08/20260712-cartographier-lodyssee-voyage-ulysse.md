# Peut-on cartographier l'Odyssée ? Comment géographes antiques et chercheurs d'aujourd'hui ont retracé le voyage d'Ulysse

**Date**: 2026-07-12
**Author**: Pragya Agarwal (Loughborough University)
**Keywords**: history of cartography, Odyssey, Homer, Ulysses, ancient geography, Ptolemy, Strabo, Ortelius, Victor Bérard, Ithaca, Paliki, myth and maps

## Elevator pitch
Coinciding with Christopher Nolan's film adaptation of The Odyssey, a historical cartographer traces centuries of attempts to map Ulysses' journey — from ancient geographers who mixed myth with reality to modern researchers using geoscience and archaeology to locate Homer's Ithaque.

## Takeaways
- The question of whether Ulysses' journey can be mapped has fascinated historians and cartographers for centuries, with some ancient scholars like Eratosthenes dismissing any geographic connection as futile
- Strabo's 17-book Geography and Ptolemy's world maps both included Homeric locations, mixing real and mythological places — a practice that continued on maps for centuries
- Dutch cartographer Abraham Ortelius created the first complete map of Ulysses' wanderings in his Theatrum Orbis Terrarum (late 16th century), even inventing a fictional island that persisted on maps into the 19th and 20th centuries
- Victor Bérard (1912) personally travelled the Mediterranean to reconstitute Ulysses' itinerary, placing Calypso's island near Gibraltar and the Cyclopes' land near Naples
- Cambridge and Aberdeen researchers recently argued that Homer never describes Ithaque as an island, making Paliki on Céphalonie a more credible candidate, supported by geoscientific and archaeological evidence of Bronze Age settlement

## Synthesis
Published on July 12, 2026, in The Conversation to coincide with the release of Christopher Nolan's film L'Odyssée, this article by Pragya Agarwal — a visiting professor at Loughborough University studying the history of cartography and mental representations of space — explores the centuries-old fascination with mapping Ulysses' ten-year journey home from the Trojan War.

The article traces the tension between those who dismiss any geographic reading of the Odyssey as literary pursuit and those who see its spatial dimensions as central to the narrative. The ancient Greek scholar Eratosthène, first to measure Earth's circumference, famously dismissed the quest: 'You will find the setting of Ulysses' wanderings when you find the cobbler who sewed the bag of the winds.' Yet Polybius, six centuries after Homer, argued the Odyssey relayed a real journey embellished with mythology, citing fishing techniques near Scylla that matched Sicilian practices.

The cartographic history is rich. Strabo's Geography placed Homeric islands in the Indian Ocean. Ptolemy's second-century world map included locations like Lotophagitis, the Circaeum Promontorium, and the Sirenusae Insulae — though his erroneous Earth circumference estimate made precise mapping impossible. In the late 16th century, Abraham Ortelius created the first complete map of Ulysses' voyage in his Theatrum Orbis Terrarum, placing Ithaque at modern Corfu and inventing a fictional island for Calypso that cartographers copied for centuries. In 1912, Victor Bérard personally retraced the Mediterranean journey, arguing Phoenician navigation charts influenced Homer.

The most recent contribution comes from Cambridge and Aberdeen researchers who argue that Homer never describes Ithaque as an island but as a territory belonging to a larger island, making Paliki on Céphalonie's west coast a stronger candidate. Geoscientific and archaeological studies confirm Paliki as a significant Bronze Age site. The article concludes that whether real or mythological, the map of Ulysses' journey reveals how ancient peoples perceived their world — and that maps are ultimately stories we tell ourselves about the unknown.
