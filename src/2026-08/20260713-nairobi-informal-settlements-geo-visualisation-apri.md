# Wicked urban problems in Africa: geo-visualising the proliferation of informal settlements in Nairobi
**Source**: https://afripoli.org/wicked-urban-problems-in-africa-geo-visualisation-of-proliferation-of-informal-settlements-in-nairobi
**Date**: 2026-07-13
**Author**: Mildred M. Ambani Songoro (APRI – Africa Policy Research Institute)
**Keywords**: GIS, geo-visualisation, informal settlements, Nairobi, ArcGIS StoryMaps, 3D flood simulation, participatory mapping, KISIP, KENSUP, Kenya, urban planning, remote sensing

## Elevator pitch
An APRI policy brief demonstrates how geo-visualisation tools — ArcGIS StoryMaps and 3D flood simulations — can bridge the methodological gaps in mapping Nairobi's rapidly expanding informal settlements, transforming fragmented data into actionable intelligence for urban policy.

## Takeaways
- Over 60% of Nairobi's population lives in informal settlements, with Kibera growing from 199,339 (2019) to 549,322 (2025) at an 18.3% annual rate
- Current mapping methodologies are fragmented across institutions, using disjointed tools (ODK Central, Excel, QGIS, ArcGIS) with no unified data schema, leading to inconsistent and outdated datasets
- The study demonstrates two geo-visualisation tools using Mathare as a case study: ArcGIS StoryMaps for interactive narrative mapping and Africa GeoPortal 3D Flood Simulation for disaster risk visualisation
- Land cover change detection between 2019 and 2025 reveals continuing densification of settlement cores and loss of green buffer zones along the Mathare River corridor
- Policy recommendations include mainstreaming geo-visualisation in urban planning, developing a centralised data integration platform, and strengthening community participation through Participatory GIS (PGIS)

## Synthesis
Published on July 13, 2026, by the Africa Policy Research Institute, this policy brief by Mildred M. Ambani Songoro examines the persistent challenge of mapping informal settlements in Nairobi and proposes geo-visualisation as a transformative solution. The study situates the problem within Kenya's development priorities under SDG 11 (Sustainable Cities and Communities), noting that despite decades of policy interventions — from colonial-era segregation through KENSUP and KISIP — informal settlements continue to grow at alarming rates.

The brief traces the historical evolution of informal settlement mapping in Nairobi from early aerial photography (1993) through the landmark Map Kibera Project (2009), which revolutionised participatory mapping using GPS devices and OpenStreetMap. Despite legislative frameworks like the County Government Act (2012) and Physical and Land Use Planning Act (2019) mandating GIS-based planning databases, mapping remains underdeveloped and fragmented. The 2025 KISIP exercise mapped 209 settlements using a multi-tool workflow (ODK Central, Excel, QGIS, ArcGIS) but was inherently fragmented, requiring manual data transfers and relying on potentially outdated ESRI basemaps.

The study's methodological contribution is a dual-tool geo-visualisation approach demonstrated through a Mathare case study. ArcGIS StoryMaps integrates high-resolution Planet Imagery (2019–2025), thematic maps, and satellite imagery into an interactive narrative accessible to non-specialists. A swipe template enables side-by-side comparison of land cover changes, while quantitative change detection reveals 65.3% bareland-to-built-up conversion. The Africa GeoPortal 3D Flood Simulation models flood scenarios based on April 2024 rainfall data, generating water depth layers visualised in an interactive 3D web scene using building footprint data from the ArcGIS Living Atlas.

The policy recommendations are practical and multi-layered: institutionalise geo-visualisation within urban planning frameworks, develop a centralised interoperable platform as a 'single source of truth', invest in VHR imagery and drone mapping capabilities through a National Urban Data Infrastructure, and strengthen community participation through PGIS practices. The brief argues that geo-visualisation tools can transform complex databases into visually intuitive formats that bridge the communication gap between technical experts and policymakers.
