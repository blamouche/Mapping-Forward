France's IGN has consolidated its mapping services into Cartes.gouv.fr, a sovereign, collaborative alternative to Google Maps and Street View, with 87% of French citizens supporting state-managed mapping tools independent of US tech giants.

## Synthesis
Published on July 14, 2026, in L'Éveil de la Haute-Loire, this article by Maxime Escot examines Cartes.gouv.fr, the IGN's unified mapping platform designed as a sovereign French alternative to Google Maps and Google Street View. The article arrives at a moment of heightened awareness about digital sovereignty, with the IGN (Institut National de l'Information Géographique et Forestière) positioning its consolidated platform as both a practical tool and a statement of independence from GAFAM dominance.

The article cites a recent Ipsos BVA study commissioned by the IGN revealing striking public sentiment: 87% of French citizens judge it indispensable or important that the State manages public cartographic tools independent of major American or Chinese multinationals. More than eight in ten French people say they are ready to modify their habits and usage to do without GAFAM services — a finding that suggests the political will exists for sovereign alternatives to succeed if they are competitive.

Cartes.gouv.fr consolidates services previously scattered across multiple IGN platforms, serving as a successor to Géoportail. A key component is Panoramax, the French alternative to Google Street View, which already incorporates 110 million photos. Unlike Street View's vehicle-based capture model, Panoramax operates on a collaborative model, allowing citizens to contribute imagery — an approach that aligns with the platform's broader emphasis on participatory cartography.

The platform represents a broader strategy of digital sovereignty in the cartographic domain. In a digital landscape dominated by American and Chinese players, the IGN's initiative asserts that geographic information — a strategic asset for governance, defence, and economic development — should not be entirely dependent on foreign multinational platforms. The article is partially paywalled, but the available content reveals a significant development in the European digital sovereignty movement, specifically in the domain of geographic information.
