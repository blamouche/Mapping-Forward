# I stopped using Google Maps in Android Auto and switched to an open-source navigator that respects my privacy
**Source**: https://www.howtogeek.com/i-stopped-using-google-maps-in-android-auto-and-switched-to-an-open-source-navigator-that-respects-my-privacy/
**Date**: 2026-07-15
**Author**: How-To Geek
**Keywords**: Google Maps, Android Auto, open-source, OsmAnd, Organic Maps, privacy, OpenStreetMap, navigation

## Elevator pitch
How-To Geek explores replacing Google Maps on Android Auto with open-source alternatives like OsmAnd and Organic Maps that work offline, collect no identity-tied data, and respect user privacy — at the cost of live traffic and business reviews.

## Takeaways
- Open-source navigators like OsmAnd and Organic Maps work fully offline with pre-downloaded OpenStreetMap data
- Neither app collects data tied to user identity, and both are free of advertising networks
- Getting these apps to display through Android Auto requires some setup since the platform tries to block non-Play Store apps
- The primary trade-off is losing live traffic updates and nearby business recommendations
- Community reactions highlight the ongoing debate between privacy and convenience in navigation

## Synthesis
The article documents a growing movement of users seeking privacy-respecting alternatives to Google Maps for in-car navigation. With Google Maps collecting extensive data — including names, email addresses, phone numbers, physical addresses, search history, audio data, and photos, all tied to user identity — some drivers are turning to open-source alternatives built on OpenStreetMap data. Two apps stand out: OsmAnd and Organic Maps, both available on the Play Store and both offering fully offline navigation without identity-tied data collection.

Setting up these alternatives on Android Auto requires some preparation. Users need to download map data ahead of time, and the platform's tendency to block non-Play Store apps means some configuration is needed. However, since both apps are now on the Play Store, the process is more straightforward than in the past. The core navigation experience is solid, with both apps offering turn-by-turn directions using community-mapped OpenStreetMap data.

The trade-offs are significant. Without live traffic data, users cannot automatically reroute around congestion. Business reviews, nearby recommendations, and the social discovery features that make Google Maps a daily-use app are absent. The article's comment section reveals a sharp divide: privacy advocates argue that the data collection is excessive and unnecessary for navigation, while others point out that phones, cars, and cameras already track location data, making the navigation app switch marginal in practice. The debate reflects a broader tension in the mapping ecosystem between the convenience of connected, ad-supported platforms and the privacy of community-built alternatives.
