# Adieu Waze et Google Maps ? Ce GPS français voit à travers 50 mètres de béton

**Author**: Journal du Geek

## Elevator pitch
French startup Wheere has developed a geolocation technology that tracks people and objects through up to 50 meters of concrete with sub-meter precision using low-frequency VHF radio signals, and plans to launch its own satellite constellation by 2027 for global coverage independent of GNSS.

## Takeaways
- Wheere can localize a person or object through up to 50 meters of concrete with sub-meter precision
- The technology uses low-frequency VHF radio signals paired with a proprietary algorithm, not GNSS satellites
- Only four antennas are needed to cover one square kilometer, far less dense than competing indoor positioning solutions
- The startup raised €8.5 million in fresh funding, bringing total to ~€20 million since 2020
- Wheere is planning its own satellite constellation in low orbit, with a first launch targeted for 2027

## Synthesis
Montpellier-based startup Wheere has tackled one of GPS's most persistent weaknesses: the total loss of signal in tunnels, underground parking, and enclosed buildings. While traditional GNSS-based systems like Google Maps and Waze go blind the moment satellite line-of-sight is lost, Wheere's technology maintains sub-meter precision through up to 50 meters of concrete using low-frequency VHF radio signals and a proprietary algorithm that sorts valid signals from the multipath reflections bouncing off walls and metallic structures.

The system's efficiency is notable: just four antennas cover a square kilometer, a dramatically lower infrastructure density than competing indoor positioning solutions. The company has raised approximately €20 million since its founding in 2020, with a recent €8.5 million round intended to scale production and develop an integrated chip for IoT devices.

Currently, Wheere targets industrial applications rather than consumer navigation: tracking isolated workers on sensitive sites, equipment traceability in hospitals, coordinating rescue operations in collapsed buildings, and defense applications where GPS is jammed or unavailable. However, the company's ambitions extend far beyond terrestrial infrastructure. Wheere is developing its own satellite constellation in low orbit, with a first launch hoped for 2027, aiming to provide precise geolocation anywhere on the globe — including areas without any ground infrastructure — independent of existing GNSS systems like GPS or Galileo.

The company plans to expand internationally, prioritizing Europe before attacking North American and Asian markets, while keeping design and production in France. Though Wheere won't replace Waze or Google Maps for daily commutes, its technology could become essential wherever conventional GPS fails.
