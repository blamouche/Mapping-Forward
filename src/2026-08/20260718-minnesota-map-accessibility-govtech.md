# Minnesota Uses Accessibility to Improve Maps' Communication
**Source**: https://www.govtech.com/gov-experience/minnesota-uses-accessibility-to-improve-maps-communication
**Date**: 2026-07-18
**Author**: GovTech
**Keywords**: map accessibility, digital maps, WCAG, federal compliance, Minnesota IT Services, GIS, screen readers, symbology, accessible design

## Elevator pitch
Minnesota IT Services is leading a multi-year effort to make government digital maps accessible to users with disabilities, establishing a map accessibility resource center and training modules ahead of federal compliance deadlines in 2027-2028.

## Takeaways
- Federal law requires state and local governments to make digital products including maps accessible by April 2027 or April 2028 depending on population size
- Minnesota IT Services (MNIT) has built a map accessibility resource center and training modules since 2019
- Application Analyst Jason Ewert emphasizes that maps are communication tools first — a focused message prevents information overload
- Simplifying base maps and aerial photography backgrounds is critical so symbology remains distinguishable for screen readers and assistive tech
- Philadelphia overhauled its Disability Characteristics Map in 2023, creating a model for accessible local government maps

## Synthesis
As federal deadlines approach for digital accessibility compliance, Minnesota is emerging as a leader in making government maps usable by people with disabilities. The state's IT services division (MNIT) has been working on map accessibility since 2019, building both a resource center and training modules to help staff create maps that work with screen readers and other assistive technologies.

Application Analyst Jason Ewert, who works within the Minnesota Pollution Control Agency, frames the challenge around a fundamental principle: a map is a communication tool first and foremost. Without a focused, concise message, maps become overloaded with data layers that overwhelm accessibility tools. The design process must start with the intended message and structure content to make it consumable for users with disabilities.

A key technical challenge is that maps often use aerial photography as backgrounds, which creates visual noise that makes symbology difficult to distinguish. Ewert emphasizes simplifying base maps so that overlay symbols stand out clearly — essential both for visual clarity and for assistive technology interpretation. Minnesota has also formed an internal community of practice where staff address accessibility questions and share what works.

The effort is gaining national traction. Massachusetts offers GIS accessibility guidance for static maps from ArcGIS Pro, and Philadelphia overhauled its Disability Characteristics Map in 2023, an initiative that permanently impacted city government by building broader support for accessibility. As Ewert notes, the solutions being developed now will open new worlds for users who have been excluded from map-based government services.
