# New Interactive Map Highlights Income Inequalities Between N.B. Neighbourhoods
**Source**: https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/new-brunswick/interactive-map-poverty-by-neighbourhood-9.7274378
**Date**: 2026-07-19
**Author**: Hope Edmond, CBC News
**Keywords**: interactive map, income inequality, New Brunswick, Human Development Council, socioeconomic indicators, poverty mapping, Statistics Canada, data visualization, community development

## Elevator pitch
The Human Development Council has launched an interactive map visualising socioeconomic indicators — income, employment, education, family structure — across New Brunswick neighbourhoods, revealing clear disparities between urban cores, cities, and suburbs to inform place-based service delivery.

## Takeaways
- The Human Development Council launched an interactive map showing socioeconomic data by neighbourhood across New Brunswick
- The map is based on Statistics Canada 2021 census data and visualises low income, employment, education, family structure, and senior demographics
- Clear discrepancies exist between suburbs and cities, and between urban cores and surrounding areas
- Suburbs tend to have better socioeconomic status due to lack of public transportation and affordable housing, creating self-reinforcing cycles
- Urban cores concentrate poverty because that is where most social services are located, forcing residents to live nearby

## Synthesis
The Human Development Council, a Saint John-based research organisation, has launched an interactive online map that makes socioeconomic data about New Brunswick neighbourhoods more accessible to community developers, social inclusion teams, and place-based initiatives. The map, called the Key Indicators Dissemination Areas Dashboard, is built on Statistics Canada 2021 census data and breaks down a variety of indicators at the neighbourhood level: low income status, children per household, one-parent families, number of renters, and percentage of seniors.

Liam Fisher, the council's data analyst and research coordinator, explained that the map reveals how poverty is not simply the absence of income but a cluster of correlated factors. Indicators such as one-parent families, unemployment, low income, and housing status tend to correlate together, painting a multidimensional picture of socioeconomic disadvantage that single-metric analyses miss. The visualisation makes these patterns immediately apparent across geographic space.

The map highlights consistent patterns across New Brunswick's three main cities. Suburbs tend to have better socioeconomic status than the cities themselves — Fisher pointed to Saint John compared to neighbouring Rothesay and Quispamsis — because suburbs typically lack public transportation and affordable housing, making them effectively closed off to lower-income residents. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle where suburban advantage persists. Within cities, urban cores consistently show the highest concentration of poverty, because that is where most social services are located, forcing residents who depend on those services to live in those neighbourhoods. The map is publicly available on the Human Development Council's website and is designed to inform targeted service distribution and community development planning.
