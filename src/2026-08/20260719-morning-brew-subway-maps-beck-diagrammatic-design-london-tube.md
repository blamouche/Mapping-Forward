# Why Less Accurate Subway Maps Are More Useful
**Source**: https://www.morningbrew.com/stories/why-less-accurate-subway-maps-are-more-useful
**Date**: 2026-07-19
**Author**: Sam Klebanov, Morning Brew
**Keywords**: subway maps, Harry Beck, London Tube map, diagrammatic design, transit cartography, map usability, geographic accuracy, transit design

## Elevator pitch
Morning Brew explores how Harry Beck's revolutionary 1930s London Tube map design — which prioritised utility over geographic accuracy — established the diagrammatic mapping principle that makes modern subway maps worldwide more useful than geographically precise alternatives.

## Takeaways
- Harry Beck's 1930s London Tube map revolutionised transit cartography by prioritising usability over geographic accuracy
- Beck's design used only vertical, horizontal, and 45-degree-angle lines in bold contrasting colours for visual navigation
- The map removed cluttering city features like street names to avoid distracting viewers
- Central areas with the most intersecting lines were zoomed in, abandoning accurate neighbourhood scale
- Cities worldwide continue to draw inspiration from Beck's design, creating metro maps that resemble electrical circuit diagrams

## Synthesis
Morning Brew's Sam Klebanov examines the counterintuitive design principle that makes modern subway maps more useful by being less geographically accurate. The article traces this principle to Harry Beck, an Underground drafter who in the 1930s created a revolutionary London Tube map that abandoned geographic fidelity in favour of diagrammatic clarity. Beck recognised that existing tube maps, which overlaid train lines on detailed London geography with twisting routes and cluttered street names, were more confusing than helpful to passengers.

Beck's solution was to strip away geographic detail and present the network as a clean diagram. His design featured only vertical, horizontal, and 45-degree-angle train lines rendered in bold contrasting colours, making line connections and interchange points immediately legible. City features that cluttered previous maps — street names, neighbourhood boundaries, accurate distances — were removed entirely. The diagram also abandoned uniform geographic scale, instead zooming in on the busy central areas where most lines intersect and where navigation decisions are most complex, while compressing the outer reaches where stations are farther apart and simpler to navigate.

This design philosophy, which makes metro maps resemble electrical circuit diagrams rather than geographic representations, has been adopted worldwide. New York's recently revamped subway map follows the same principle, featuring boldly coloured angular train lines overlaid on a deliberately simplified depiction of the five boroughs. The enduring success of Beck's approach demonstrates a fundamental insight in cartographic design: the purpose of a transit map is not to show where things are in geographic space, but to show how they connect in network space. For passengers, understanding that three transfers are needed to reach a destination is more useful than knowing the exact distance or compass direction — a lesson that continues to influence map design across transportation systems globally.
