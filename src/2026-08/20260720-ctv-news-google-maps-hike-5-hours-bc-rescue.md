## Synthesis
Hikers recently rescued from an extremely challenging trail in British Columbia had planned their excursion using Google Maps, which estimated the hike would take approximately five hours. The actual conditions proved far more demanding, requiring rescue intervention and prompting search and rescue teams to issue a public warning about the perils of relying on consumer navigation applications for backcountry trip planning.

The incident underscores a fundamental limitation of Google Maps and similar navigation apps when applied to wilderness environments: their time estimates are optimised for urban and road-network contexts and do not account for factors such as elevation gain, trail condition, terrain difficulty, weather, and the physical demands of unmaintained routes. The five-hour estimate that lured the hikers into a false sense of preparedness illustrates how the authority that users grant to digital navigation tools in everyday settings can become dangerous when transferred to backcountry contexts where the underlying data and algorithms are not fit for purpose.

British Columbia search and rescue teams have repeatedly warned hikers to use specialised resources — such as Parks Canada trail reports, provincial park websites, and dedicated hiking apps with trail-specific data — rather than general-purpose navigation apps. This case adds to a growing body of incidents where Google Maps has directed hikers onto inappropriate or dangerous routes, including similar cases in other regions. The pattern highlights the need for greater awareness of the limitations of consumer mapping platforms in outdoor recreation contexts and the importance of using purpose-built tools for wilderness navigation.
