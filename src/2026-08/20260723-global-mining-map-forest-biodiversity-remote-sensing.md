# Global Map Reveals Mining's Uneven Toll on Forests and Biodiversity
**Source**: https://phys.org/news/2026-07-global-reveals-uneven-toll-forests.html
**Date**: 2026-07-23
**Author**: Tohoku University / Phys.org
**Keywords**: mining, deforestation, biodiversity, remote sensing, machine learning, global map, green energy minerals

## Elevator pitch
Japanese researchers used remote sensing and machine learning to produce the first global map of mined commodities, revealing that green-energy minerals like lithium pose disproportionate biodiversity risks despite lower deforestation footprints compared to gold and coal.

## Takeaways
- Researchers analyzed ~70,000 mining sites across 20 commodities using remote sensing and machine learning
- Mining caused 16,268 km² of forest loss between 2001 and 2022 — an area roughly the size of Beijing
- Gold and coal remain the largest drivers of deforestation, but lithium shows highest biodiversity risk despite lower forest loss
- Most forest loss occurred in rainforests in the Amazon, Southeast Asia, and the Congo Basin
- The study published in Nature Communications reveals a critical shift: energy transition minerals exert subtle but serious pressures on ecologically sensitive habitats

## Synthesis
A groundbreaking study led by researchers from Tohoku University and the National Institute for Environmental Studies (NIES) has produced the first comprehensive global map of mined commodities, revealing the uneven impact of mineral extraction on forests and biodiversity worldwide. Published in Nature Communications, the research combines remote sensing, machine learning, and public biodiversity datasets to quantify how mining is reshaping ecosystems globally.

The researchers analyzed approximately 70,000 mining sites across 20 commodities, finding that mining activities caused the loss of 16,268 km² (6,300 square miles) of forest between 2001 and 2022 — an area roughly comparable to the size of Beijing. The majority of this loss occurred in the world's most critical rainforest ecosystems: the Amazon, Southeast Asia, and the Congo Basin.

The study's most significant finding is the divergence between deforestation and biodiversity impacts. While gold and coal remain the largest drivers of deforestation in absolute terms, green energy minerals such as lithium exhibit some of the highest biodiversity risks despite being associated with lower levels of forest loss. This contrast highlights a critical and previously underappreciated dynamic: the energy transition's mineral demands may be exerting subtle but equally serious pressures on ecologically sensitive habitats.

Lithium extraction in salt flat and wetland environments, for example, can disrupt fragile ecosystems and endanger species despite minimal visible deforestation. Traditional deforestation metrics would miss this impact entirely, making the biodiversity-specific analysis essential for understanding the true environmental cost of the green energy transition.

The global map of mined commodities was produced using automated analysis of satellite imagery, representing an application of remote sensing and machine learning at unprecedented scale. By combining this map with public datasets on forest loss and species extinction risk, the researchers created a tool that can inform policy decisions about where and how minerals are extracted. The study underscores the need for nuanced environmental assessments that go beyond deforestation metrics to capture the full spectrum of ecological impacts, particularly as the world ramps up extraction of minerals essential for renewable energy technologies.
