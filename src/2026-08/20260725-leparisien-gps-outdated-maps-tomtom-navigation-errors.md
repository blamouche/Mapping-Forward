## Synthesis
As millions of drivers take to the roads for summer holidays, Le Parisien explores the various reasons why GPS navigation systems — despite being near-universal — remain fallible. The investigation, featuring commentary from TomTom spokesperson Vincent Martinier, reveals that the root cause of most navigation errors is not satellite positioning but map currency.

According to Martinier, "the lack of updates is at the origin of most errors." Navigation systems rely on complex technologies including satellite positioning, cartography, and external data collection, all of which evolve rapidly. When any of these components falls behind, the system can produce erroneous guidance — directing drivers through abandoned roads, miscalculating traffic jams, or missing recently constructed roundabouts.

The problem is most acute for built-in car navigation systems — those with screens embedded in the dashboard. The older the vehicle, the greater the risk of error, as manufacturers discontinue map updates for older models. Martinier notes that 15 years ago, updates were distributed via CD or USB key, a system now completely obsolete. Some models also suffer from insufficient internal memory, making it impossible to install modern map updates that require gigabytes of data.

Smartphone navigation apps like Google Maps and Waze offer a solution through continuous cloud-based map updates, but they introduce a different vulnerability: they become blind when mobile internet coverage is lost. This creates a paradox where the most up-to-date systems are also the most fragile in rural or mountainous areas.

A specific warning concerns camping-car drivers: using a car-calibrated navigation app in a motorhome can lead to dangerous situations, as the system may route through tunnels or under bridges with insufficient clearance height. Specialized RV navigation apps account for vehicle dimensions, a factor that standard car apps ignore.

Satellite positioning itself, while now highly precise thanks to multi-constellation data (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS), can still falter in specific scenarios — such as when a 110 km/h highway runs parallel to an 80 km/h road, causing the system to confuse the two. Camera-based vehicle data can also introduce errors through misinterpretation of the road environment.
