# Devastating European wildfires in maps - and how they're being tackled
**Source**: https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cj638jx0l53o
**Date**: 2026-07-29
**Author**: Tom Bennett and the Visual Journalism team / BBC
**Keywords**: wildfires, Europe, France, Spain, Portugal, Gironde, Madrid, maps, NASA FIRMS, EFIS, climate change, evacuation

## Elevator pitch
BBC's visual journalism team mapped the devastating July 2026 wildfires across France, Spain, and Portugal using NASA FIRMS satellite data and EFIS information, showing how fires burned areas larger than Greater London and displaced over 300,000 people across the three countries.

## Takeaways
- Since mid-July 2026, wildfires in France, Spain, and Portugal have forced more than 300,000 people to evacuate their homes
- Fires west of Madrid burned approximately 606 sq miles (1,570 sq km) since July 1, more than twice the size of Madrid itself
- In France's Gironde region, more than 200,000 people were evacuated as fires burned 162 sq miles (420 sq km) in under four days—four times the size of Paris
- BBC used NASA FIRMS satellite data and European Forest Fire Information System (EFIS) data to create time-series maps showing fire progression from July 22-28
- The fires became so extreme they generated their own fire thunderstorms, a phenomenon where intense heat creates pyrocumulonimbus clouds
- French President Macron called the Gironde wildfire "the toughest since World War Two"; Spanish PM Sánchez linked the fires to climate change

## Synthesis
On July 29, 2026, the BBC's Visual Journalism team published a comprehensive mapped analysis of the devastating wildfires that tore through France, Spain, and Portugal since mid-July. The article combines satellite data, on-the-ground reporting, and custom cartographic visualizations to tell the story of a fire season that French President Emmanuel Macron called "completely unprecedented" and "the toughest since World War Two."

The BBC's mapping approach relies on NASA's Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS), which provides near-real-time active fire data from satellite observations. The article features multi-panel maps showing the progression of wildfires over time—dark red dots for active fires detected in the past 24 hours, light pink dots for fires recorded since July 23. This time-series visualization reveals how fires spread rapidly: a small fire near Almorox on July 22 grew into two large fronts by July 24, expanded west toward Ávila by July 26 displacing 90,000 people, and by July 28 left a broad swath of burned land west of Madrid.

A particularly effective visualization compares the burned area near Madrid with the size of Greater London. Since July 1, the largest fire burned more than 66,000 hectares (660 sq km)—an area that, overlaid on London, would stretch from Heathrow and Richmond in the west through central London to Walthamstow and Dartford in the east, spanning the diameter of the M25.

In France, the Gironde region around Bordeaux was the worst affected. The BBC maps show how a small blaze west of Bordeaux on July 22 expanded around Le Porge by July 24, grew into multiple large fire fronts by July 26 prompting the evacuation of more than 200,000 people, and by July 28 left most areas as burned land with fewer active hotspots. The fire burned more than 162 sq miles (420 sq km) in less than four days and came as close as nine miles to Bordeaux.

The fires also approached sensitive military and industrial sites, including facilities working on French ballistic missiles, rocket motors, and hazardous chemical processing. The fires became so extreme they created fire thunderstorms—pyrocumulonimbus clouds generated by intense heat—demonstrating how climate change is producing fire behavior that exceeds historical patterns.

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez directly attributed the fires to climate change, stating: "The climate emergency is worsening year after year" and that "climate denial is the worst fuel for the fight against the climate emergency."
