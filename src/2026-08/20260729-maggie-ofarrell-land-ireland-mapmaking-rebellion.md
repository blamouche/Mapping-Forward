## Elevator pitch
Bestselling author Maggie O'Farrell discusses her novel Land and the artistry of mapmaking, exploring how the Ordnance Survey of Ireland became both an instrument of colonial control and a devastating record of pre-famine Ireland.

## Synthesis
National Geographic's interview with Maggie O'Farrell about her novel Land offers a profound meditation on the cultural and political power of mapmaking. The bestselling author of Hamnet has turned her attention to Ireland's cartographic history, specifically the Ordnance Survey of Ireland (1825-1846)—an enterprise that was, at the time, the world's largest mapmaking endeavor.

The Ordnance Survey embodies the dual nature of cartography: simultaneously an act of scientific precision and political control. Originally commissioned by the British government for taxation purposes, the survey required teams of surveyors and mapmakers to traverse and document every corner of the Irish landscape. Yet what began as an administrative tool became something far more haunting: a record of a country on the eve of catastrophe. Just six years after the survey's completion in 1846, the Great Famine had killed a million people and driven nearly two million more to emigrate. The maps became a snapshot of a world that would soon vanish.

O'Farrell's description of mapmaking as a "peculiar mix of science and storytelling, mathematics and artistry" captures the essential tension at the heart of cartography. Maps are never neutral—they embed the perspectives, priorities, and power structures of their makers. The British Ordnance Survey of Ireland imposed English place names and administrative categories onto an Irish landscape with its own Gaelic geography and cultural memory. In this sense, as O'Farrell suggests, maps can be acts of rebellion as much as instruments of control.

The novel's structure—following an unnamed peninsula through thousands of years of human habitation—mirrors the layered nature of maps themselves. Each map is a palimpsest, recording one moment's understanding of a place while erasing or obscuring others. O'Farrell's fascination with maps speaks to a broader cultural yearning in the digital age: the wonder of cartographic representation that many have let fade in an era of ubiquitous GPS and satellite imagery. Land reminds us that behind every map lies a human story of discovery, loss, and the enduring desire to make sense of the world through spatial representation.
