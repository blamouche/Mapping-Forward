# Corporate Home Buying Expands: Maps Reveal Changing Ownership Patterns
**Source**: https://www.esri.com/about/newsroom/blog/mapping-who-owns-us-housing
**Date**: 2026-07-30
**Author**: Patricia Cummens (Esri)
**Keywords**: GIS, housing ownership, corporate investors, parcel data, Center for Geospatial Solutions, Lincoln Institute, property mapping

## Elevator pitch
Esri analysis reveals that 32 mega-investors hold nearly 450,000 single-family US homes, with GIS mapping exposing corporate ownership patterns at the parcel level across the country.

## Takeaways
- 32 "mega-investors" collectively own nearly 450,000 single-family homes; the top 5 hold 300,000 homes (2% of national housing supply).
- 9% of residential land parcels—nearly one in eleven—are held by a corporate entity, according to a Center for Geospatial Solutions report.
- CGS at the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy spent three years developing a method to trace corporate ownership through parcel records and corporate entities.
- GIS mapping reveals that corporate investors are active in diverse locations including college towns and fast-growing suburbs, not just disinvested cities.
- The analysis intersects ownership patterns with community demographics to understand affordability impacts.

## Synthesis
The Center for Geospatial Solutions (CGS) at the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy has published a landmark report, "Who Owns America: Mapping Corporate Ownership of Residential Land," leveraging GIS analysis to expose the scale of corporate investment in US single-family housing. The findings are striking: 32 mega-investors collectively own nearly 450,000 homes, with the top five companies holding 300,000—representing 2% of the combined national housing supply.

The GIS methodology developed over three years goes beyond simple data overlay. CGS analysts created a systematic approach for tracing ownership through parcel records, corporate entity structures, and jurisdiction-level data. This methodological innovation was necessary because corporate ownership is often obscured through complex legal structures, making it invisible in standard property records. By mapping these ownership patterns at the parcel level, the analysis reveals clusters of corporate-owned homes that would otherwise remain hidden in aggregate statistics.

A key finding challenges the dominant media narrative. While national coverage tends to focus on corporate investment in disinvested cities, the GIS analysis reveals that corporate investors are active across diverse community types, including college towns and fast-growing metropolitan suburbs. This geographic insight is only possible through the block-by-block granularity that GIS mapping provides.

The report's associate director, Reina Chano Murray, emphasizes that "the real story of the housing market isn't told at the state or city level; it's told block by block." This philosophy underpins the value of geospatial analysis in housing policy: national and regional statistics can mask important local patterns that directly affect community affordability and character.

The intersection of ownership maps with demographic data adds another analytical dimension, enabling communities to understand how corporate buying patterns relate to neighborhood characteristics and to develop targeted policy responses. This application of GIS to housing policy exemplifies how spatial analysis can inform evidence-based governance and community protection.
