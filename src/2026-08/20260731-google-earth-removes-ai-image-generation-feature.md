# Google Removes AI Image Generation Feature from Google Earth Over Misinformation Concerns
**Source**: https://www.jpost.com/international/article-904277
**Date**: 2026-07-31
**Author**: Jerusalem Post / Reuters
**Keywords**: Google Earth, AI image generation, Nano Banana 2, geospatial deepfakes, satellite imagery, misinformation, SynthID watermark

## Elevator pitch
Google disabled an AI-powered image generation feature in Google Earth just one day after launch, following concerns that users could create geospatial deepfakes — convincing fake satellite imagery grounded in real coordinates that could spread as misinformation.

## Takeaways
- Google added a "Create Image" button to Google Earth's web version on July 30, 2026, powered by Nano Banana 2 AI model, allowing users to generate custom images over real satellite, aerial, and 3D imagery
- Within hours, OSINT researchers and journalists found that the tool could generate convincing geospatial deepfakes at real-world coordinates, including fake damage to military installations
- A fake image of a US military base in Bahrain reportedly circulated in Iranian media, created using Google Earth AI, though real satellite imagery later showed different damage patterns
- Google implemented SynthID watermarking and topic restrictions, but BBC Verify demonstrated it was possible to circumvent the checks and trick Gemini into identifying fake Google Earth images as real
- The feature was disabled on July 31, 2026, with no timeline for its return; Google stated it observed people sharing screenshots that appeared to violate policies

## Synthesis
The rapid rollout and withdrawal of Google Earth's AI image generation feature represents a watershed moment in the intersection of geospatial technology and generative AI. The feature — which allowed users to type text prompts and generate AI images grounded in real satellite coordinates — was conceptually appealing for legitimate uses like historical reconstruction or urban planning visualization. However, its potential for misuse was equally immediate and dramatic.

The incident reveals the fundamental tension in embedding generative AI into authoritative mapping platforms. Google Earth's credibility as a source of factual geographic imagery is its core value proposition. By introducing AI-generated content — even in a separate, labeled layer — Google risked undermining the trust that makes the platform valuable. The fact that screenshots could be detached from their AI-labeled context and circulated as real imagery demonstrated that technical watermarking alone is insufficient when the social media sharing pipeline strips metadata.

The geographic specificity of the tool made it especially dangerous. Unlike general AI image generators that produce obviously stylized content, Google Earth's tool generated images grounded in real 3D coordinates and terrain data, making fakes more credible. The reported misuse — fake military damage imagery circulated in international media — demonstrates how geospatial deepfakes could become instruments of information warfare. This incident will likely influence how all mapping platforms approach AI integration, establishing that provenance verification must extend beyond watermarks into the social distribution chain.
