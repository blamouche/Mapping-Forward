## Synthesis
Mexico City has emerged as one of the world's most congested urban centers, with TomTom Traffic data revealing a 75.9% congestion level in 2025. This places CDMX above cities like Bangalore, Dublin, and Bogotá in terms of traffic severity. The practical consequence: drivers average just 17.4 km/h, covering a mere 4.3 kilometers in a 15-minute window, turning daily commutes into endurance tests.

The article explores the psychological dimension of this congestion. Psychologist Clara Haydée Solís Ponce from UNAM's Facultad de Estudios Superiores Zaragoza explains that road rage—first studied systematically by Dr. Jerry Deffenbacher in 1994—is not merely momentary frustration but a complex phenomenon with clinical, behavioral, and social components. Drivers with impulsivity traits, irritability, and low emotional control are most susceptible.

The urban environment of CDMX amplifies these factors. Reckless maneuvers, disregard for traffic regulations, mobile phone use while driving, and constant lane invasions create a hostile atmosphere that intensifies frustration among law-abiding drivers. Environmental conditions—heat, poor ventilation, noise, accumulated fatigue, and work-related stress—further increase susceptibility to aggressive reactions.

The article highlights a concerning feedback loop: as traffic worsens, stress increases, leading to more aggressive behavior, which in turn compromises road safety for all users. When anger escalates to using the vehicle as an intimidation tool, the situation becomes a genuine public safety crisis. This TomTom data-driven analysis underscores how urban congestion metrics translate into real human impact, making the case for integrated transport planning that addresses both infrastructure and psychological well-being.
