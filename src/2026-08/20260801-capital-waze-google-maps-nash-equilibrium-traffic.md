# Route des vacances : pourquoi Waze et Google Maps ne sont plus toujours les meilleurs pour éviter les bouchons
**Source**: https://www.capital.fr/auto/route-des-vacances-pourquoi-waze-et-google-maps-ne-sont-plus-toujours-les-meilleurs-pour-eviter-les-bouchons-1528944
**Date**: 2026-08-01
**Author**: Capital.fr
**Keywords**: Waze, Google Maps, traffic congestion, Nash equilibrium, navigation apps, route optimization, CO2 emissions, France

## Elevator pitch
Capital.fr explains why Waze and Google Maps — both owned by Google and sharing data — can actually worsen traffic congestion by sending thousands of drivers down the same alternative routes, creating a real-world Nash equilibrium where everyone's attempt to save time results in everyone losing time.

## Takeaways
- Waze and Google Maps share data and send the same alerts and diversions to millions of users simultaneously, causing traffic to flood alternative routes
- The phenomenon mirrors Nash equilibrium: when everyone optimizes individually, the collective outcome worsens — villages become shortcuts, dangerous roads become transit routes, residential streets turn into through-fares
- User complaints on app store reviews are increasing, denouncing irrelevant itineraries, highway-biased routing, and mid-route changes with no real benefit
- Since 2022, France's Climate and Resilience law requires GPS apps to offer CO2-conscious routing, but both apps still heavily favor highway routes
- Users can mitigate the problem by enabling alternative routes, avoiding tolls, or prioritizing distance over travel time in settings

## Synthesis
Capital.fr's analysis highlights a counterintuitive but well-documented problem in the navigation app ecosystem: the very algorithms designed to optimize individual routes can create systemic traffic problems when deployed at scale. The core issue is that Waze and Google Maps, now both under Google's umbrella, share traffic data and simultaneously reroute thousands of drivers to the same alternative roads when a slowdown occurs. Instead of distributing traffic across multiple paths, this creates concentrated bottlenecks on secondary roads that were never designed to handle high volume.

The article invokes Nash equilibrium — a game theory concept where individual optimization leads to collectively suboptimal outcomes — to explain why this happens. When every driver follows the same algorithm's recommendation, the "alternative" route becomes as congested as the original. The consequences extend beyond mere inconvenience: villages become transit corridors, dangerous rural roads see high-speed traffic, and residential streets transform into commuter routes. The social and environmental costs of algorithmic routing are borne by communities along these alternative routes, not by the apps or their users.

The French legislative response — requiring CO2-aware routing since 2022 — represents one policy approach, but Capital suggests users must also take proactive steps. By adjusting settings to prefer distance over time, enable alternative routes, or avoid tolls, drivers can break out of the algorithmic herd behavior. The article implies that the monopoly structure (both apps under Google) exacerbates the problem: when a single entity controls both major navigation apps, there is no competitive pressure to develop genuinely different routing strategies that would distribute traffic more evenly.
