# Face à Google Maps, reprendre les cartes en main
**Source**: https://www.la-croix.com/economie/face-a-google-maps-reprendre-les-cartes-en-main-20260802
**Date**: 2026-08-02
**Author**: Timothée David, La Croix
**Keywords**: Google Maps, cartographic sovereignty, IGN, Michelin, critical cartography, map representation, France

## Elevator pitch
La Croix examines how Google Maps has become a reflex for navigation but raises the question of cartographic sovereignty, as French institutions like IGN and Michelin seek to offer alternative map representations that go beyond Google's homogenized worldview.

## Takeaways
- Google Maps has become the default navigation reflex for most French citizens, but map representation is never neutral — every map reflects choices and a singular vision
- French cartographic institutions IGN and Michelin are developing alternative map types that emphasize different aspects of territory, landscape, and activity
- The article invokes Borges's famous parable of the map that coincides point-by-point with the Empire, illustrating that exhaustive, objective representation is impossible — all maps are selective
- Cartographic sovereignty is an emerging concern: reliance on a single provider (Google) for how citizens perceive their territory carries political and cultural implications
- The piece is behind a paywall, limiting detailed analysis, but frames the debate around who controls how we see the world through maps

## Synthesis
La Croix's analysis touches on a fundamental tension in modern cartography: the convenience of Google Maps' ubiquitous navigation has come at the cost of cartographic diversity and sovereignty. While Google Maps excels at routing and points of interest, its representation choices — which roads to emphasize, which landmarks to highlight, which neighborhoods to detail — are driven by a single corporate logic. French institutions like IGN (the national geographic agency) and Michelin (the historic map publisher) argue that different map representations can valorize different aspects of territory, from ecological features to cultural heritage to economic activity.

The Borges reference is particularly apt: a map that tries to represent everything ends up being the territory itself, which is useless. Every map must select, and the act of selection is inherently political. When one company dominates the mapping space, its selection criteria become the de facto standard for how millions of people perceive space. This has implications beyond aesthetics: it affects which areas receive economic attention, which communities are visible, and which environmental features are prioritized.

The article appears to be primarily an analysis/opinion piece (paywalled), so detailed policy proposals or technical comparisons are limited. However, the core argument — that France and other nations should maintain independent cartographic capabilities as a matter of sovereignty — resonates with broader debates about digital sovereignty in the EU. The emergence of tools like cartes.gouv.fr as French alternatives to Google Maps suggests this is not merely theoretical but an active policy direction.
