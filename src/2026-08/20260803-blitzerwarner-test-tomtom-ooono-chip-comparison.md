## Elevator pitch
CHIP's comprehensive comparison test of four dedicated Blitzerwarner devices finds that even the most expensive — TomTom's "Tom" — falls short on speed camera detection accuracy, while a simple app may ultimately be the best solution.

## Takeaways
- CHIP tested four devices: Ooono Co-Driver No2, Tom by TomTom, Saphe T, and Needit Drive One 2, evaluating accuracy, hazard detection, and legality
- Tom by TomTom (~80€), despite being the most expensive, "harks" in coverage — its speed camera database is less comprehensive than Ooono's, missing several current mobile speed cameras
- Warning timing varies: the Tom delivers warnings adequately in the city but too late on the autobahn, while competitors like Drive One 2 let users customize warning distance and Ooono uses automatic logic
- The Tom's community reporting system — pressing specific buttons to report or cancel cameras — is unintuitive and raises questions about whether enough users will contribute to improve the database
- TomTom's app integration with Apple CarPlay is a plus, but CHIP's overall conclusion suggests that the Blitzer.de app may outperform all dedicated hardware devices

## Synthesis
CHIP's test reveals a paradox in the speed camera warning market: dedicated hardware devices, designed to be safer and less distracting than smartphone apps, are being outperformed by the very apps they aim to replace. The comparison of Ooono, TomTom, Saphe, and Needit shows that data coverage — the lifeblood of any Blitzerwarner — remains the critical differentiator, and community-driven apps like Blitzer.de continue to dominate.

The Tom by TomTom embodies this tension. Its premium build quality, discrete warnings, and CarPlay integration make it an attractive proposition, but its core function — detecting speed cameras — is undermined by an incomplete database. CHIP notes that multiple current mobile speed cameras were missed during testing, with Blitzer.de having them all on file. This gap traces back to TomTom's community reporting mechanism: users must press specific buttons to report or cancel cameras, a process CHIP found confusing. Without active user contributions, the database cannot grow.

The broader implication for the mapping and navigation ecosystem is that crowd-sourced data networks, where users actively contribute real-time information, remain superior to proprietary data pipelines — at least in niche domains like speed camera reporting. While TomTom's massive traffic data network (600 million connected devices) excels at traffic conditions, it appears less effective for the hyper-local, rapidly-changing nature of speed camera locations. CHIP's implicit conclusion — that a free app may beat all paid hardware — underscores the ongoing challenge for hardware manufacturers in a software-dominated world.
