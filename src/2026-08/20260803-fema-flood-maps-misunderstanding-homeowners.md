# FEMA Flood Maps Cause 'Misunderstanding' Among Some Homeowners, Leaving Millions Without Insurance
**Source**: https://abcnews.com/US/fema-flood-maps-cause-misunderstanding-homeowners-leaving-millions/story?id=134950060
**Date**: 2026-08-03
**Author**: Ginger Zee, Matthew Glasser, Megan Christie, Emily Kohlberg, Daniel Peck, ABC News
**Keywords**: FEMA flood maps, flood insurance, Special Flood Hazard Area, Hurricane Helene, First Street, climate risk modeling, binary risk perception, NFIP

## Elevator pitch
Experts warn that FEMA flood maps are misunderstood as binary risk indicators — causing tens of millions of Americans to skip flood insurance — because the maps don't incorporate future climate conditions, heavy rainfall, or development-driven flood risk.

## Takeaways
- FEMA flood maps are not predictions of where it will flood, but the public treats them as binary: inside the Special Flood Hazard Area means "at risk," outside means "safe"
- Less than 1% of homes in Buncombe County, NC had federal flood insurance when Hurricane Helene hit; on one Swannanoa street, only 1 of 24 flooded homes had coverage
- Expert Susan Crawford estimates "tens of millions of Americans" lack flood insurance when they should have it, due to this map misconception
- FEMA uses proprietary climate-inclusive data for NFIP pricing but doesn't make it public-facing; the public maps omit heavy rainfall, future conditions, and climate change projections
- First Street offers alternative risk assessments incorporating climate change and heavy rainfall, showing different risk levels than FEMA maps in some locations

## Synthesis
Published on August 3, 2026, by ABC News, this investigation by Ginger Zee and colleagues examines how FEMA flood maps create a dangerous public misconception about flood risk, leaving millions of Americans without insurance. The article centers on Liz Barker, a midwife in Swannanoa, North Carolina, whose home was submerged up to the attic during Hurricane Helene in September 2024. Barker, like 23 of her 24 neighbors on the street, did not have flood insurance — deterred by cost and the fact that FEMA maps placed her home outside a Special Flood Hazard Area.

The core problem, explained by former Harvard Law professor Susan Crawford, is that FEMA's maps are "seen by the public now as sort of binary representations of risk." Property owners interpret being inside a flood zone as "I face risk" and being outside as "I don't need insurance." This binary reading is fundamentally incorrect: FEMA maps are regulatory tools requiring insurance for federally backed mortgages in high-risk zones, not comprehensive flood risk predictions.

Ed Kearns, chief science officer at First Street (recently acquired by MSCI), explains that FEMA's publicly available maps don't incorporate heavy rainfall, future climate conditions, development-driven flood risk, or climate change. While FEMA does use proprietary data including these factors for NFIP pricing, that data isn't public-facing. First Street offers alternative risk assessments that include climate change and heavy rainfall modeling, showing different risk levels than FEMA in some locations.

FEMA told ABC News it is managing approximately 1,000 active map revision projects nationwide, with each process taking at least 18 months. However, Crawford notes that even updated maps "still aren't looking at rainfall risk, at future conditions risk, showing where development is going to increase risks of flooding, or at climate change." The map for Barker's area was released in January 2010 with only minor revisions since — 14 years before Helene devastated the region.
