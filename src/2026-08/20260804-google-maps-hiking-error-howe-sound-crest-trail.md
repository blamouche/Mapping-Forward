## Elevator pitch
A group of hikers on British Columbia's Howe Sound Crest Trail relied on Google Maps' 5-hour estimate for what turned out to be a 14-hour alpine trek requiring rescue — illustrating how the app's urban-focused routing algorithm dangerously underestimates mountain terrain.

## Takeaways
- Google Maps estimated 5h13 for the Howe Sound Crest Trail (Cypress to West Lion to Lions Bay); the actual hike took 14 hours and required rescue by Lions Bay Search and Rescue
- The app calculates primarily horizontal distance at urban walking speeds (4-5 km/h), ignoring elevation gain, trail technicality, and terrain conditions
- The trail covers nearly 18 km with over 1,300m of elevation gain, unstable rocks, and sections requiring hand placements — vastly different from Google's simple turn-by-turn instructions
- Search and rescue teams recommend dedicated mountain apps like AllTrails, Granite, Gaia GPS, or Avenza Maps that show elevation profiles, difficulty ratings, and topographic maps
- The "alpine formula" (4 km/h on easy terrain, 300m ascent per hour, 500m descent per hour, plus 20-40% for pauses) can help hikers independently verify app estimates

## Synthesis
Published on August 4, 2026, in La Crème Du Gaming, this article by Valentin Grégoire recounts a dangerous incident on the Howe Sound Crest Trail north of Vancouver, where a group of hikers was rescued after a Google Maps navigation error left them stranded on technical alpine terrain for 14 hours.

The hikers had relied on Google Maps, which estimated the trail at 5 hours 13 minutes (2h55 outbound, 2h18 return). They even printed the app's instructions, which included reassuring directions like "Turn slightly left to stay on Howe Sound Crest Trail." In reality, the trail spans nearly 18 km with over 1,300m of elevation gain, featuring unstable rocks, sections requiring hand placements, and delicate navigation. Local guides describe the route as a 12-14 hour effort, and the trail is known for generating "numerous avoidable accidents and rescue operations each spring and summer," according to Lions Bay Search and Rescue.

The article explains the root cause: Google Maps is designed for roads and urban walking. Its algorithm calculates primarily horizontal distance and applies urban pedestrian speeds (4-5 km/h), completely ignoring elevation gain, trail technicality, terrain conditions, and snow presence. This produces dangerously optimistic time estimates that cascade into insufficient water and food supplies, risk of dehydration, hypoglycemia, night hiking without headlamps, hypothermia, and falls.

The article recommends using dedicated mountain navigation apps instead — AllTrails, Granite, Gaia GPS, Avenza Maps for international users, or IGN Rando, Visorando, Komoot, and Iphigénie for French hikers. These tools provide elevation profiles, cumulative ascent, GPX tracks, and recent user reviews, often with offline mode. The article also shares the "alpine formula" for independent time estimation: 4 km/h on easy terrain, 300m ascent per hour, 500m descent per hour, with 20-40% added for pauses. The rescuers' golden rule: "Google Maps for the car to the parking lot, a hiking app for everything after."
