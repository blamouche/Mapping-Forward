## Synthesis
Published on August 6, 2026, in Phys.org via The Conversation, this article by James Cheshire, a professor of cartography, examines how Google's addition of generative AI to its Google Earth satellite image platform challenges the centuries-old trust placed in mapmaking. The article weaves together historical cartographic errors, copyright precedent, and modern AI ethics to argue that the trust we place in maps is fragile and increasingly under threat.

The article opens with the historical case of the Mountains of Kong, a mythical mountain range that appeared on maps of Africa for over a century. Scottish surgeon Mungo Park described them to cartographer James Rennell in the 1790s as a possible source for the Niger River. Rennell produced maps featuring the range, extending it well beyond Park's observations into areas he could never have seen. These maps were widely copied, and doubts only grew toward the end of the 19th century when French officer Louis-Gustave Binger proved the mountains didn't exist. Remarkably, a reference to "Kong Mountains" still appeared in the 1936 Oxford Advanced Atlas—nearly 50 years after their debunking.

Cheshire connects this historical case to a 1785 copyright ruling where London map publisher Robert Sayer sued rival John Hamilton Moore for copying his nautical charts. The court ruled in Moore's favor, finding that earlier charts could be improved upon provided the new work was not a "servile imitation." This ruling has been cited as a defense of AI, which consumes copyrighted material but reconstitutes it in ways that don't appear as direct imitations.

The article's central concern is that trust in mapmaking technology—from the ancient Greek dioptra to modern satellites—has been fundamental to cartography. Google's addition of generative AI to Google Earth, enabling the creation of convincing but fictional satellite images, threatens that trust. Rather than using Google's suggestions to render historic buildings or dream homes, some users quickly created and shared disturbing images, including bombed suburbs. The article argues that maps have a lasting impact on how we understand the world, and that AI-generated map content demands careful consideration of the trust and verification mechanisms that have underpinned cartography for centuries.
