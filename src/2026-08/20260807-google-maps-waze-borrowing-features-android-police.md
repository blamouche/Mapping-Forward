# Google Maps Borrowed Waze's Best Features, So Why Do I Still Need Both Apps?
**Source**: https://www.androidpolice.com/google-maps-waze-keep-borrowing-from-each-other-neither-gets-it-right/
**Date**: 2026-08-07
**Author**: Anu Joy
**Keywords**: Google Maps, Waze, navigation apps, feature convergence, road hazard reporting, community-driven mapping, Android Auto

## Elevator pitch
More than a decade after Google acquired Waze, the two navigation apps have increasingly borrowed each other's features yet remain fundamentally different tools—Maps excels at discovery and trip planning while Waze dominates real-time driving conditions, leaving users to switch between both.

## Takeaways
- Google acquired Waze in 2013 for approximately $1.1 billion, but the two apps remain separate despite over a decade of feature convergence
- Google Maps has adopted Waze's road hazard reporting (crashes, lane closures, construction, objects on road) but never fully embraced Waze's community-first philosophy
- Waze has improved significantly with cleaner interface, better Android Auto support, and improved voice navigation, borrowing from Google's ecosystem
- Google Maps tries to be everything: navigation app, local discovery platform, transit guide, walking companion, cycling assistant, and travel planner
- The author still uses both apps: Maps for discovering places and planning trips, Waze for handling the unpredictability of the drive itself

## Synthesis
Published on August 7, 2026, in Android Police by Anu Joy, this article examines why, more than a decade after Google's $1.1 billion acquisition of Waze in 2013, the two navigation apps remain separate despite having become increasingly similar. The article argues that while both apps have borrowed heavily from each other, neither has become the obvious choice for every driving scenario.

Google Maps has adopted many of Waze's signature features over the years. Users can now report crashes, lane closures, objects on the road, construction, and other hazards directly from the navigation screen, and see reports from other users with the ability to confirm their accuracy. However, the article notes that Google Maps "never fully embraced Waze's community-first philosophy"—the borrowing has been selective, adopting features without the underlying community ethos that makes Waze distinctive.

The borrowing hasn't been one-sided. Waze has improved significantly, with a cleaner interface, better navigation, and improved integration into Google's ecosystem. Support for Android Auto has matured, voice navigation has improved, and route planning feels more reliable. But Waze remains fundamentally a driving-focused app; outside the car, it offers very little for planning a day out, comparing cafés, or exploring neighborhoods.

The article concludes that each app solves different problems. Google Maps is the richer overall experience with superior search, business information, offline maps, and multi-modal transportation support, making it one of the most versatile smartphone apps. Waze excels at responding to changing road conditions with its excellent incident reporting and driving-focused interface. The author still switches between both: Maps for discovering places and planning trips, Waze for handling the unpredictability of driving. Until one app combines the best of both worlds, users will continue to need both.
