# J'ai essayé de remplacer Google Maps pendant 4 ans : mon bilan sans concession
**Source**: https://www.lesnumeriques.com/societe-numerique/j-ai-essaye-de-remplacer-google-maps-pendant-4-ans-mon-bilan-sans-concession-a259052.html
**Date**: 2026-08-09
**Author**: Corentin Bechade
**Keywords**: Google Maps, alternatives souveraines, cartographie, données personnelles, monopole, OpenStreetMap, Apple Plans

## Elevator pitch
After four years of attempting to replace Google Maps with sovereign and open-source alternatives, a journalist from Les Numériques concludes that the service's dominance rests not just on technology but on a locked economic and political model that makes switching nearly impossible for ordinary users.

## Takeaways
- Google Maps has become so dominant that it functions as a quasi-social network where user reviews, photos, and contributions continuously strengthen its database and competitive moat
- The crowdsourced data flywheel means that since everyone uses Google Maps, nobody contributes to alternatives, preventing competitors from building viable databases
- Even Apple, with its vast resources, has failed to make Apple Plans a serious competitor to Google Maps
- A survey by Cartes.gouv found that 73% of respondents would prefer a map produced by a French public institution over one from a large tech company
- The barrier to alternatives is more political and economic than technical: the lock-in comes from user data contributions, not from superior mapping algorithms

## Synthesis
Published on August 9, 2026, in Les Numériques, this article by Corentin Bechade offers a candid personal assessment of a four-year attempt to break free from Google Maps. The author acknowledges that despite growing desire to reduce dependence on big tech and protect personal data, Google Maps remains his app of choice faute de mieux—because of a structural problem that is "less technical than political."

The article identifies a vicious cycle at the heart of Google Maps' dominance. Unlike Gmail or Google Drive, which are tools with feature lists, Google Maps has become a quasi-social network where every review, photo, and comment left by users strengthens the service's relevance and cements its market position. This crowdsourced information, made possible by Google's massive investments, creates a self-reinforcing flywheel: since everyone is on Google Maps, there's no incentive to enter the same information on competing apps. As a result, everyone continues using Google Maps and enriching its database, preventing viable alternatives from emerging.

The author notes that even Apple, with its hundreds of billions in resources, has not managed to position Apple Plans as a serious competitor. Yet the public seems to want alternatives: a survey by Cartes.gouv found that 73% of respondents prefer a map produced by a French public institution over one from a large international tech company. The article argues that Google Maps' hegemony rests on a locked economic and political model, not merely superior technology, making the emergence of sovereign and free alternatives extremely difficult for daily use. The problem, the author concludes, requires collective action: quitting Google Maps demands a collective effort, not just an individual one.
