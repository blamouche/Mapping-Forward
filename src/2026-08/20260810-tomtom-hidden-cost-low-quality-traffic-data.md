# The Hidden Cost of Low-Quality Traffic Data
**Source**: https://www.webwire.com/ViewPressRel.asp?aId=358678
**Date**: 2026-08-10
**Author**: TomTom / WebWire
**Keywords**: TomTom, traffic data, Floating Car Data, procurement, probe data, data quality, penetration, accuracy, latency

## Elevator pitch
TomTom's Public Sector Segment Lead explains why choosing traffic data on price alone is a false economy, arguing that penetration, accuracy, and latency—not just availability—define true data quality and determine the quality of downstream decisions.

## Takeaways
- Public sector procurement often treats traffic data as a commodity, with price becoming the decisive factor since quality metrics like penetration, accuracy, and latency are hard to evaluate in tenders
- Traffic data has evolved from manual counting at junctions to induction loops and roadside sensors, and now to Floating Car Data from connected vehicles
- Vehicle probe data provides network-wide visibility rather than point-specific observations, enabling a step change in traffic data quality
- Penetration (how many vehicles contribute), accuracy (how precise the measurements are), and latency (how fresh the data is) are the three dimensions that define true traffic data quality
- Low-quality data leads to poor decisions in traffic management, infrastructure planning, and insurance, with costs that far exceed the savings from cheaper procurement

## Synthesis
The article, published by TomTom via WebWire on August 10, 2026, makes a compelling case for why public agencies and insurance companies should evaluate traffic data on quality rather than price alone. Saurav Miglani, TomTom's Public Sector Segment Lead, argues that "data availability is one thing, but penetration, accuracy, and latency are what defines the true quality"—a distinction that rarely appears in procurement specifications but surfaces everywhere afterward.

The piece traces the evolution of traffic data collection from manual hand-counting at junctions through induction loops and pneumatic tubes to modern Floating Car Data from connected vehicles. The key shift is from point-specific observation to network-wide visibility: instead of understanding traffic at a single location, agencies can now monitor movement, speed, and congestion across entire road networks in real time. This matters because decisions historically made with limited overviews and extrapolated models can now be based on comprehensive, empirical data.

The article's core argument is that treating traffic data as an undifferentiated commodity leads to a hidden cost: decisions made with incomplete or inaccurate data. For traffic management, this means suboptimal signal timing and routing. For infrastructure planning, it means models built on weak assumptions. For insurance, it means risk assessments that don't reflect reality. The procurement challenge is that the quality metrics that matter—penetration (the proportion of vehicles contributing data), accuracy (the precision of speed and position measurements), and latency (how quickly data reflects current conditions)—are notoriously difficult to specify and compare in tender documents. The result is that price becomes the most visible comparison point, and the true cost of low-quality data only materializes in the decisions that follow.
