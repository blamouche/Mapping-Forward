# Global Building Atlas Maps 2.75 Billion Structures in 3D

## Elevator pitch
A research team led by Xiaoxiang Zhu at the Technical University of Munich has built the first high-resolution 3D Global Building Atlas mapping 2.75 billion structures worldwide, enabling applications in urban planning, disaster management, and climate modeling.

## Takeaways
- First global 3D building atlas maps 2.75 billion structures with 97% having full 3D attributes (2.68 billion)
- Provides three core metrics per structure: footprint area, structural geometry, and vertical height
- Open-access dataset funded by a European Research Council grant, publicly available
- Enables disaster response, heat vulnerability modeling, and carbon tracking at building level
- Reveals socioeconomic disparities by comparing structural volume against population figures globally
- Advanced computer vision algorithms extracted 3D attributes from thousands of orbital satellite images

## Synthesis
The Global Building Atlas, developed by Xiaoxiang Zhu's team at the Technical University of Munich, represents a breakthrough in worldwide built-environment mapping. By analyzing thousands of orbital satellite images with advanced computer vision algorithms, the team identified and measured 2.75 billion structures globally, with 2.68 billion having complete three-dimensional attributes including footprint area, structural geometry, and vertical height from ground to roof line.

The resulting open-access dataset, funded by a European Research Council grant, establishes the first standardized global foundation for built-up volume per capita. This uniform census of the built environment provides policymakers and researchers with an objective tool to measure living conditions across borders, compare structural data against population figures to reveal overcrowding, highlight informal settlements, and track urban expansion in rapidly growing cities.

The applications span multiple domains. Urban planners can track population density, evaluate housing access, and identify infrastructure deficits by mapping structural volume alongside demographic data. Disaster response teams can overlay flood, landslide, and storm surge projections onto building heights to identify high-risk zones. Climate scientists can model urban heat islands at the street-block level and estimate local energy consumption and carbon emissions by calculating total building volume across metropolitan regions. The dataset is particularly valuable for developing regions where basic architectural data has been sparse or outdated, enabling international aid organizations to distribute resources more effectively.
