# Google Maps Is Great for Road Trips. On the Trail, It's Getting Hikers Rescued.
**Source**: https://www.backpacker.com/news-and-events/news/google-maps-is-great-for-road-trips-on-the-trail-its-getting-hikers-rescued/
**Date**: 2026-08-14
**Author**: Adam Roy
**Keywords**: Google Maps, hiking, search and rescue, navigation, backcountry, trail safety

## Elevator pitch
Search and rescue teams are warning hikers not to rely on Google Maps for backcountry trip planning after multiple incidents where the app's inaccurate time estimates and unsuitable trail data led hikers into dangerous situations.

## Takeaways
- Teenage hikers on British Columbia's Howe Sound Trail needed rescue after Google Maps estimated 5 hours for a 12-14 hour alpine trek with 4,200 feet of elevation gain
- A 15-year-old hiker in Colorado slid 200 feet down a snow-covered mountain after planning a multi-day trip using Google Maps
- Google Maps' public lands data often comes from outdated government datasets that may include non-navigable trails
- The app's time estimates appear based on distance and elevation gain but don't account for talus fields, scrambling, switchbacks, or technical terrain
- SAR teams recommend using specialized hiking apps with topo maps (like Gaia GPS) and carrying paper maps and compass

## Synthesis
Google Maps has been expanding onto hiking trails worldwide, even adding Street View for some routes. However, search and rescue teams are increasingly sounding the alarm about the app's inadequacy for backcountry navigation. Multiple incidents this summer have highlighted the dangers: in July, three teenage hikers on British Columbia's Howe Sound Trail needed rescue after Google estimated 5 hours 13 minutes for what is actually a 12-14 hour alpine trek involving 4,200 feet of elevation gain and technical scrambling. The teens ran out of supplies, were caught by sunset, and one injured his tailbone falling off a boulder before being airlifted out. In Colorado, a 15-year-old hiker slid 200 feet down a snow-covered mountain after planning his trip with Google Maps.

The problems stem from several factors. Google Maps' public lands data often derives from government datasets that may be outdated, including trails that are no longer navigable. The base maps lack the topographic detail needed to assess slope angles and terrain difficulty. Most critically, the time estimates appear to be computed from distance and elevation gain alone, failing to account for talus fields, scrambling sections, switchbacks, and difficult tread—factors that dramatically affect hiking speed. Search and rescue teams consistently advise hikers to use specialized navigation apps with detailed topo maps, carry paper maps and compass as backup, seek local knowledge from experienced hikers or gear shops, and trust their instincts when progress doesn't match the app's estimates.
