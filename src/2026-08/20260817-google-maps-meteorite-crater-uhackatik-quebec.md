# Amateur Astronomer Discovers 390-Million-Year-Old Meteorite Crater on Google Maps
**Source**: https://www.cbc.ca/news/canada/montreal/meteor-crater-quebec-discovery-390-million-years-old-9.7268583
**Date**: 2026-08-15
**Author**: CBC News / Times of India
**Keywords**: Google Maps, meteorite crater, Quebec, Uhackatik, citizen science, satellite imagery, discovery

## Elevator pitch
Amateur astronomer Joël Lapointe discovered a previously unknown 390-million-year-old meteorite impact crater in Quebec's Côte-Nord region while planning a camping trip on Google Maps, leading to a major scientific find named Uhackatik.

## Takeaways
- Joël Lapointe spotted a suspicious circular indentation around Lake Marsal in Quebec's Côte-Nord region while planning a 2024 camping trip on Google Maps
- Scientists confirmed the feature as a previously unidentified ancient meteorite impact crater, approximately 390 million years old
- The crater was named Uhackatik in consultation with the Innu Council of Ekuanitshit, on whose traditional lands the crater lies
- Researchers presented findings at the annual congress of the Meteoritical Society, with impact melt rock samples providing strong evidence
- The discovery highlights how satellite imagery on platforms like Google Maps enables citizen science and accidental discoveries

## Synthesis
In 2024, Canadian amateur astronomer Joël Lapointe was using Google Maps to plan a camping trip through Quebec's remote Côte-Nord region when he noticed an enormous circular indentation surrounding Lake Marsal. To Lapointe's trained eye, the feature looked suspiciously like a meteorite impact crater. He contacted geologists who investigated the site, collecting impact melt rock samples that provided strong evidence confirming the crater's impact origin. The findings were presented at the annual congress of the Meteoritical Society.

Named Uhackatik in consultation with the Innu Council of Ekuanitshit on whose traditional lands it lies, the crater dates to approximately 390 million years ago. The discovery demonstrates the continuing value of satellite imagery platforms like Google Maps for citizen science. While professional scientists use specialized tools, freely accessible platforms enable anyone with curiosity and observational skills to potentially identify significant geological features. The Uhackatik crater joins a growing list of remarkable features discovered through Google Earth and similar platforms, from archaeological sites to geological formations, underscoring how democratized access to satellite imagery continues to yield scientific discoveries.
