## Synthesis
The Inter-American Development Bank (IDB) is proposing a fundamental rethink of how road infrastructure is designed and protected in Latin America and the Caribbean. The region faces a threefold challenge: poor logistics performance, growing exposure to natural hazards, and accelerating loss of natural capital. Between 1972 and 2010, disasters caused an average of US$400 million in infrastructure damage per event, with transport assets the hardest-hit subsector. Meanwhile, nearly 3 million hectares of forest are lost annually, removing natural protective barriers and increasing flooding, landslides, and erosion.

The IDB's regenerative roads model goes beyond conventional approaches by expanding the scale of intervention. Rather than treating roads as isolated engineering projects, the model understands infrastructure as part of an integrated system with its natural surroundings. Conserving forests and restoring soils at the watershed level — including in the upper and middle reaches of watersheds, far from the road itself — can significantly reduce surface runoff and lower risks to road infrastructure. Evidence cited by the IDB shows that maintaining forested buffer zones near roads can be 16 times more cost-effective than repairing damage after it occurs.

The pilot project along Guatemala's CA-9 North Corridor demonstrates the approach. This road section connects Guatemala City with the country's Atlantic and Pacific ports and carries 71.5% of national exports, yet faces severe traffic congestion (40 vs. 80 km/h expected), and high exposure to landslides, flooding, and erosion. 49% of Guatemala's roads face severe landslide risk, exacerbated by deforestation within watersheds. The regenerative roads model aligns forest conservation with national incentive programs, payments for ecosystem services, and private financing schemes, generating rural employment and improved livelihoods alongside climate resilience.
