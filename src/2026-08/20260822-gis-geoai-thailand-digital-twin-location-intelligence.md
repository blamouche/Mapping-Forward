**Date**: August 22, 2026
**Author**: Siwadee Aksornnam, PostToday

## Elevator pitch
A comprehensive overview of how GIS in Thailand is evolving from static mapping into Location Intelligence, GeoAI, and Digital Twin systems, driven by GISTDA and the National Geo-Informatics Committee's roadmap from Foundation to Open Data to AI to simulation.

## Takeaways
- GIS has evolved far beyond map-making: it links data to geographic locations to reveal patterns, relationships, and spatial context that tabular data alone cannot show
- Thailand's GISTDA is piloting GeoAI in Sukhothai province for water management and disaster response, marking a shift from data display to AI-supported decision-making
- The National Geo-Informatics Committee (กภช.) has set a four-stage national roadmap: Foundation → Open Geo-Informatics Data → GeoAI → Digital Twin
- GIS applications span government (disaster management, urban planning), business (site selection, logistics), infrastructure (asset management), and statistics (NSO's digital statistical maps)
- Data quality remains the critical foundation — without accurate, current, standardized, and interoperable data, GeoAI and Digital Twin systems cannot deliver reliable insights

## Synthesis
This article from Thai outlet PostToday traces the evolution of Geographic Information Systems from their traditional role as digital map-making tools into a broader platform for spatial analysis, Location Intelligence, and ultimately GeoAI and Digital Twin technologies. The framing is explicitly contextual to Thailand's national strategy, drawing on examples from GISTDA (Geo-Informatics and Space Technology Development Agency) and Esri's conceptual definitions.

The article argues that GIS is fundamentally about giving data a spatial context. Population statistics in a table tell you how many people live in a province; the same data on a map reveals density patterns, service gaps, and infrastructure needs. This shift from "what" to "where" and "why" is the essence of Location Intelligence — using spatial data to generate insights that support decision-making. Esri's framework is cited throughout, emphasizing that GIS connects the "where" question with the "how" and "what next."

Thailand's concrete steps are notable. In July 2026, GISTDA partnered with Sukhothai province to pilot GeoAI for water management and disaster response — a practical application that moves beyond visualization into prediction and planning. In August 2026, the National Geo-Informatics Committee approved a national operational plan (2027–2031 in Buddhist calendar years) structured around four stages: Foundation (infrastructure, standards, reliable data), Open Geo-Informatics Data (enabling cross-agency data use), GeoAI (AI-driven spatial analysis and prediction), and Digital Twin (simulation of real-world systems). This roadmap signals that Thailand treats geo-informatics as national data infrastructure, not merely a specialized tool.

The article also covers the breadth of GIS applications: disaster management (flood mapping, response routing), business (retail site selection, logistics optimization), infrastructure asset management (tracking roads, pipes, utilities by location), smart cities (integrating IoT, traffic, and 3D data), statistics (NSO's GIS-based statistical maps), and satellite remote sensing (land-use change, agriculture, environmental monitoring). The unifying thread is that location provides the connective tissue between disparate datasets.

The article closes with a critical caveat: technology is only as good as the data behind it. If data is inaccurate, outdated, non-standardized, or siloed, then GeoAI and Digital Twin analyses will not reflect reality. This is why Thailand's roadmap starts with "Foundation" before advancing to AI and simulation — smart systems require solid data infrastructure as their base.
