# How Maps Harden Territorial Claims
**Source**: https://www.lowyinstitute.org/the-interpreter/how-maps-harden-territorial-claims
**Date**: 2026-08-19
**Author**: Henry Yep, Lowy Institute
**Keywords**: cartography, geopolitics, South China Sea, territorial claims, map regulation, sovereignty disputes, dashed-line map

## Elevator pitch
The Lowy Institute analyzes how maps serve as tools of geopolitical power, examining how China's dashed-line map and state map regulation harden territorial claims by compressing complex legal questions into single authoritative images.

## Synthesis
Henry Yep's analysis for the Lowy Institute examines the political power of cartography through the lens of the South China Sea disputes. Following a July 2026 clash between Philippine and Chinese personnel near Second Thomas Shoal, Yep notes that while a map can specify where an encounter took place, it can also cast one side as the lawful occupant and the other as the intruder. Maps that appear neutral or objective often reproduce one government's position without qualification.

The historical context is instructive. Early modern maps increasingly represented political authority as bounded territory separated by lines, creating an image cleaner than reality: claims overlapped, frontiers remained porous, and physical control often faded before the edge of territory shown on paper. Colonial governments drew boundaries around places where their presence was thin, making incomplete claims appear complete. The same pattern persists today, where maps compress complex and unsettled questions about land ownership and water rights into a single image.

China's dashed-line map illustrates the effect powerfully. In May 2009, Beijing attached the map to diplomatic notes responding to continental-shelf submissions by Malaysia and Vietnam. The text asserted sovereignty over islands and adjacent waters but did not explain the legal effect of the dashes. The image enclosed most of the South China Sea within a broken line, with no indication that other governments claimed the same space. China also regulates maps through official review processes, making unauthorized depictions illegal, and promotes national-territory awareness through annual publicity campaigns. When a government consistently presents contested space as national territory, compromise becomes harder to explain domestically, making maps not just representations of reality but active instruments in shaping it.
