## Synthesis
The Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History in Washington, DC, has created an exhibit that uses cartography as its central organizing principle. "From These Lands: Sharing Our Natural and Cultural Heritage," on view through December 2029, draws more than 600 specimens and cultural objects from all 50 states, the District of Columbia, and five inhabited US territories to mark the nation's 250th birthday. The exhibit team ruled out a state-by-state alphabetical grouping early, choosing geography instead to show how places shape and knit together people, place, and history.

The exhibit's centerpiece is an interactive map built around the instinct of starting with a place a visitor already knows, then showing how it connects to everything else. Five animated map layers trace how biomes, migrations, and geology move across the country, each running for about 60 seconds. A paleogeographic map rolled back far enough shows how a shallow sea once split the middle of what is now the US, revealing linkages between states that today share no coastline but once did. Two sea star fossils found a thousand miles apart in Minnesota and West Virginia, neither state holding an ocean today, compress this large idea into two small objects.

The exhibit demonstrates how GIS and interactive mapping can transform a traditional museum experience. State-symbol displays let people find natural history tied to home. Interactive stations allow visitors to send digital postcards. The Chesapeake Bay case ties Virginia oysters, Maryland blue crab, and Delmarva shad to the people who have worked those waters for thousands of years. The exhibit's use of maps grounds visitors in familiar places while widening their perspective from local to national to global, showing that geography is not just a reference tool but a storytelling medium.
