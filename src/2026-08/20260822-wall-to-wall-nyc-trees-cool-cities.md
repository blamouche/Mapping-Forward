# 'Wall-to-Wall' Map of NYC Trees Could Help Cool Cities Worldwide

## Synthesis
Cornell University researchers have produced the first "wall-to-wall" citywide tree map of New York City, identifying 1.8 million individual trees — including 1.4 million that had never been surveyed. Published in Scientific Data, the study combines satellite imagery, on-the-ground datasets, and 3D data collected with lidar (light detection and ranging) to identify tree types with 82% overall accuracy, with higher accuracy for common NYC tree species.

The map's significance lies in its coverage of previously unmapped areas. Trees on private property, in natural areas, and other unsurveyed zones together constitute an estimated 65% of the city's canopy and were not accounted for on previous maps. The research team, part of the Cool Trees project led by Dr. Arnab Ghosh, will use the map to determine which trees are best for cooling — increasingly critical as heat waves in New York City become more frequent and intense.

The data could inform a host of planting decisions where tree type matters: reducing allergens, managing invasive pests, and optimizing cooling effects. NYC has set a goal of expanding tree cover from 22% to 30% by 2040, and the new map provides the granular data needed to target plantings effectively. An unrelated Cornell project, "The Generative Canopy," aims to leverage street-tree data to determine where more trees are needed to provide shade to vulnerable residents. The approach is replicable in cities worldwide, offering a methodology for data-driven urban forestry.
