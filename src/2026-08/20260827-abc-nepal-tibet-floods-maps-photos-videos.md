# Nepal–Tibet Floods in Maps, Photos and Videos

## Elevator pitch
A devastating flash flood triggered by a landslide on the Nepal–Tibet border has killed at least 168 people with over 1,300 missing, and ABC News uses maps, verified satellite imagery, and video footage to document the unfolding humanitarian disaster along the Trishuli River.

## Takeaways
- A landslide approximately 12 miles northeast of the Rasuwagadhi border post triggered massive flash flooding in Nepal's Rasuwa district on August 27, 2026
- At least 168 people confirmed dead and over 1,300 missing, including 65 American tourists and 39 Australians
- The US Geological Survey registered a 5.2-magnitude landslide event, initially misreported as a 4.4 earthquake
- Verified maps and satellite imagery show the path of destruction from the Gyirong border through Nepal's Nuwakot district
- The disaster highlights the critical role of crisis mapping and satellite verification in covering remote, rapidly evolving natural disasters

## Synthesis
The Nepal–Tibet border region experienced catastrophic flash flooding on August 27, 2026, when a landslide in the Himalayas took away part of a glacier in the mountain pass near Rasuwagadhi. The resulting wall of water swept through the Trishuli River valley, burying buildings, damaging bridges and roadways, and inundating communities in Nepal's Nuwakot district. ABC News published a comprehensive visual investigation combining maps, verified satellite imagery, and video footage to trace the disaster's path and document the ongoing rescue mission.

The cartographic dimension of this story is significant. News organisations including ABC News and The New York Times deployed interactive maps showing the flood's trajectory from the Gyirong border crossing through downstream communities, overlaying satellite data with ground-level verification. This approach exemplifies how modern crisis cartography — combining satellite imagery, geolocated video, and GIS data — has become essential for reporting on remote disasters where access is limited. The flood occurred at approximately 8:40 AM local time in a mountainous area popular with international tourists, making geospatial tools critical for coordinating search and rescue across a vast, difficult terrain.

The disaster also underscores the increasing importance of real-time mapping in emergency response. Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority relied on geospatial data to track the missing — at least 826 people including 579 tourists — across an area spanning the Nepal–China frontier. The US State Department deployed a disaster response advisor to the region, and the US government committed $500,000 in emergency assistance. As climate change intensifies glacial lake outburst floods and landslide risks in the Himalayas, the mapping technologies used to document and respond to such events are becoming indispensable infrastructure for disaster management.
