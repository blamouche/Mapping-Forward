# Lake Ontario to Lake America: Who Gets to Decide What Goes on Maps?

**Date**: 2026-08-28
**Author**: The Conversation
**Keywords**: Lake Ontario, Lake America, place names, geographic names, Board on Geographic Names, Trump executive order, toponymy, cartography

## Elevator pitch
The Conversation examines the formal process behind geographic name changes in the United States, using Trump's renaming of Lake Ontario to "Lake America" as a case study in who gets to decide what appears on maps.

## Takeaways
- President Trump signed an executive order on August 27, 2026, renaming Lake Ontario as "Lake America" amid an escalating trade war with Canada
- In the U.S., place names are standardised by the U.S. Board on Geographic Names, part of the USGS
- Renaming typically starts locally: state or county proposals work up through state authorities to the federal Board
- The Denali/Mount McKinley name change took 40 years due to congressional opposition from Ohio
- Trump's previous executive order asked the Board to "honor the contributions of visionary and patriotic Americans," changing its policies and procedures

## Synthesis
The Conversation provides a detailed examination of the formal processes behind geographic renaming in the United States, using President Trump's August 27, 2026 executive order renaming Lake Ontario as "Lake America" as its focal point. The article explains that while presidential executive orders can direct naming changes, there is an established bureaucratic process that usually starts at the local level.

The U.S. Board on Geographic Names, part of the U.S. Geological Survey, is the agency responsible for standardising place names. Typically, a renaming begins when people in a state or county propose a change and gather local support. The request moves through county commissioners, state natural resources departments, and ultimately to the Board on Geographic Names, which makes the name official for the entire country. The article cites the 2018 renaming of Lake Calhoun to Bde Maka Ska in Minneapolis as an example of this process working as intended.

However, the Lake America case bypasses this bottom-up process entirely. Trump's earlier executive order had already directed the Board on Geographic Names to change its policies and procedures to "honor the contributions of visionary and patriotic Americans." The Denali/Mount McKinley case illustrates how politically fraught naming can be: Alaska requested the change in 1975, but Ohio's congressional delegation blocked it for 40 years because President McKinley was from Ohio. Trump reverted Denali to Mount McKinley in 2025.

The article highlights the tension between the technical, consensus-based tradition of geographic naming and the political use of toponymy as a tool of nationalism. While the Board's policies state that input from state geographic names authorities, local governments, and Tribal Governments is "actively pursued," executive orders can override this consultative process, raising questions about who truly gets to decide what goes on maps.
