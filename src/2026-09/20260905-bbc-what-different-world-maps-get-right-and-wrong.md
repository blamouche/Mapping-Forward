# What Different World Maps Get Right — and What They Get Wrong

**Source**: https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/cly5r60v4mro
**Date**: 2026-09-05
**Author**: BBC
**Keywords**: Mercator, Equal Earth, Peters projection, map projections, cartography, Correct the Map, UN

## Elevator pitch
The BBC provides a comparative analysis of the world's most prominent map projections — Mercator, Equal Earth, and Peters — explaining the trade-offs between area accuracy, shape fidelity, and navigational utility.

## Takeaways
- No flat map can perfectly represent a spherical Earth; every projection sacrifices something
- Mercator preserves compass bearings but massively distorts area, making Africa look Greenland-sized
- Equal Earth, adopted by the UN, preserves area accuracy but cannot be used for navigation
- The Peters projection (1973) also preserves area but severely distorts shapes of landmasses
- Critics argue Mercator's distortions have psychological effects, shaping how regions are perceived politically and economically

## Synthesis
Following the UN's adoption of the "Correct the Map" resolution, the BBC produced a detailed comparison of the three most prominent world map projections, each representing a different compromise in the impossible task of rendering a sphere on a flat surface.

The Mercator projection, created in 1569, remains the most widely used world map and underpins Google Maps. University College London cartography professor James Cheshire explains its enduring appeal: Mercator designed it so that "a straight line to be drawn on a map to look like a straight line in reality if you followed a compass bearing." This made it invaluable for sailors, but the cost is severe area distortion — countries near the equator appear much smaller, while those near the poles appear inflated.

The Equal Earth map, developed in 2018 and now endorsed by the UN, addresses this by preserving area accuracy. Africa appears at its true proportional size relative to Greenland and Europe. However, it sacrifices the straight-line compass bearings that made Mercator useful for navigation. As Cheshire notes, "You can preserve area, or you can preserve shape, or you can preserve distance and direction, but not everything at once perfectly, because otherwise you'd be back to a sphere."

The Peters projection, designed by German historian Arno Peters in the 1970s based on James Gall's 1855 work, also preserves area but at the cost of severe shape distortion — landmasses look stretched and elongated. Kenyan geographer Kioko Muendo tells the BBC that maps "shape how regions are perceived politically and economically," arguing that Mercator has implicitly downplayed Africa's resources, population, and investment opportunities.
