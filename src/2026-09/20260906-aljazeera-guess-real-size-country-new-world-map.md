## Synthesis
Following the UN General Assembly's vote to adopt the "Correct the Map" resolution, Al Jazeera published an interactive explainer that demonstrates the distortions embedded in the Mercator projection. The quiz format asks users to pick which of two countries is larger, then morphs the map from Mercator to Equal Earth to reveal the correct answer — often contradicting what users see on standard classroom maps.

The distortion is not subtle: on a Mercator map, Greenland appears roughly the size of Africa, which is actually 14 times larger. The exaggeration increases with distance from the equator, systematically inflating Europe, Russia, and North America while shrinking Africa, Latin America, and South Asia. The Mercator projection was designed by Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator in 1569 to preserve angles and straight bearings for maritime navigation — a trade-off that made sense for 16th-century ships but has shaped global perceptions for nearly 500 years since.

The Equal Earth projection, developed in 2018, makes the opposite trade-off: it preserves true area so continents can be compared honestly, at the cost of slightly bending their outlines. The UN resolution encourages schools, institutions, and tech companies to adopt it, though it does not ban Mercator or impose a replacement.

The US representative, Yaryna Ferencevych, dismissed the resolution before the vote, calling it "the reason this institution is losing its credibility" and arguing the body was "debating map projects from the 16th century" instead of genuine problems. The African Union had already adopted Equal Earth in March 2025. Togo's Foreign Minister Robert Dussey announced plans for a meeting in Lomé early next year with UNESCO, the African Union, and technology companies including Google to work out practical implementation.
