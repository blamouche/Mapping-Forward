## Synthesis
The TomTom Traffic Index 2025 has delivered a stark diagnosis for Colombia's urban centers: Bogotá, Medellín, Cali, Barranquilla, and Cartagena all registered congestion levels above 57%, with four of these cities placing among the 20 most congested in the world. The figures renew pressure on Colombian authorities to address what has become a systemic national challenge in mobility and infrastructure planning.

Bogotá leads with 69.6% congestion, meaning that travel times in the capital were on average 69.6% longer than they would be under free-flowing traffic conditions. Medellín follows at 66.9%, Cali at 65.6%, and Barranquilla at 62.8%. Cartagena, while somewhat lower at 57.7%, still faces significant mobility challenges. These levels place Colombian cities in the same conversation as global congestion leaders like Lima, Mumbai, and London.

Patricio Zapata, Customer Success Manager Latam at Graphisoft, argues that the figures reveal a challenge that transcends any single city. "These figures show that Colombia has a road planning challenge that goes beyond one city. Urban growth and new mobility dynamics require moving away from thinking about projects solely in terms of building more infrastructure, and starting to plan them with better information, coordination, and the ability to anticipate how they will work before construction begins," he stated.

Graphisoft proposes five key changes for transforming how Colombia plans, designs, and executes road infrastructure projects. First, integrating information from the start through a common digital model that coordinates design, engineering, and construction from early stages. Second, simulating before building by combining traffic data, urban growth models, and digital models to compare scenarios before committing investment. Third, detecting errors before they become cost overruns through interdisciplinary coordination. Fourth, planning for future urban growth rather than just current conditions, integrating public transport, private vehicles, pedestrians, cyclists, and public space into a unified city vision. Fifth, strengthening digital talent in the sector — engineers, architects, and managers capable of working with BIM, managing information, and coordinating multiple disciplines.

The use of open standards like IFC (Industry Foundation Classes) is recommended to facilitate information consultation by all actors in the design process, not only during development but throughout the asset's operational life, avoiding the obsolescence typical of native formats. As Zapata concludes: "The infrastructure we build today will define how we move for the next decades, so modernizing road planning cannot be limited to responding to current problems, but must anticipate how cities will grow and leverage technology and information to make better decisions before building."
