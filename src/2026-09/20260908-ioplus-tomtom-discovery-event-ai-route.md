## Elevator pitch
At its TomTom Discovery Event in Amsterdam, the company revealed a fundamental strategic repositioning: maps are no longer primarily for guiding people, but for navigating AI and machines, with Orbis serving as a spatial knowledge layer for autonomous driving and enterprise AI agents.

## Synthesis
At a dedicated press morning at its Amsterdam headquarters — the TomTom Discovery Event — Managing Director Mike Schoofs provided an unprecedented inside look into the company's strategic repositioning. The shift is fundamental: "For forty years, digital maps helped humans understand the physical world; the next opportunity is to help intelligent systems and AI understand that physical world and move within it," Schoofs summarized.

The company is betting heavily on two markets. First, automated driving technology, where TomTom aims to provide the critical safety map layer for more than half of all new European cars by 2030. The argument is that while modern sensors and cameras have a limited field of view, a detailed map eases the burden on the onboard computer by delivering static road information ahead of time. As Leo Sei, Chief Product Officer, put it: "With the intelligent map, the car no longer drives around like a tourist, but like a local who knows exactly where to go."

Second, location intelligence for enterprises, governments, and AI agents. This market is growing at a double-digit annual pace, and TomTom estimates that roughly 70 to 80 percent of all enterprise data contains a spatial component. The company is making this data directly machine-readable for software agents through Orbis APIs and an MCP (Model Context Protocol) server.

The financial transformation underscores the strategic shift. CFO Taco Titulaer explained that where the company in its heyday relied on navigation devices with physical margins around 40 percent, it now operates as a pure software company with gross margins heading toward 90 percent. The focus has moved radically from hardware to software, with the company concentrating on its core strength: owning the data with location intelligence layered on top.

The linchpin is Orbis, TomTom's AI-native map infrastructure. Orbis combines OpenStreetMap, Overture Maps, TomTom's own proprietary data, and real-time data from vehicles and other sensors. The platform now uses more than 5 billion data points to continuously enrich and verify the map, with 2.8 trillion kilometres of worldwide distance data stored and trillions of GPS points collected. Every time software is activated in a manufacturer's car, anonymous data is passed to TomTom.

For autonomous driving, TomTom has developed Orbis Lane Model Maps containing lane-level information — exact geometry of lanes, connections between lanes, and road markings. But the most interesting development is Orbis functioning as a spatial knowledge layer for AI. AI agents can use the map data to answer questions or carry out tasks where location plays a role: determining which stores lie within a given travel time, which roads are affected by an incident, or which route is most suitable for a truck.

TomTom distinguishes between AI assistants and AI agents. TAIA (TomTom AI Assistant) works at the front end, handling conversational prompts. AI agents are integrated deeper into the architecture and can answer complex questions. The Agent Toolkit ensures that agents from other parties can access TomTom's spatial intelligence. Users interact through a ChatGPT-like environment — an insurer could ask which outstanding damage claims are within 500 metres of a flood area, a city planner could query how travel times around a bridge have changed due to roadworks, or a transport planner could instruct an agent to reroute vehicles stuck behind an incident.

Whether this new direction can match the record revenue of 1.7 billion euros achieved in 2007, CFO Titulaer hesitates to predict: "Back then, we entered a market that didn't exist yet, and we never foresaw it becoming such a massive success."
