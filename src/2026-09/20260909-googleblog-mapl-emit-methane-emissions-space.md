## Synthesis
In a study published in PNAS, Google and NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory introduced MAPL-EMIT, a deep learning model that tracks methane emissions globally from space using NASA's EMIT (Earth Surface Mineral Dust Source Investigation) instrument. The model addresses a critical bottleneck in methane detection: the sheer volume of satellite imagery that must be analyzed to identify methane plumes — clouds of methane gas released into the atmosphere.

MAPL-EMIT was trained on 3.6 million physics-simulated methane plumes, enabling it to cut through complex, noisy terrain to detect plumes that human experts miss. The results are significant: it detects 50% more plumes than human experts and identifies more than 23,000 additional plumes globally, including 24 out of 25 of the world's largest-emitting landfills. This is particularly important because methane is a potent greenhouse gas with 30 times the warming potential of carbon dioxide over a 100-year timeframe. Finding and fixing methane sources is one of the fastest and most cost-effective ways to slow near-term warming.

Google has made the results openly accessible. The global plume database is available on Google Earth Engine, alongside an interactive Earth Engine app for visualization. The open-source models are published on Kaggle, and inference tools are available on GitHub, enabling researchers, policymakers, and operators to use the model for climate mitigation efforts. The six-panel satellite graphic accompanying the announcement shows detected methane plumes in California (USA), Turkmenistan, Delhi (India), São Paulo (Brazil), Katowice (Poland), and Shanxi (China) — illustrating the global scale of the detection capability.
