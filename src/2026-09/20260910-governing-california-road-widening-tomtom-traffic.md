# California Keeps Widening Roads. Traffic Keeps Getting Worse.
**Source**: https://www.governing.com/infrastructure/california-keeps-widening-roads-traffic-keeps-getting-worse
**Date**: 2026-09-09
**Author**: Alexiah Syrai Olsen, The Sacramento Bee (Governing Magazine)
**Keywords**: TomTom Traffic Index, California, Los Angeles, congestion, induced demand, road widening, infrastructure, transportation

## Elevator pitch
California ranks as the third most congested state in the US according to the TomTom Traffic Index, and billions spent on road widening show no clear evidence of reducing traffic — illustrating the phenomenon of induced demand.

## Takeaways
- California was the 3rd most congested state in the US in the 2025 TomTom Traffic Index
- Los Angeles ranked 25th worldwide for worst average congestion, with 59.8% time lost to traffic
- LA drivers lose 83 hours during rush hour per year; San Francisco has the 6th worst congestion in North America
- San Francisco has the lowest average speed (12.6 mph) and lowest average distance in 15 minutes (4.5 miles) in the US
- The American Society of Civil Engineers gave California roadways a "D" grade
- Transport for America reports that "expanding highways to improve traffic flow can actually increase congestion by encouraging people to drive more"
- Nearly one-third of California's roads are in poor condition

## Synthesis
This article uses TomTom Traffic Index data to examine a paradox in infrastructure policy: California continues to invest billions in road widening despite evidence that adding highway capacity may increase congestion through induced demand. The TomTom data provides granular city-level metrics: Los Angeles drivers lose 83 hours per year to rush-hour congestion, while San Francisco drivers crawl at 12.6 mph average speeds — the lowest in the nation.

The article frames this as a case study in why adding highway capacity fails to solve congestion. Citing Transport for America, the authors note that wider roads encourage more driving, which ultimately fills the new capacity. The TomTom Traffic Index serves as the quantitative backbone for the analysis, demonstrating how location intelligence data can inform infrastructure policy debates — and challenge conventional wisdom about road building.
