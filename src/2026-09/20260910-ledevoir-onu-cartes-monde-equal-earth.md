# Will the UN Decision Change Our World Maps?
**Source**: https://www.ledevoir.com/monde/1007777/decision-onu-modifiera-elle-cartes-monde
**Date**: 2026-09-10
**Author**: Sarah Boumedda (Le Devoir)
**Keywords**: UN, Equal Earth, Mercator projection, cartography, Africa, colonialism, world map, Togo, African Union

## Elevator pitch
The UN General Assembly adopted a resolution with 164 votes in favor and one against, calling for world maps to use representations that more faithfully reflect the relative size of continents — favoring the Equal Earth projection over the colonial-era Mercator projection.

## Takeaways
- The resolution was passed with 164 votes for and 1 against, presented by a coalition of African countries led by Togo
- The Equal Earth projection, developed in 2018, preserves proportional area while accepting distortions elsewhere
- Greenland appears similar in size to Africa on Mercator maps, but Africa is actually 14 times larger
- Brazil appears the same size as Alaska on Mercator but is in reality five times larger
- UQAM geography professor Rodolphe Gonzalès frames this as a "symbolic injustice, a geometric injustice between North and South"
- Google Maps and other navigators predominantly use Mercator because it enables smooth zoom

## Synthesis
The UN General Assembly's adoption of the "Correct the Map" resolution marks a milestone in the long-running debate over cartographic representation. The Mercator projection, developed in the 16th century for maritime navigation, systematically exaggerates the size of territories towards the poles, making Europe and North America appear larger relative to equatorial regions. This creates what scholars call a "geometric injustice" that reinforces colonial-era hierarchies.

The Equal Earth projection, jointly developed in 2018 by Bojan Šavrič (Esri), Tom Patterson (US National Park Service), and Bernhard Jenny (Monash University), offers an equal-area alternative. However, as Le Devoir notes, Google Maps and other digital navigators continue to use Mercator because it allows smooth, continuous zoom. The practical question remains whether tech companies and national mapping agencies will adopt the UN recommendation or continue with Mercator for navigational purposes.

Professor Gonzalès emphasizes that no map is perfectly neutral: mapping a spherical Earth necessarily involves choices about what to preserve and what to distort. The resolution's significance is therefore both symbolic — recognizing colonial legacies in how we see the world — and practical, encouraging institutions to adopt fairer representations.
