# Correcting the Map, as We Must, and Yet Still
**Source**: https://verfassungsblog.de/correct-the-map-resolution/
**Date**: 2026-09-10
**Author**: Luis Eslava (Verfassungsblog)
**Keywords**: Correct the Map, UN resolution, Mercator, Equal Earth, Togo, African Union, cognitive justice, colonialism, cartography

## Elevator pitch
A legal scholar analyzes the UN's "Correct the Map" resolution (A/RES/80/297), arguing that correcting cartographic representation is a matter of cognitive justice — recognizing and contesting inherited colonial ways of seeing the world.

## Takeaways
- 164 UN Member States adopted resolution A/RES/80/297, with only one voting against
- The resolution was led by Togo on behalf of the African Group, building on an African Union campaign
- The Equal Earth projection was developed in 2018 by researchers at Esri, US National Park Service, and Monash University
- Togo's Foreign Minister Robert Dussey framed the issue as "cognitive justice" — access to geographical knowledge, dignity, and historical memory
- The Mercator projection "midwifed a particular global socio-economic and political order" centered on Europe
- The author argues that no perfectly neutral map exists; every projection involves choices about what to preserve

## Synthesis
Luis Eslava's analysis on Verfassungsblog places the UN's "Correct the Map" resolution in a broader frame of colonial legacy and cognitive justice. The Mercator projection, developed in 1569 for navigation, did not merely represent the world — it actively helped create a global order centered on Europe. By exaggerating the size of territories toward the poles, it gave visual prominence to Europe and North America, reinforcing colonial hierarchies.

The Equal Earth projection, adopted by the UN resolution, addresses the area-distortion problem but cannot resolve all representational choices. As Eslava notes, "no perfectly neutral map waiting to be discovered behind Mercator" exists — every projection involves decisions about how the world becomes visible. The resolution's significance therefore lies not in achieving cartographic perfection but in recognizing that inherited representations organize how we see the world, and that correcting them is a matter of justice. The sole "no" vote (implied to be the United States) underscores the political sensitivity of challenging established geographic narratives.
