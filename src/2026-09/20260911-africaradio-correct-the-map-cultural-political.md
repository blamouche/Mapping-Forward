# "Correct the Map" Resolution: From Cartographic Question to Cultural and Political Issue
**Source**: https://www.africaradio.com/actualite-116461-la-resolution-correct-the-map-adoptee-on-passe-d-une-question-cartographique-a-une-vraie-question-culturelle-et-politique
**Date**: 2026-09-11
**Author**: Keisha Mougani (Africa Radio)
**Keywords**: Correct the Map, Speak Up Africa, Fara Ndiaye, UN resolution, Mercator, Equal Earth, Togo, African Union, cartographic representation, cultural justice

## Elevator pitch
In an interview with Africa Radio, Speak Up Africa co-founder Fara Ndiaye discusses the UN's adoption of the "Correct the Map" resolution, arguing that cartographic representation is not merely a technical issue but a cultural and political one that shapes how generations perceive the world.

## Takeaways
- The "Correct the Map" campaign was launched by NGOs Africa No Filter and Speak Up Africa, co-founded by Fara Ndiaye
- The campaign does not exclusively promote Equal Earth — it advocates ending the default use of Mercator, and would accept any equal-area projection
- Equal Earth was chosen partly because it is free to use and less controversial than the Gall-Peters projection
- 164 countries voted in favor — described as "not Africa against Western countries, but 164 countries against one"
- The campaign's next phase focuses on working with education ministries to update school textbooks
- The resolution also calls on UN agencies to update their working documents to use equal-area projections as the default
- Ndiaye emphasizes the issue affects not just Africa but also Latin America and South Asia, whose surfaces are particularly reduced on Mercator

## Synthesis
Fara Ndiaye, co-founder of Speak Up Africa — one of the NGOs behind the "Correct the Map" campaign — frames the UN's adoption of the resolution as the beginning of a long implementation effort rather than an endpoint. In an interview with Africa Radio, she emphasized that the campaign's core objective is not to promote a specific projection but to end the unquestioned default use of the 16th-century Mercator projection, which was designed for navigation but became the standard world map in classrooms, media, and digital tools.

The Equal Earth projection was selected because it is free to use and less politically charged than the Gall-Peters projection. However, Ndiaye is clear that the campaign would accept any equal-area alternative — the goal is representational accuracy, not a particular cartographic method. She describes the 164-1 vote as "not Africa against Western countries, but 164 countries against one," emphasizing the broad consensus rather than a geopolitical divide.

The campaign's next phase is practical implementation: working with education ministries to update textbooks, and pushing UN agencies to adopt equal-area projections in their working documents. Ndiaye frames the issue as fundamentally cultural: a representation that generations of children see in school and billions of people encounter on their screens is not merely a technical tool but something that shapes our vision of the world. "We pass from a cartographic question to a truly cultural and political question," she says.
