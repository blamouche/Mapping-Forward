# Apple Maps Caught in "New America" Toponymy Controversy Over New Mexico
**Source**: https://www.appsystem.fr/260344/apple-maps-new-america-nouveau-mexique/
**Date**: 2026-09-11
**Author**: Adrien Vasseur (AppSystem)
**Keywords**: Apple Maps, Google Maps, New Mexico, New America, toponymy, GNIS, Geographic Names Information System, Trump, cartographic politics, place names

## Elevator pitch
New Mexico's congressional delegation has asked Apple and Google to commit to keeping the state's name on their maps, preempting potential "New America" renaming after social media posts by the US president suggested the change.

## Takeaways
- Democratic representatives from New Mexico wrote to Apple and Google asking them not to rename their state "New America" in mapping services
- The request follows social media posts by President Trump suggesting the name change, though no official measure has been taken
- Apple Maps and Google Maps rely on the federal Geographic Names Information System (GNIS) for US place names
- Previous GNIS updates (Gulf of Mexico, Lake Ontario) were reflected in Apple Maps after presidential decrees modified entries
- Google Maps applied the Lake Ontario name change immediately upon the decree's signing; Apple Maps updated after GNIS was updated
- The episode highlights how digital mapping has become a concrete terrain for political decisions

## Synthesis
A new toponymic controversy has emerged in the United States, this time involving New Mexico. After President Trump posted on social media suggesting the state be renamed "New America," New Mexico's Democratic congressional delegation proactively wrote to Apple and Google asking them to publicly commit to keeping the state's name on their maps. While the president cannot rename a state by executive order, the preemptive request reflects lessons learned from previous toponymic changes — notably the renaming of the Gulf of Mexico and Lake Ontario, which were reflected in both Apple Maps and Google Maps after presidential decrees modified the federal Geographic Names Information System (GNIS).

The controversy highlights the dependency of digital mapping platforms on federal naming databases. Apple Maps reflected GNIS changes for Lake Ontario only after the database was updated, while Google Maps applied the change immediately upon the decree. This difference in approach illustrates how the same political decision can propagate through mapping platforms at different speeds depending on their data pipelines. The episode extends a trend where cartographic naming has become a visible battleground for political power, with the names displayed in everyday navigation apps directly translating federal administrative decisions into public debate.
