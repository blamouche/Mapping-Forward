# After Google Maps, Simon Weckert Now Targets AI Cameras With "Digital Camouflage" Shirt
**Source**: https://www.bluewin.ch/fr/tech-et-digital/apres-google-maps-il-veut-maintenant-pieger-les-cameras-ia-avec-cette-chemise-li.3596070
**Date**: 2026-09-11
**Author**: Nicolas Barman (Bluewin)
**Keywords**: Simon Weckert, Digital Camouflage, adversarial attack, YOLO, AI surveillance, Google Maps, cartographic manipulation, computer vision, Berlin, Kottbusser Tor

## Elevator pitch
Six years after tricking Google Maps with 99 smartphones to fake a Berlin traffic jam, artist Simon Weckert has created a "Digital Camouflage" shirt that uses adversarial patterns to make its wearer invisible to AI-powered surveillance cameras.

## Takeaways
- Weckert's "Digital Camouflage" shirt uses psychedelic patterns designed to defeat YOLO object detection algorithms
- The technique uses "adversarial attacks" — the pattern is iteratively modified until the algorithm's confidence in detecting a person drops below threshold
- The project is a response to 30 new AI-assisted surveillance cameras installed at Kottbusser Tor in Berlin's Kreuzberg district
- The cameras are not yet active; their activation is expected after Berlin's September 20 election
- Weckert's 2020 "Google Maps hack" used 99 smartphones in a cart to create a fake traffic jam on Google Maps in Berlin
- The new project shifts from manipulating cartographic data to exposing the fragility of AI vision systems

## Synthesis
Berlin-based artist and designer Simon Weckert, known for his 2020 performance where 99 smartphones in a cart tricked Google Maps into showing a phantom traffic jam, has turned his attention to AI surveillance. His "Digital Camouflage" shirt exploits a well-known vulnerability in computer vision systems: adversarial patterns that, while visible to humans, cause object detection algorithms to fail. The shirt's psychedelic design was developed through iterative adversarial attacks against YOLO ("You Only Look Once"), a widely used family of object detection models.

The project is timely: Berlin has installed 30 new AI-assisted surveillance cameras at Kottbusser Tor, a area authorities consider high-crime. While the cameras are not yet operational — their activation is expected after Berlin's September 20 election — Weckert's work raises questions about the reliability of AI-powered surveillance and the ease with which it can be defeated. The project also illustrates a broader pattern in Weckert's work: using art to expose the assumptions and vulnerabilities of the algorithmic systems that increasingly shape our perception of the world, from navigation maps to surveillance cameras.
