# Mapping Silicate Minerals From Space
**Source**: https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/news/mapping-silicate-minerals-from-space
**Date**: 2026-09-11
**Author**: Charlie Plain (NASA Earthdata)
**Keywords**: NASA, ECOSTRESS, ASTER, thermal infrared spectroscopy, silicate minerals, mineral mapping, lithium, geothermal energy, satellite remote sensing, ECOSTRESS, GeoTIFF

## Elevator pitch
NASA-funded scientists have created the first global maps of silicate, sulfate, and carbonate minerals in Earth's arid regions using thermal infrared spectroscopy data from the ECOSTRESS and ASTER satellite sensors, enabling space-based identification of mineral resources including lithium-bearing rocks.

## Takeaways
- Researchers created 1-kilometer resolution global maps of silicate, sulfate, and carbonate minerals using ECOSTRESS (ISS) and ASTER (Terra satellite) thermal infrared data
- Thermal infrared spectroscopy is the only known method to detect silica from space, using each mineral's unique spectral signature
- Silicate deposits are associated with lithium-bearing minerals used in batteries, making the maps valuable for resource exploration
- Silicate alteration on the surface can also indicate underground geothermal activity, pointing to potential renewable energy sites
- The maps were validated by comparing satellite observations with field data from sites in California, New Mexico, and Morocco
- The data complement NASA's EMIT sensor, which detects oxides, sulfides, and clays associated with precious minerals
- The dataset is publicly available through NASA's Earthdata Search and the Land Processes DAAC as GeoTIFF

## Synthesis
NASA-funded researchers led by Federico Rabuffi from the Jet Propulsion Laboratory have produced the first global maps of silicate and related minerals using thermal infrared (TIR) spectroscopy data from two orbiting sensors: ECOSTRESS aboard the International Space Station and ASTER aboard the Terra satellite. The 1-kilometer resolution maps cover Earth's arid and semi-arid regions, where mineral deposits are most exposed at the surface.

The significance of mapping silicates from space goes beyond geology. Lithium-bearing minerals — critical for batteries and electronics — are often found in silicate-rich rocks like quartz and feldspar. By identifying these formations from orbit, the maps can guide resource exploration without expensive ground surveys. Additionally, silicate alteration patterns can signal underground geothermal activity, pointing to potential sites for renewable energy production.

The TIR data complement NASA's EMIT imaging spectrometer, which detects a different set of minerals (oxides, sulfides, clays) using visible to shortwave infrared observations. Together, the sensors provide a multi-layered view of Earth's surface composition. The maps are publicly available through NASA's Earthdata Search as GeoTIFF files, making them accessible to researchers, mining companies, and geothermal energy developers worldwide.
