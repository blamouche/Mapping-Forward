# A New UN Resolution Takes a Step Toward Decolonizing World Maps
**Source**: https://thetyee.ca/Analysis/2026/09/11/UN-Resolution-Decolonizing-World-Maps/
**Date**: 2026-09-11
**Author**: Sébastien Caquard (The Tyee / The Conversation)
**Keywords**: Correct the Map, UN resolution, Mercator, Equal Earth, colonialism, Web Mercator, Google Maps, decolonization, Arno Peters, cartographic justice

## Elevator pitch
A geography professor argues that the UN's "Correct the Map" resolution is a meaningful step toward decolonizing cartography, but notes that online platforms like Google Maps still use the Web Mercator projection, limiting the resolution's practical impact on everyday mapping.

## Takeaways
- The UN resolution (A/RES/80/297) was adopted 164-1 with 6 abstentions; the US was the sole opponent
- The Mercator projection's distortions were known for centuries but only politically challenged in the 1970s, beginning with Arno Peters' equal-area map
- The Equal Earth projection (2018) is positioned as a less controversial alternative to the Gall-Peters projection
- Web Mercator remains the default for Google Maps and similar platforms, perpetuating the same distortions the UN resolution seeks to correct
- Online maps don't just reproduce Mercator's size distortions — they also structure worldviews around road networks, business accessibility, and consumption
- Maps are "instruments of power" that influence how we see and interact with the world, not neutral representations

## Synthesis
Sébastien Caquard, a geography professor at Concordia University, frames the UN's "Correct the Map" resolution within the long history of cartographic politics. The Mercator projection, designed in 1569 for maritime navigation, became the de facto world map despite dramatically distorting the relative size of continents — enlarging Europe and North America while shrinking Africa, Latin America, and South Asia. The political implications of this distortion were only broadly recognized in the 1970s, when Arno Peters proposed an equal-area alternative that ignited the "map wars."

The UN resolution represents another step in this struggle, but Caquard identifies a critical gap: its impact on online mapping platforms remains uncertain. Web Mercator, a variant of the original projection, remains the default for Google Maps and similar services because it divides the Earth into computationally efficient squares for smooth zooming. These platforms don't just reproduce Mercator's size distortions — they also impose a worldview structured around road networks, business locations, and consumption patterns. The resolution's practical impact will depend on whether digital platforms adopt equal-area projections, a shift that has not yet materialized despite the UN's endorsement.
