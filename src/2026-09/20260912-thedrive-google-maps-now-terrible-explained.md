# Google Maps Is Now Terrible: Explained
**Source**: https://www.thedrive.com/news/google-maps-is-now-terrible-explained
**Date**: 2026-09-12
**Author**: Stephen Edelstein (The Drive)
**Keywords**: Google Maps, GPS, navigation apps, traffic congestion, routing algorithms, Nash equilibrium, induced demand, fuel savings, GPS adoption

## Elevator pitch
The Drive explains why Google Maps and similar navigation apps have gotten worse at saving time: mass adoption of GPS routing means too many users are sent down the same alternate routes, creating a Nash equilibrium where individual optimization degrades collective outcomes — and there is no easy fix.

## Takeaways
- An estimated 7 billion devices worldwide rely on GPS, from phones to golf balls
- A 2019 study found that between 2007 and 2017, services like Google Maps saved 52 billion gallons of fuel and a trillion miles through more efficient routing
- GPS adoption has generated an estimated $1.4 trillion in economic benefits since the 1980s
- The core problem: routing apps don't track how many users they've sent on a given route and can't know where competitor services are sending users
- When everyone uses the same app to avoid traffic, alternate routes become congested too, negating the benefit
- This creates a Nash equilibrium where individual route optimization degrades collective traffic outcomes
- There is no simple solution — the problem is structural to how decentralized routing works at scale

## Synthesis
The Drive offers an explainer for a frustration many drivers feel: Google Maps and other navigation apps just don't seem to work as well as they used to. The article traces the problem to the very success of GPS-based routing. With an estimated 7 billion GPS-dependent devices worldwide, the algorithms that once saved 52 billion gallons of fuel and a trillion miles between 2007 and 2017 are now victims of their own adoption.

The core issue is that routing apps optimize for the individual user without accounting for how many other users are receiving the same recommendation. When thousands of drivers are simultaneously directed to the same "faster" alternate route, that route becomes congested, negating the advantage. Since competing services like Google Maps and Waze don't share routing data with each other, the problem is compounded across platforms. The result is a classic Nash equilibrium: what is optimal for each individual becomes suboptimal for the system as a whole. The article concludes that while the benefits of GPS routing remain enormous, the diminishing returns at scale represent a structural problem with no straightforward fix.
