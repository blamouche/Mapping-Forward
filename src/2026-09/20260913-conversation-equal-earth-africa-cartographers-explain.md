## Elevator pitch
Cartographers Jack Swab and Derek Alderman explain the UN's endorsement of the Equal Earth projection, why the Mercator map distorts Africa's true size, how the new projection works, and why the US was the lone "no" vote against the resolution.

## Takeaways
- The UN passed a non-binding resolution endorsing the Equal Earth projection over the Mercator projection, which exaggerates land towards the poles by 2-14x
- The Mercator projection remains useful for navigation (preserving compass bearings), which is why Google Maps and Apple Maps still use it
- The Equal Earth projection minimizes distortions of continental sizes, making it better for educational, public communication, and policy purposes
- Map-making is decentralized — no single authority regulates which projection is used; the UN endorsement is symbolic, not mandatory
- The US was the only "no" vote, calling the resolution a "radical and ideological project" and objecting to language linking Mercator's distortions to colonial legacies
- Ukraine raised concerns over the depiction of Crimea, while other nations raised contested territory issues — but these were geopolitical posturing, not map issues
- African nations framed the resolution as "cognitive justice" — correcting the mental map that equates size with power and importance

## Synthesis
The Conversation invited cartographers Jack Swab and Derek Alderman, geographers who study the social and communicative power of maps, to explain the significance of the UN's "Correct the Map" resolution. The resolution endorses the Equal Earth projection, which more fairly represents the true size of the Earth's landmasses compared to the 16th-century Mercator projection that greatly exaggerates land toward the poles.

A map projection is the technique cartographers use to transform the round, three-dimensional Earth into a flat, two-dimensional map. This flattening inevitably distorts shapes and areas — no projection is distortion-free. The Mercator projection, designed in 1569 for ocean navigation, preserves compass bearings but inflates polar regions and shrinks equatorial ones, making Africa look much smaller than it is. In the Mercator projection, countries near the poles can appear 2-14 times their true size. The Equal Earth projection, unveiled in 2018, minimizes these distortions, making it better suited for global-scale world maps used in education, public communication, and policy.

The Mercator projection won't disappear entirely — it remains the standard for navigation, which is why Google Maps and Apple Maps use a variant of it. But the UN endorsement signals a shift toward Equal Earth for general-purpose world maps. The resolution is non-binding: map-making is decentralized with no single regulatory authority. Real adoption requires intentional revision of maps in textbooks, databases, media, and diplomatic discussions.

The political dimensions are striking. The US was the only "no" vote, calling the proposal a "radical and ideological project" and objecting to language linking Mercator's distortions to colonial legacies and Africa's marginalization. The cartographers note the hypocrisy: the US objects to discussing the political implications of cartography even as President Trump unilaterally renamed the Gulf of Mexico to the Gulf of America and Lake Ontario to Lake America. African nations framed the resolution as "cognitive justice" — correcting the mental map of global leaders is an important step in amplifying Africa's relevance and rights.
