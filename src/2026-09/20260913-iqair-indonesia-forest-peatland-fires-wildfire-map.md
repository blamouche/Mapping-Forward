# Wildfire Map Spotlight: Indonesia Forest and Peatland Fires
**Source**: https://www.iqair.com/fr/newsroom/wildfire-map-spotlight-indonesia-forest-and-peatland-fires-september-2026
**Date**: 2026-09-13
**Author**: IQAir Staff Writers
**Keywords**: Indonesia, wildfires, peatland fires, karhutla, Kalimantan, Sumatra, air quality map, El Niño, haze, IQAir

## Elevator pitch
Indonesia is experiencing its most intense wildfire season in 11 years, with forest and peatland fires burning across 1.36 million hectares by August 2026, concentrated in Kalimantan and Sumatra, producing massive smoke haze that has affected over 12.5 million people and spread to Malaysia and Singapore.

## Takeaways
- Indonesia's 2026 wildfire season is the most intense in 11 years, driven by a strong El Niño and positive Indian Ocean Dipole
- Cumulative burned area reached 1,358,714 hectares by August 31, with 1.32 million hectares burned in August alone
- Peatland fires account for ~447,600 hectares and produce 3x more fine particles and 5x more sulfur dioxide than conventional forest fires
- Over 12.5 million people across seven Indonesian provinces have been exposed to haze, with respiratory infections doubling to 113,336 cases by September 9
- 688 high-confidence hotspots detected on September 11, with Central Kalimantan recording 271 alone
- Indonesia deployed 53 aircraft across six priority provinces, and Japan delivered three CH-47 Chinook helicopters plus 180 personnel
- 19.7 million metric tons of fire-related emissions in the first week of September alone — the highest of any country globally that week

## Synthesis
Indonesia is facing its worst wildfire crisis in over a decade, with the archipelago's unique tropical peatland ecosystems driving an exceptionally destructive and polluting fire season. The fires, known nationally as "karhutla" (karhutla — hutan dan lahan, forest and land fires), consist of both surface and underground fires burning across the archipelago, with the most intense activity concentrated in Kalimantan (Indonesian Borneo) and Sumatra, and a major front in South Papua.

What distinguishes this event from conventional wildfires is the fuel: Indonesia holds approximately 36% of the world's tropical peatlands. When these waterlogged soils dry out during drought, fire spreads below the surface and smolders in deep organic deposits, producing roughly three times more fine particles, five times more sulfur dioxide, three times more organic carbon, and twice as much methane and carbon monoxide as other tropical forest fires. The fire has reached areas meant to be protected, including conservation forests, biodiversity zones, national parks, and forest reserves.

The scale is staggering: cumulative burned area reached 1,358,714 hectares by August 31, with August alone accounting for 1.32 million hectares — a dramatic escalation from July's 109,000 hectares. Fire emissions were 273% above seasonal average in early September, and between September 1-7, Indonesia released 19.7 million metric tons of fire-related emissions, the highest of any country worldwide that week, representing over a third of the global total.

The health impact extends far beyond the burn zones. Over 12.5 million people in seven provinces have been exposed to haze, with respiratory infections surging from 50,891 cases on September 1 to 113,336 by September 9. Kuching, Kuala Lumpur, and Singapore have frequently ranked among the world's most polluted major cities. The Indonesian government has distributed nearly a million masks, deployed thousands of health workers, closed nine national parks, and shifted schools to remote learning. El Niño is expected to persist until early 2027, meaning natural rainfall may not arrive until late October or November.
