## Synthesis
Australia is set to look noticeably larger on world maps following the UN General Assembly's historic vote to endorse the Equal Earth projection over the centuries-old Mercator map. The resolution, supported by 164 countries with only the United States voting against, urges member states to adopt the "Equal Earth" projection, which more fairly represents the true proportions of the Earth's landmasses.

Australian geographer Greg Lauer, a founding member of OSGeo Oceania, explained that the change is about visual perception rather than geographic reality. Australia will appear around 10% larger relative to northern landmasses — not because Australia itself looks bigger, but because Greenland appears much smaller. "In simple terms how we see Australia in terms of size may change," Lauer said. "The actual size of course doesn't change, but if you look around on the Equal Earth projection, Greenland is a lot smaller and you can better see how Australia compares to Africa for example."

The Mercator map, first designed in 1569 by Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator, was built for ocean navigation. While it preserved accurate compass bearings, it distorted continental sizes by inflating polar regions and shrinking equatorial ones. "The old map was never wrong. It was right for navigation, but it was wrong for comparison. I always argue that it was misused, not mistaken," Lauer explained. Africa is 14 times the size of Greenland, yet the Mercator map makes them look roughly equal.

Lauer noted that where the new map is centred matters significantly for Australian and New Zealand viewers. A map centred over the UK — the traditional choice — distorts Australia and pushes New Zealand to the far edge. A Pacific-centred projection would be preferred Down Under. He also noted that the UN resolution is largely symbolic: "The UN aren't going to send the blue helmets out to every classroom in the country and tell them to pull down that map and put up a new map." His concluding insight captures the fundamental truth of cartography: "Every flat map lies about something. The honest ones kind of tell you what they're lying about. I think every map is an argument, right? And the question for us is, do we know what it's arguing?"
