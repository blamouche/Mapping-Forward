# How Do GPS Apps Like Google Maps Know When There's Police Ahead?
**Source**: https://www.slashgear.com/2252697/how-gps-apps-google-maps-know-police-ahead/
**Date**: 2026-09-13
**Author**: SlashGear
**Keywords**: Google Maps, Waze, police reports, crowdsourcing, GPS apps, navigation, Android Auto, Apple CarPlay, incident reports

## Elevator pitch
Google Maps and other GPS apps detect police sightings through crowdsourced user reports, not through any official data feed — drivers submit reports that other users confirm or deny, with Waze data also integrated into Google Maps.

## Takeaways
- Google Maps relies on crowdsourced user reports for police sightings, not official data feeds
- When a driver reports police, the report appears on the maps of other users on the same road
- Other drivers can confirm whether the police sighting is still valid, keeping reports up to date
- Google Maps also integrates police report data from Waze, which Google owns
- Waze rewards users with points for submitting reports, creating a larger dataset for Google Maps
- Users can submit reports via the "Report" icon in navigation mode on smartphone, Android Auto, or Apple CarPlay
- Reports are temporary and evolve with the situation — they are not permanent markers
- The feature raises concerns about helping drivers evade checkpoints, but also helps with speed awareness and safety

## Synthesis
Navigation apps like Google Maps have become integral to daily driving, providing not just traffic conditions but also road closures, speed cameras, flooded roads, roadwork, low visibility warnings, and police sightings. But how exactly do these apps know about real-time police presence? The answer is crowdsourcing — the data comes from real users driving on those roads.

When driving with Google Maps in navigation mode, users can report what they see, including police sightings. These incident reports then appear on the maps of other users driving on the same road. Google Maps takes feedback from other drivers to confirm whether the report is still valid, creating a self-correcting system. The app also sources reports from Waze, which Google owns. Waze has a built-in system to reward users with points for submitting reports, and integrating this data gives Google Maps a larger dataset to work with.

Users can submit reports easily: in navigation mode, tap the "Report" icon on the smartphone or Android Auto/Apple CarPlay screen, select "Police," and the report is submitted with a small police icon appearing at the user's location. Users can also enable or disable these alerts through Settings > Navigation > Alert options. The feature is not available in every region but should be available for US users.

The reports are not permanent — they change as the situation evolves and should not be blindly relied upon since they are user-submitted. There are concerns that these features can help drivers evade checkpoints, potentially putting others at risk (for example, someone driving under the influence avoiding a DUI checkpoint). But for many drivers, the feature makes navigation simpler, reminds them to slow down, and helps avoid speeding tickets.
