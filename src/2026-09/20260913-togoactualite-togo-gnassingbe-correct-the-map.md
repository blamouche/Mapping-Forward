## Elevator pitch
Togolese President Faure Gnassingbé has championed the UN's "Correct the Map" resolution as a triumph for Africa, but critics at home dismiss the celebration as a distraction from two decades of domestic infrastructure failure and political stagnation.

## Synthesis
While African nations celebrated the UN General Assembly's adoption of the "Correct the Map" resolution — which endorses the Equal Earth projection to more accurately represent Africa's true size on world maps — Togo's President Faure Gnassingbé and Foreign Minister Robert Dussey positioned themselves as vocal champions of the cartographic victory. However, the Togolese press has responded with sharp domestic criticism, framing the celebration as a demagogic distraction from two decades of governance failure.

The critique from Togo Actualité is blistering: while Gnassingbé celebrates correcting global cartography, the country's own physical and social landscape remains frozen in the 1960s. The CHU Sylvanus Olympio, the capital's main teaching hospital, remains a post-colonial relic described as a "mouroir" (death house). The railway network is still the colonial-era infrastructure. The promised highway connecting north to south was never built. No grand monuments distinguish Lomé from other West African capitals. The country's two universities lack infrastructure to inspire student pride, and modern markets, tourist sites, and reliable utilities remain absent.

The article draws a pointed contrast with neighboring countries: Benin has built new monuments and modernized its road network, Senegal has developed its railway, and Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana have constructed multiple bridges and interchanges. While these states have redrawn their physical landscapes to earn international respect, Togo's leaders have focused on redrawing the world map instead — an endeavor critics call "ridiculous and useless" given the regime's inability to modernize its own country since Gnassingbé succeeded his father in 2005 amid violence.
