## Synthesis
When thousands of drivers simultaneously follow GPS navigation apps, the result can be counterproductive for the city as a whole. A study published in Nature Communications by researchers from CNR-Isti and the University of Pisa demonstrates this phenomenon through traffic simulations of Florence, Milan, and Rome, integrating GPS data, road network characteristics, and routes proposed by services like Google Maps, TomTom, Bing Maps, and Mapbox.

The researchers identified a "concentration effect" — as more people use navigation services, routes increasingly resemble each other. Algorithms identify the most efficient roads and funnel a growing share of drivers onto the same arterial roads. In simulations where all drivers follow navigation, route diversity can decrease by up to 14%.

The study found that when traffic is light, navigation systems generally distribute trips efficiently and can even reduce CO2 emissions. However, during peak congestion hours, a critical threshold emerges: when 60-80% of drivers follow navigation, environmental benefits begin to diminish. In some Rome scenarios, these benefits disappeared entirely or even reversed.

The researchers emphasize that the problem is not navigation itself but the relationship between individual optimization and collective outcomes. An algorithm answers "What is the best route for me?" but when thousands receive similar answers, the result modifies the system on which the advice was based. The fastest road becomes congested precisely because many drivers were directed there. The study calls for systems that consider not only individual user benefits but also the overall effects on the city.
