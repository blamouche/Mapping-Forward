# Drug Dealers Listed on Google Maps as Ordinary Businesses
**Source**: https://www.franceinfo.fr/societe/drogue/des-dealers-references-sur-google-maps_8192528.html
**Date**: 2026-09-14
**Author**: Le Journal de 20h, France Télévisions
**Keywords**: Google Maps, drug dealing, cannabis, platform abuse, content moderation, France

## Elevator pitch
Drug dealers in France are using Google Maps as a virtual storefront, listing dealing spots as ordinary businesses with addresses, ratings, reviews, and even phone numbers — exposing a major content moderation gap on the world's most popular mapping platform.

## Takeaways
- Cannabis dealers are creating fake business listings on Google Maps with precise addresses, star ratings, customer reviews, and contact numbers
- These listings appear as ordinary commercial points of interest to unsuspecting users
- The phenomenon raises serious questions about Google's ability to moderate user-generated business content on its mapping platform
- Law enforcement faces new challenges as drug distribution moves onto mainstream digital platforms
- The case highlights how legitimate mapping infrastructure can be repurposed for illicit commerce

## Synthesis
A franceinfo investigation has revealed that Google Maps is being used as a directory for drug dealing operations in France. Dealers are creating business listings on the platform that display their dealing locations as if they were ordinary commercial establishments, complete with street addresses, customer ratings out of five stars, written reviews, and in some cases phone numbers for direct contact.

The listings are sophisticated enough to pass as legitimate businesses to casual users browsing the map. They include business hours, customer feedback describing product quality, and even photos. The drug being sold is primarily cannabis, and the listings effectively function as a virtual storefront for the illicit trade. Customers can locate the nearest dealing spot, check its rating, and read reviews from previous buyers — all through the same interface millions of people use to find restaurants and gas stations.

This discovery exposes a significant vulnerability in Google's content moderation systems. While the company has invested heavily in automated tools to detect fake reviews and fraudulent business listings, the drug dealing listings demonstrate that bad actors can still exploit the platform's open business registration process. Google Maps allows anyone to suggest a business listing, and while the company verifies some information, the volume of submissions makes comprehensive verification impossible.

The case also illustrates how mainstream digital platforms are being repurposed for illegal activities. Unlike darknet marketplaces that operate on specialized infrastructure, these Google Maps listings exist on a platform that most smartphone users already have installed. This normalizes access to illicit drugs and makes enforcement more complex, as the platform itself is not designed to distinguish between a legitimate business and a drug dealing operation. Law enforcement agencies face the challenge of monitoring a constantly shifting landscape of listings that can be created and removed within hours.
