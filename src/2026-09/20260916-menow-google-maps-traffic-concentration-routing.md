# Google Maps Tests New Approach to Prevent Everyone Taking the Same Route
**Source**: https://www.menow.fr/google-maps-teste-une-nouvelle-approche-pour-eviter-que-tout-le-monde-prenne-la-meme-route/
**Date**: 2026-09-16
**Author**: Eric Thomas — Menow.fr
**Keywords**: Google Maps, traffic routing, congestion, route optimization, network-level optimization, fuel consumption

## Elevator pitch
Google Maps is testing a system that deliberately sends some drivers on different routes to optimize overall network traffic flow rather than individual trip time — early trials show a 2% increase in average speed and 1% reduction in fuel consumption by modifying only 2% of trips.

## Takeaways
- Google Maps is experimenting with routing that optimizes global traffic flow rather than each driver's individual fastest path
- The system selectively redirects only some drivers to alternate routes, preventing secondary roads from becoming saturated when a main road has an incident
- Trials in major American cities modified only about 2% of trips but achieved ~2% increase in average speed and ~1% reduction in fuel consumption
- Current GPS apps share a weakness: when an accident or congestion blocks a main road, thousands of drivers receive the same detour, simply moving the problem elsewhere
- The approach represents a shift from individual optimization to network-level traffic management

## Synthesis
Google Maps is testing a fundamentally different approach to navigation routing that aims to optimize overall traffic network flow rather than simply finding the fastest route for each individual driver. The current paradigm — used by virtually all GPS apps — calculates the optimal route for each user independently, but this creates a systemic weakness: when a major incident blocks a primary road, thousands of drivers receive the same alternate route suggestion, simply displacing the congestion to secondary roads.

The new experimental system, tested in several major American cities, takes a network-level perspective. Rather than automatically sending all drivers toward the same optimal alternative, Google's algorithm selectively redirects only certain drivers to different routes. The goal is to prevent secondary roads from becoming overwhelmed the moment a main artery encounters a problem.

The results are notable for their efficiency: by modifying only about 2% of analyzed trips, the system achieved approximately a 2% increase in average speed on studied road segments and about a 1% reduction in fuel consumption. The principle relies on very targeted interventions — redirecting a small number of drivers to prevent too many vehicles from converging at the same place simultaneously, effectively distributing traffic more intelligently across the available road network.
