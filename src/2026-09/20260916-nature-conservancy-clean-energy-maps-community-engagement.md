# Maps Are a Starting Point for Responsible Clean Energy Development
**Source**: https://www.nature.org/en-us/what-we-do/our-insights/perspectives/clean-energy-map-data-community-engagement/
**Date**: 2026-09-16
**Author**: Maya Batres — The Nature Conservancy
**Keywords**: clean energy, spatial data, mapping tools, community engagement, renewable energy siting, Clean Energy Compass, environmental justice, GIS

## Elevator pitch
The Nature Conservancy argues that spatial data mapping tools like its Clean Energy Compass are valuable for early-stage renewable energy siting, but relationship-driven community engagement — not data-driven siting — remains the strongest predictor of project success.

## Takeaways
- The Nature Conservancy's Clean Energy Compass is an interactive mapping tool for early clean energy planning that incorporates conservation and community considerations
- Mapping tools now include socioeconomic and environmental vulnerability indicators (income, pollution exposure, health burdens, energy affordability) to identify community-level impacts
- Maps can highlight opportunities like brownfields and landfills for renewable energy redevelopment, aligning clean energy goals with land restoration
- Despite their value, maps have limitations: spatial datasets lack local detail, cannot capture community experiences, and may reinforce inequities if used without care
- Early, meaningful community engagement is the strongest predictor of renewable energy project success and long-term acceptance
- Relationship-driven planning, not data-driven siting, is the key to accelerating the clean energy transition

## Synthesis
The Nature Conservancy (TNC) has published a perspective arguing that while mapping tools are valuable for early-stage renewable energy siting, they cannot substitute for meaningful community engagement. The article highlights TNC's Clean Energy Compass, an interactive mapping tool that helps decision-makers identify potential conservation and community considerations early in the clean energy planning process.

Modern mapping tools increasingly incorporate socioeconomic and environmental vulnerability indicators — including income, pollution exposure, health burdens, and energy affordability — to help reflect where communities may face cumulative impacts. These tools can also identify opportunities for community benefit, such as mapping brownfields, landfills, and previously disturbed lands that could be redeveloped into renewable energy projects.

However, TNC's own research and experience demonstrate clear limitations. Spatial datasets often lack important local detail and cannot capture a community's experiences with infrastructure development, the cultural or economic significance of places, or changing local conditions. Maps can also unintentionally reinforce inequities if data is interpreted in ways that overlook community priorities or used as a substitute for direct engagement.

The article cites research by Nilson et al. (2024) from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory showing that early, meaningful engagement is one of the strongest predictors of renewable energy project success and long-term acceptance. The conclusion is that mapping tools can help give direction, but only partnership with communities will create durable, locally supported clean energy projects.
