# Amateur Astronomer Discovers 390-Million-Year-Old Crater on Google Maps

**Source**: https://www.futura-sciences.com/en/he-was-picking-a-campsite-on-google-maps-when-he-noticed-a-circle-it-turned-out-to-be-a-15-mile-crater-nobody-had-recorded-g1_900175/
**Date**: 2026-09-17
**Author**: Futura Sciences
**Keywords**: Google Maps, crater, meteorite, Quebec, impact structure, satellite imagery, shock metamorphism, planetary geology, discovery

## Elevator pitch
An amateur astronomer planning a camping trip on Google Maps spotted a near-perfect 15-mile ring in Quebec's Côte-Nord region that turned out to be a previously unrecorded 390-million-year-old meteorite impact crater — the first confirmed crater discovery by a member of the public.

## Takeaways
- Joël Lapointe discovered the ring-shaped feature around Lake Marsal while browsing satellite maps for a camping trip in Quebec's Côte-Nord region in 2024
- The crater, approximately 25 km in diameter, dates to the Devonian period (~390 million years ago) — over 100 million years before the first dinosaurs
- Planetary geologist Gordon Osinski of Western University led a 2025 field expedition that confirmed the impact origin through shatter cones and impact melt rock
- The team named the crater "Uhackatik" in consultation with the Innu Council of Ekuanitshit, on whose traditional land it lies
- This is reportedly the first time a public sighting reported through the Impact Earth database has been confirmed as an impact crater
- NASA's Earth Observatory published a Landsat 8 view of the structure, with the circular outline faintly visible around the lake
- Geologists estimate hundreds more impact craters remain unidentified on Earth, many under sediment, ice, or ocean

## Synthesis
In 2024, amateur astronomer Joël Lapointe was scrolling through Google Maps satellite imagery looking for camping trails in Quebec's Côte-Nord region when he noticed a near-perfect ring, roughly 15 miles across, around Lake Marsal. Recognizing the shape but assuming he must be wrong, Lapointe checked crater databases and found nothing — the feature was unrecorded.

He reported his finding to the Impact Earth database run by Gordon Osinski, a planetary geologist at Western University in Ontario. Circular features are common in nature — volcanic calderas, collapsed salt domes, eroded intrusions, and glacial lakes can all produce rings — so satellite imagery alone cannot confirm an impact. What settles it is evidence of shock: the immense pressures of an asteroid impact leave physical signatures in rock that cannot form through any other geological process.

In 2025, Osinski and three colleagues, including Jérôme Gattacceca from the European Centre for Research and Teaching in Environmental Geosciences in France, flew to the site by float plane. The shoreline was too overgrown for landing, requiring the team to wade over 160 feet through water carrying their equipment. On the second day, they found shatter cones — grooved, conical fracture patterns that form only when a shock wave passes through rock — and cliffs of impact melt rock. A conference abstract describes roughly 50 metres of impact melt rock preserved at the site.

The crater dates to the Devonian period, approximately 390 million years ago. The team named it Uhackatik in consultation with the Innu Council of Ekuanitshit. Osinski noted that this is the first time a sighting reported by a member of the public has been confirmed as an impact crater — geologists had previously examined the unusual rocks around Lake Marsal but had a different explanation. The ring had been visible in satellite imagery the entire time.

NASA's Earth Observatory published a Landsat 8 view of the structure, highlighting how satellite imagery — and platforms like Google Maps that make it accessible — continue to enable serendipitous geological discoveries.
