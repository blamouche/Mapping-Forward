# A fairer world map – but old borders creep back in

**Source**: https://www.lowyinstitute.org/the-interpreter/a-fairer-world-map-but-old-borders-creep-back-in
**Date**: 2026-09-18
**Author**: Abhinandan Kumar / Lowy Institute
**Keywords**: UN, Equal Earth, Mercator, Correct the Map, cartography, sovereignty, borders, Africa, projections, geopolitics

## Elevator pitch
The Lowy Institute analyzes how the UN's "Correct the Map" resolution, intended to replace the Mercator projection with equal-area alternatives, has become entangled in sovereignty disputes as countries object to how the new maps depict contested territories.

## Takeaways
- The UN General Assembly voted 164-1 in favor of the "Correct the Map" resolution, with the United States the sole opponent and six abstentions (Ukraine, Moldova, Estonia, Georgia, Lithuania, Serbia)
- The resolution encourages governments, schools, and tech companies to adopt equal-area projections like Equal Earth that show accurate relative sizes of landmasses
- Ukraine abstained because the Equal Earth map depicts Crimea as part of Russian territory; Moldova abstained in solidarity with Ukraine
- India and Japan voted in favor but subsequently raised concerns about how the map depicts Jammu & Kashmir, Ladakh, Arunachal Pradesh, and the Kuril Islands respectively
- The US argued the resolution is part of a "radical ideological project" and questioned why the UN was devoting attention to a 16th-century map
- The resolution is non-binding, and the UN's own emblem uses an azimuthal equidistant projection centered on the North Pole, not an equal-area projection

## Synthesis
The Lowy Institute's Abhinandan Kumar provides a geopolitical analysis of the UN's "Correct the Map" resolution that goes beyond the cartographic debate to examine how map projections have become proxy battles for sovereignty and historical claims.

The resolution, introduced by Togo and backed by the African Union, passed with 164 votes in favor. It encourages the gradual replacement of the Mercator projection — which dramatically enlarges landmasses away from the equator, making Greenland appear comparable to Africa despite being 7.1% of its size — with equal-area projections like Equal Earth.

However, Kumar identifies three distinct political claims emerging from the debate. The first, from African states, frames the issue as cognitive justice: maps shape how people imagine the relative importance of different parts of the world. France appeared to accept this reasoning, announcing on the same day as the UN vote that it would abandon Mercator for diplomatic uses in favor of the Eckert IV projection.

The second claim involves sovereignty. Ukraine abstained not on principle but because the Equal Earth map shows Crimea in the same color as Russian territory. Moldova followed in solidarity. India and Japan voted in favor but raised concerns about contested territories — India insisting that Jammu & Kashmir, Ladakh, and Arunachal Pradesh must match its official map, and Japan objecting to the Kuril Islands appearing as Russian territory.

The third claim comes from the United States, the sole opponent, which argued the resolution is part of a broader "radical ideological project" and questioned the UN's credibility for addressing a 16th-century map.

Kumar notes a final irony: the UN's own emblem uses an azimuthal equidistant projection centered on the North Pole, not an equal-area projection. If maps shape how we see the world, the UN may need to lead by example and reconsider its own emblem.
