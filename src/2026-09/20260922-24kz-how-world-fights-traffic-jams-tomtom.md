## Synthesis
Kazakh news outlet 24KZ provides a global survey of how different cities are tackling traffic congestion, drawing on TomTom's geolocation data. The report profiles four very different approaches from Mexico, China, South Korea, and the United States.

**Mexico City**, which ranks as the world's most congested city with nearly 76% road occupancy and drivers losing about 184 hours per year in traffic, is turning to aerial transport. The city is building three new cable car lines totaling nearly 40 kilometers. One line will connect the large district of Tlalpan to the university campus, where the current 70-minute car or bus commute is expected to be nearly halved. The 11-kilometer line will have nine stations and a capacity of about 65,000 passengers per day, providing residents of remote districts direct access to the metro. Completion is planned for December 2027.

**China** is deploying robots to manage school-related traffic. In the eastern province of Zhejiang, authorities installed 18 mobile lane dividers on a 100-meter stretch of road near schools. During peak hours, the robots shift to temporarily convert one left-turn lane into two, allowing more cars to pass through intersections per traffic light cycle. Police control the system and adjust lane configurations based on real-time road conditions.

**South Korea** is using artificial intelligence to regulate traffic in Incheon. The system analyzes traffic flow and optimizes the duration of green and red signals at intersections. In 2026, the technology was deployed on 25 major roads. On one of the most congested 6-kilometer sections, average speed increased by nearly 30% and delay time was halved — from four and a half minutes to two minutes.

**New York City** is reducing the number of cars in its center through congestion pricing. Since January 2025, drivers pay a base fare of $9 per trip into the most congested part of Manhattan. In the first year, vehicle traffic decreased by 11%, with 27 million fewer cars entering the zone. Average speed on major entrances during morning hours increased by 23%, and on some routes by more than half. The system generated over $550 million in net revenue, which is being directed toward modernizing the subway and other public transit.

Overall, TomTom's data shows that the global congestion indicator increased by an average of 5 percentage points over the past year, underscoring the urgency of these diverse urban mobility solutions.
