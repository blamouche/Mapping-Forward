## Synthesis
Bicycling.nl uses TomTom Traffic Index data to answer a question many urban dwellers have wondered about: how much faster is a car compared to a bicycle during rush hour? The answer, at least in Amsterdam, is surprising: not faster at all.

According to the TomTom Traffic Index, Amsterdam is the slowest car city in the Netherlands. Motorists average less than 23 km/h, largely because the speed limit on many roads is 30 km/h. In Den Haag the average is over 25 km/h, and in Nijmegen over 28 km/h. In the evening rush hour, a 10-kilometer drive in Amsterdam takes an average of 27 minutes — about 22 km/h. For comparison, a city bike travels at 15–18 km/h, an e-bike at 20–25 km/h, and a race bike at 23–27 km/h in city traffic. In other words, in Amsterdam's evening rush hour, a car travels at the same speed as an e-bike.

The article provides a detailed comparison table for 5-km and 10-km trips across different modes of transport. A race bike emerges as the fastest option in rush-hour traffic, even before parking time is considered. The aero bar position makes no difference in the city, since there's never enough straight road to benefit, and not having hands on the brakes in dense traffic is dangerous.

What the table doesn't capture is where the car really loses: arriving. A cyclist can almost always park right at the door, while a motorist must find a spot, pay, and walk. For distances under 5 km in the city, the bicycle is virtually always faster once parking is accounted for. The CBS had already found that on distances up to 5 km, cyclists are nearly as fast as motorists — and that was before Amsterdam massively adopted 30 km/h limits.

The crossover point depends on the city, the route, and whether it's rush hour. In Nijmegen, where cars average over 28 km/h, an e-bike is slower over 10 km. On the highway without traffic, there's no contest. But in a 30 km/h city during evening rush hour, the e-bike matches the car and the race bike beats it.

Beyond speed, there's the time calculation that doesn't appear in travel time: an Amsterdam car commuter lost 97 hours to traffic in 2025 according to TomTom — more than four full days. A cyclist uses that same time to move. A University of Utrecht study found that cycling 75 minutes per week extends life by about half a year. The motorist who arrives home at the same time has spent the time sitting still; the cyclist has spent it moving.
