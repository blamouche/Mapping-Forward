# Behind the paper: South African model maps Ebola spillover risks

**Source**: https://www.nature.com/articles/d44148-026-00296-5
**Date**: 2026-09-24
**Author**: Nature Africa
**Keywords**: Ebola, spillover risk, habitat suitability models, ecological modeling, anthropogenic factors, DRC, zoonotic transmission, GIS, spatial epidemiology

## Elevator pitch
Researchers from South Africa, DRC, UK, and US developed a model that integrates ecological and anthropogenic factors—including mining, forest loss, and human displacement—to better predict where Ebola spillovers from animals to humans may occur, improving on traditional habitat suitability models.

## Takeaways
- Traditional habitat suitability models using temperature, rainfall, and land cover fail to capture human activity drivers of Ebola spillover
- The 2026 Bundibugyo virus outbreak in DRC's Ituri province emerged in a conflict zone with food insecurity and displacement
- Researchers integrated mining, forest loss, and other human pressures into ecological models to improve spillover predictions
- The preprint "Integrating ecological and anthropogenic risk identifies emerging Ebola spillover hotspots" presents the findings
- Four orthoebolaviruses have caused human illness: Ebola virus, Sudan virus, Taï Forest virus, and Bundibugyo virus
- The international team included researchers from CERI at Stellenbosch University, DRC, UK, and US

## Synthesis
The 2026 Ebola outbreak caused by the Bundibugyo virus in the Democratic Republic of the Congo brought renewed urgency to predicting where spillovers may occur. Traditional habitat suitability models use environmental factors like temperature, rainfall, and land cover to identify places where conditions could support viruses or their animal hosts. However, the 2026 outbreak emerged in a region affected by conflict, food insecurity, and large-scale displacement—factors that habitat models don't capture.

People escaping violence may depend more heavily on forests for food and income through subsistence hunting and artisanal mining, bringing communities into closer contact with wildlife and disturbed habitats. Monika Moir, a researcher at the Centre for Epidemic Response and Innovation at Stellenbosch University, and colleagues from the DRC, UK, and US developed a model that incorporates human activity alongside environmental factors to provide a clearer picture of where orthoebolavirus spillover may occur.

Their preprint brings together environmental and sociodemographic factors associated with previous spillover events, representing a significant evolution in spatial epidemiology modeling for Ebola risk assessment.
