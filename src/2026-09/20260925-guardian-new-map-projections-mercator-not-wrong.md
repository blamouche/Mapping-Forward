# New map projections don't make the Mercator wrong

**Source**: https://www.theguardian.com/travel/2026/sep/25/new-map-projections-dont-make-the-mercator-wrong
**Date**: 2026-09-25
**Author**: Paul Harwood (The Guardian)
**Keywords**: map projections, Mercator, Equal Earth, cartography, bias, UTM, Ordnance Survey

## Elevator pitch
A letter to The Guardian defends the Mercator projection against charges of inherent bias, arguing it was fit for its original purpose and that its misuse reflects user laziness rather than cartographic prejudice.

## Takeaways
- The transverse Mercator projection was created for European sailors and served that purpose effectively — its continued universal use is the fault of later users, not its creators
- The Equal Earth projection is better suited to modern global representations, but that does not invalidate the Mercator for the tasks it was designed to accomplish
- The claim of a "London-centric worldview" in maps is overstated: the international coordinate system centres on a point off west Africa, and UTM uses 60 different zone centres
- Even the UK Ordnance Survey is not centred on London — its effective centre is in Lancashire
- Map projections are tools, not political statements; choosing the right one depends on the use case, not on ideological correctness

## Synthesis
Paul Harwood's letter responds to a earlier Guardian feature about cartographers challenging centuries of western bias in map projections. He argues that the Mercator projection, while unsuitable for modern global displays, was a rational solution for the sailors it was designed to serve. The distinction matters: the projection itself is not biased, but the decision to use it universally — long after its intended scope — reflects inertia rather than intent.

Harwood challenges two specific claims from the original article. First, the notion that maps are "London-centric" is technically inaccurate: the Greenwich meridian defines a coordinate system whose centre is in the Atlantic, not in London, and no map is required to use it as a visual centre. Second, he points out that the Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) system — the current best practice for many applications — divides the globe into 60 zones, each with its own centre, making the idea of a single dominant centre moot. The UK's own Ordnance Survey, far from being London-centric, is effectively centred in Lancashire.

The letter is a nuanced contribution to the ongoing debate about cartographic representation. It does not dismiss the value of alternatives like Equal Earth but cautions against framing the discussion as a moral failing of historical cartographers. Projections are mathematical tools with trade-offs; the responsibility lies with those who choose which tool to apply to which problem. This perspective adds balance to a conversation that often polarises between "old = biased" and "new = correct" without acknowledging the technical context that governs projection selection.
