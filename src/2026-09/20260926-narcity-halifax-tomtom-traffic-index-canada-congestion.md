## Synthesis
Halifax's city centre ranked third worst in Canada for traffic congestion in 2025, behind only Vancouver and Toronto, according to the annual TomTom Traffic Index. Drivers in the Halifax Regional Municipality (HRM) lost 111 hours to rush hour last year, more than commuters in Toronto, who lost 100 hours in a city with a metro population close to 14 times its size.

The core problem is geographic: unless aboard a ferry, every commuter from Dartmouth to Halifax funnels across the MacDonald or MacKay bridge. Development in Dartmouth South has been fast-tracked to keep up with housing demand, but road infrastructure has not kept pace. On Pleasant Street, average daily traffic climbed 8 percent between December 2021 and May 2025, from 10,987 to 11,828 vehicles per day. Traffic counts on Mount Hope Avenue near Baker Drive rose nearly 5 percent in a year.

Claudia Chender, MLA for Dartmouth South, says housing approvals are outpacing infrastructure, a problem made more acute by Special Planning Areas (SPAs) that let Nova Scotia's housing minister bypass municipal zoning and public hearings. Nearly 1,800 residential units were approved for Mount Hope Village and Opal Ridge through the SPA process, with another 414 units along the Pleasant Street corridor.

HRM's open data portal records 518 collisions on Pleasant Street between 2018 and 2026, resulting in three fatalities. Halifax Transit is also struggling: Route 5, which carries riders across the MacDonald Bridge through downtown Dartmouth, saw passenger overload incidents more than triple from 17 in 2022 to 57 in 2024. The proposed $70-million Halifax Transit Core Service Plan would change 37 percent of bus routes, with further integration of bus rapid transit lines expected in 2028.
