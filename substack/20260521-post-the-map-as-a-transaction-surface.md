# The Map as a Transaction Surface

*Google wants to sell you coffee while you drive. The EU wants to break Google's mapping monopoly. TomTom wants to be your car's operating system. India's coastal communities want to be visible on the maps that determine their survival. And HERE just launched something called "Location Reasoning" because AI agents can't tell where things actually are. Welcome to maps in late May 2026.*

In the same seven-day window, Google I/O 2026 confirmed that Gemini is now the connective tissue across every Google product — 900 million monthly users, 3.2 quadrillion tokens processed, Maps receiving its biggest update in a decade — while Android Auto users reported that Google Maps had begun displaying pop-up ads for Dunkin' Donuts during active navigation, occupying nearly half the screen and proposing to reroute drivers to the advertised location. One user described being subtly redirected from an airport route toward a coffee shop without immediately realising it.

These two events are not contradictory. They are the same strategy viewed from different angles. Google is transforming Maps from a navigation utility into a transaction surface — a platform where every interaction, every query, every route is a potential commercial event. Ask Maps, the flagship feature of the I/O keynote, doesn't just answer questions about places; it recommends 3-8 businesses per query with specific explanations of why each fits. Google Earth, the new enterprise platform announced the same week, lets businesses extract property assessments, infrastructure conditions, and environmental changes from satellite imagery at scale. And Universal Cart — a cross-service shopping basket spanning Search, YouTube, Gmail, and Maps — closes the loop from discovery to purchase without the user ever leaving Google's ecosystem.

The pop-up ad in Android Auto is not a bug. It is the logical endpoint of this strategy: when the map itself becomes a commercial interface, the line between helping you navigate and selling you something dissolves. The user who was driving to the airport and got offered a coffee detour wasn't experiencing a product failure. They were experiencing the product working exactly as designed — for the advertiser.

This transformation is happening at the same moment that regulators are discovering how hard it is to create competition in mapping. New research published in ProMarket by economists from Télécom Paris and HEC Paris provides the most rigorous empirical test yet of the EU's Digital Markets Act approach to mapping. After the DMA forced Google to remove clickable Maps shortcuts from search results in January 2024, the researchers found that users simply typed "google maps" instead — searches for the term rose 21%, and organic traffic to Google Maps surged 33-40%. Bing Maps, Mappy, and OpenStreetMap saw no meaningful gains. In France, where Mappy actually had higher pre-DMA search volume than Google Maps, Mappy searches *declined* after the redesign and Google Maps overtook it. Removing a self-preferencing shortcut didn't redirect demand because mapping services aren't interchangeable — data coverage, interface familiarity, and feature quality matter far more than one click. The authors propose choice screens as the more effective remedy, but the deeper message is uncomfortable: network effects in mapping are so strong that removing defaults is like removing one door from a building and expecting people to walk to a different building entirely.

The French government is taking a different approach — not trying to create competition, but directly shaping what the dominant platforms do. Under decree n°2022-1199, Waze and Google Maps must now prioritise the least polluting routes over the fastest ones, and on highways with speed limits above 110 km/h, they must offer an alternative involving a 20 km/h speed reduction. Users can still manually select faster routes, but eco-routes are the default. The Île-de-France region had already pushed Google Maps toward alternative routing ahead of the Paris 2024 Olympics, and the region's mobility director went so far as to warn that the State could demand app shutdowns if platforms refused to comply. This is sovereign power reshaping routing logic — not through competition policy but through direct operational mandates.

The tension between the map as public infrastructure and the map as private platform runs through almost every story from this period. The British Geological Survey published high-resolution geological maps of Berwick-upon-Tweed to future-proof the region's water supply through better groundwater understanding — quiet, unglamorous public mapping that will shape infrastructure decisions for decades. The USGS released a library of flood-inundation maps for Lee's Summit, Missouri, covering 240 precipitation scenarios tied to real-time weather station data, designed explicitly for emergency evacuations and road closures. India's coastal communities in Puducherry and Karaikal discovered that the government's 1:25,000 scale Coastal Regulation Zone maps systematically excluded the fish-drying centres, boat-parking areas, and mangrove patches they depend on for survival — while the finer 1:4,000 maps that could show these features were prepared without community consultation. Community-led ground truthing with GIS experts found that satellite-derived data missed 70-90% of eco-sensitive areas. The map's scale is not a technical detail; at 1:25,000, a community's livelihood infrastructure simply vanishes from the official record.

HERE Technologies' announcement of Location Reasoning represents a third model entirely: enterprise infrastructure that deliberately avoids the consumer platform game. The product is a deterministic spatial computation layer for AI agents — when an LLM needs to find an EV charger within five minutes of a route or determine if a truck can safely navigate a turn, Location Reasoning takes over, delivering the same answer every time without burning tokens on probabilistic guesswork. HERE's data covers 68 million kilometres of roads across 200 countries, powers 238 million vehicles, and the company explicitly positions this as "the missing execution layer" for agentic AI. It's not a consumer product. It's plumbing for autonomous systems.

TomTom, meanwhile, is executing its own version of the pivot from consumer to enterprise. Chief Product Officer Léo Sei explained on BFM Business that AI is now at the core of TomTom's next-generation mapping platform — not for better turn-by-turn directions, but for the sensor-fusion data layers that ADAS and autonomous driving require. TomTom's Traffic Index 2025, released the same week, illustrates the kind of intelligence this enables: 3.65 trillion kilometres of driving data across 500 cities reveals patterns invisible to any individual observer. Kumamoto, Japan, is now the country's most congested city at 56.7%, directly linked to TSMC's new semiconductor fabrication plant creating intense localized traffic with limited public transit alternatives. A single factory reshaped a city's mobility profile. New York, by contrast, improved 1% — congestion pricing and bike lanes are measurable in aggregate data. These are not just statistics; they are operational inputs for city planners, logistics companies, and automakers building the next generation of vehicles.

The in-car experience is where all these threads converge, and the competitive landscape is about to be reshaped dramatically. iOS 26.4 forced Apple to open CarPlay to third-party voice assistants, ending Siri's monopoly. Code discovered in Google Maps for iOS indicates Gemini is being prepared for CarPlay integration — meaning drivers will soon be able to have natural conversations with their navigation system, asking it to find fast chargers compatible with their car, summarize restaurant reviews, or reroute around developing traffic. The potential is enormous. So is the concern. Engaging in conversational AI while driving at highway speeds is not the same as issuing a terse voice command, and the richer the interaction, the more cognitive bandwidth is diverted from the road. The data privacy implications are equally severe: predictive navigation that "anticipates your desires" requires continuous collection of driving patterns, location history, and consumption preferences — data that automakers have already been caught selling to insurance brokers.

What emerges from these fifteen stories, spanning consumer apps, enterprise platforms, regulatory interventions, scientific mapping, and automotive technology, is a picture of the map being pulled in fundamentally incompatible directions. It is simultaneously being asked to be: a commercial transaction surface that monetizes every interaction; a public infrastructure that serves community and environmental needs; a regulatory instrument subject to sovereign mandates about routing and representation; a precision data layer for autonomous systems that cannot tolerate probabilistic answers; and a conversational AI companion that anticipates desires before they're expressed.

These roles conflict. A map that sells you coffee while you drive is not the same map that helps a coastal community prove its fish-drying centre exists. A map optimized for advertiser conversion is not the same map that gives emergency managers flood extent tied to live rainfall. A routing algorithm that minimizes emissions is not the same one that minimizes travel time. And a navigation system that collects everything about your movements to anticipate your needs is not the same one that protects your privacy.

The mapping industry of 2026 is not converging on a single vision. It is splitting into parallel realities, each with its own incentives, its own definitions of accuracy, its own winners and losers. The question is not which vision will win. The question is whether users, citizens, and regulators can keep them all legible — can understand which version of the map they're looking at, and what it's been optimized to do to them.
