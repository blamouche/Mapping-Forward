# The map is no longer just a mirror

*From underground fungal networks to shade-seeking pedestrians, modern mapping reveals systems we never thought to look for — and makes claims we never thought to question*

In July 2026, an international team of researchers published the first complete global map of the underground mycorrhizal fungal network in the journal Science. The network of fungal filaments, which forms symbiotic relationships with plant roots across the planet, stretches an estimated 110 quadrillion kilometers — nearly a billion times the distance between Earth and the Sun. For centuries, cartographers mapped coastlines, borders, and roads. One of the most consequential maps of 2026 depicts something none of them could have imagined: a living system beneath our feet that regulates carbon sequestration, soil health, and drought resilience across every continent.

The same month, the Geological Survey of Victoria completed a four-year LiDAR survey that increased the official count of historic mining features in the Australian state from 30,000 to an estimated 230,000. A light aircraft equipped with laser sensors flew over the Golden Plains, Greater Melbourne, the Central Goldfields, and the High Country, bouncing laser beams off the ground to create three-dimensional point clouds. Software stripped away trees and buildings to reveal the bare earth below, where sinks and divots marked mine shafts, surface workings, and mullock heaps from 175 years of gold rush activity. The automated detection program achieved 90% accuracy. The ground had been hiding seven times more mining features than anyone had recorded.

These two mapping projects, one planetary and one regional, share something that would have seemed foreign to earlier generations of cartographers. They do not document what is visible. They reveal what is not. And that shift — from documenting the seen to uncovering the unseen — is changing what maps are for.

## The invisible made visible

The same impulse runs through mapping projects that address very different subjects. Fortune's tax escape map, published in August, traces how billionaires relocated from California and Washington to Florida, taking approximately $29 billion in tax revenue with them. The migration is concentrated in specific geographies — Indian Creek Island, Palm Beach, Coconut Grove — and the map of that concentration makes an abstract fiscal policy debate physically tangible. Larry Page and Sergey Brin left California before the January 1, 2026 deadline for the state's proposed billionaire wealth tax. Mark Zuckerberg, Jeff Bezos, and Larry Ellison followed. The map does not argue for or against wealth taxes. It shows where the money went, and that visibility itself constitutes an argument.

In York, England, researchers at the University of York attached sixteen air quality monitors to bicycles and sent citizen scientists cycling through their daily commutes. The resulting York Air Map reveals how pollution exposure varies over distances as short as a few meters, depending on traffic, road works, and construction. Fixed monitoring stations provide accurate data but only at fixed points. The bicycle-mounted sensors filled the spatial gaps, and the project translated the data into maps, a zine, public exhibitions, and smellable air filters that let visitors guess pollution sources by scent. The map made an invisible health threat tangible — not through abstraction but through participation.

When two earthquakes struck Venezuela on June 24, 2026 — thirty-nine seconds apart, killing over 2,200 people — the Associated Press built three complementary maps within days. One showed seismic intensity. A second used building footprint data from the Overture Maps Foundation to estimate structural exposure. A third visualized displacement patterns. The maps combined OpenStreetMap data with Overture's machine-derived building footprints, filling gaps where volunteer mapping was sparse, and were published on the Humanitarian Data Exchange for responders to use. Crisis cartography that once took weeks to assemble was available in days, built on open data ecosystems that did not exist a decade ago.

## From showing to knowing

The technical infrastructure behind this expansion is changing fast. Thailand's Geo-Informatics and Space Technology Development Agency (GISTDA) has laid out a four-stage national roadmap running through 2031: data foundation, open geo-informatics data, GeoAI, and Digital Twin. The roadmap treats geo-informatics as national data infrastructure rather than a mapping exercise. GISTDA is already piloting GeoAI in Sukhothai province for water management and disaster response, moving from data display to AI-supported prediction. The Thai National Geo-Informatics Committee approved the operational plan in August 2026, positioning the country among a growing number of governments that treat spatial data as strategic infrastructure.

The private sector is moving in parallel. GIS Cloud launched generative AI capabilities that let users describe what they want in natural language — "Which district contains the highest number of assets?" — and the system translates the request into spatial joins and SQL queries. The tool integrates with Anthropic's Claude through the Model Context Protocol, blurring the line between conversational AI and professional GIS software. A human-in-the-loop design requires users to confirm every AI-suggested change before it is applied, addressing the trust problem that has slowed enterprise AI adoption. CEO Dino Ravnic positions this as the next step in democratizing GIS, lowering the technical barrier to spatial analysis for non-specialists.

At the consumer level, maps are becoming context-aware in ways that go beyond finding the shortest path. Google Maps is developing a "prefer shade" option for pedestrian routing, discovered in beta code by Android Authority. The feature would prioritize streets with minimal sun exposure, accounting for building heights, tree canopy, time of day, and season. A street shaded in the morning can be fully exposed two hours later, making reliable shade routing a technically demanding problem. The feature responds to intensifying heat waves and their health risks for vulnerable pedestrians, following the lead of the French app Fraichoù, which already offers "cool" walking routes using tree cover data. Navigation is no longer just about distance and time. It is about comfort, health, and environmental conditions.

Google Maps' fuel-efficient routing tells a related story about contextual intelligence. The feature, rolled out in 2022 and later made the default for all users, calculates routes based on engine type — gas, diesel, hybrid, or electric — and terrain. The problem, as How-To Geek reports, is that most users never configure their engine type, leaving the app to apply generic assumptions that may produce routes that are slower and less efficient for their actual vehicle. A well-intentioned default becomes a source of friction when users do not understand what the algorithm optimizes for. The map is making decisions on behalf of millions of drivers who do not realize those decisions are being made.

The push for privacy and offline capability offers a counterpoint to the AI-driven direction. Organic Maps, a free open-source navigation app built on OpenStreetMap data, delivers turn-by-turn routing without cellular signal or subscription. The Exodus Privacy Project verified that the app collects no location data or tracking history. It does not offer live traffic, real-time transit, or ride-hailing — it is an offline-first tool for road trips, hikes, and international travel. A 2025 governance dispute led to a community fork called CoMaps, and the map data license has become less open than before. But the app fills a gap that commercial navigation services have no incentive to fill: reliable mapping for people who are deliberately off the grid.

## What maps claim

The same mapping technologies that reveal hidden systems can also be used to obscure or assert. When Donald Trump posted a map on Truth Social labeling the Strait of Hormuz as "new US territory," the cartography was not descriptive but declarative. The strait, through which roughly one-fifth of global oil and LNG exports once passed, has been closed since Iran shut it down on February 28. Trump's map does not change who controls the waterway. It asserts a claim, distributed instantly to millions of followers, using the authority of cartographic representation to make a territorial argument. Maps have always served as instruments of political claim-making. Social media has given that function a reach and speed that traditional printed maps never had.

Design choices in mapping carry their own tensions. The Metropolitan Transportation Commission's new Bay Area regional transit map covers 140 miles across nine counties and twenty-seven transit agencies, using a deliberate yellow-and-blue palette to make the network legible at a glance. The map omits local bus routes, replacing them with yellow splotches. It uses whimsical icons — a pumpkin for Half Moon Bay, garlic for Gilroy — to draw attention to regional attractions. Transit map historian Mark Ovenden praised it as a model for cross-agency navigation. But every simplification is also an exclusion. The map that makes regional transit comprehensible does so by deciding which routes matter and which do not. That decision is invisible to the rider who consults it.

The reliability of navigation apps remains an open question even as their capabilities expand. Android Police documented persistent Waze glitches on Android Auto — frozen screens, unresponsive touch inputs, mid-drive crashes — across multiple phone models and car brands, including Samsung, Pixel, and Toyota. The most effective fix was a complete uninstall and reinstall. The author argues that Google prioritizes Google Maps polish over Waze stability, treating Waze as a secondary app. How-To Geek's roundup of seven underused Google Maps features — parking location, intermediate stops, trip progress sharing — reveals the other side of the same problem: the gap between what these apps can do and what their users know how to do keeps widening. And the battery-saving tip of disabling satellite view in Google Maps while hiking, reported by Android MT, underscores how far these apps remain from being optimized for outdoor use. Satellite tiles drain GPU resources and cellular data faster than vector maps, and on a long trail day where charging access is limited, that difference is a safety concern.

## Looking ahead

The threads connecting these fifteen stories — from planetary fungal networks to shade-seeking pedestrians, from crisis cartography in Venezuela to billionaire tax flight in Florida — suggest that mapping is undergoing a shift in purpose. Maps were once tools for documenting what exists. They are becoming tools for discovering what is hidden and acting on that knowledge. Thailand's roadmap from data foundation to Digital Twin points toward a future where maps are not representations but simulation environments — predictive models of cities, forests, and economies that inform decisions in real time.

The challenge is epistemological rather than technical. When a map shows you a fungal network, a mining feature, or a pollution hotspot, you trust the data behind it. When it routes you through shade or along a fuel-efficient path, you trust the algorithm's assumptions about your needs. When it claims territory or reveals tax flight, you trust the cartographer's integrity. As GIS Cloud's human-in-the-loop design acknowledges, the maps that reveal the most are also the ones with the greatest capacity to mislead. The next decade of mapping will be defined less by what the technology can show than by who verifies what it claims to see.
