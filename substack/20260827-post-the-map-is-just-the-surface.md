# The map is just the surface

*From 72 ranking signals to Bluetooth beacons in tunnels, the infrastructure beneath modern maps is where the real work — and the real power — lives*

When a team of SEO researchers reverse-engineered a Google binary in August 2026, they found something that should give pause to anyone who has ever searched for a restaurant on Google Maps. The system behind Google's local search — internally called Geostore — draws on 793 data providers to build its representation of each place. The ranking system, named Oyster Rank, uses 72 named signals. Twenty-five of those signals are already deprecated, suggesting a system evolving faster than anyone outside Google can track. And the geographic layer is adaptive: search for "pharmacie" in dense Paris and the candidate area shrinks to a few blocks; search in rural France and it expands to cover kilometers. When the researchers removed geographic weighting across 5,083 queries, median result distance jumped from 6.87 kilometers to over 4,000 — yet non-geographic ranking stayed stable. Geography determines what you see, not just the order in which you see it.

This is the system that billions of people use every day without knowing it exists. The map on your phone is a surface. Beneath it sits a stack of data pipelines, ranking algorithms, sensor networks, and provider agreements that determine what appears, what disappears, and how the results are ordered. And that stack is growing more complex every month.

## The hidden layers

Consider what happens when you drive into a tunnel. GPS signals vanish. For years, navigation apps lost their position underground, continuing to issue instructions based on theoretical trajectory and hoping the driver emerged on the correct side. Google Maps on Android now offers a fix: Bluetooth Low Energy beacons embedded in tunnel walls, which the phone scans to correct its position in real time. The deployment began in early 2024 and covers nine metropolitan areas — New York, Chicago, Paris, Rio, Brussels, Oslo, Sydney, Boston, and Mexico City. Waze, Google's sister app, has used this technology since 2016. The infrastructure is physical: small transmitters bolted to tunnel ceilings, broadcasting their identity to any device willing to listen. The driver sees a blue dot that stays put. They do not see the beacon, the BLE protocol, or the years of deployment work that made it possible.

The same pattern — invisible infrastructure producing a seamless surface experience — runs through Google's "popular times" feature. When you check how busy a restaurant is on Maps, the data does not come from the restaurant. It comes from anonymized location traces collected from the subset of Android users who have enabled Location History. When enough of those users visit a venue, Google aggregates their presence into a crowd estimate. The business owner has no role. The individual user has no direct input. The feature works because millions of phones emit location signals as a side effect of other services, and Google has built the pipeline to convert those signals into derivative intelligence about the physical world.

Both of these systems — tunnel beacons and popular times — illustrate a principle that the Oyster Rank investigation makes explicit: the map is not a product. It is an output. The product is the infrastructure that feeds it. [As a previous post argued](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-map-is-no-longer-just-a-mirror), modern mapping reveals systems we never thought to look for. The infrastructure view goes further: those systems are not just revealed by maps. They are the maps.

## Building the base

The infrastructure metaphor extends beyond Google. In Wilmington, North Carolina, a 1969 vertical-lift bridge carrying 65,000 vehicles daily needed a centimeter-accurate 3D survey before its $1.1 billion replacement could enter environmental review. The North Carolina Department of Transportation prohibited lane closures, deck occupation, and interruption of marine traffic. The bridge had no shoulder for target placement. McKim & Creed, the geospatial firm that took the job, combined a Riegl VMX-2HA mobile mapping system driven at 45 mph in live traffic with a Riegl VZ-600i static scanner positioned below and above the span, then triangulated survey control from ground positions entirely off the bridge. When GNSS degraded under the steel truss, they used guardrail faces and light poles as calibration planes — an unconventional choice for mobile lidar, which typically relies on flat road surfaces. The result was centimeter-level alignment between mobile and static point clouds, validated across cross-sections. The driver crossing the bridge will never know any of this. They will simply cross a structure that was measured accurately enough to plan its replacement.

In Vietnam, Portcoast is building digital twins of ports near Ho Chi Minh City using bathymetric data, drone reality models, and live vessel tracking on a single ArcGIS platform. When a vessel collided with a berth, Portcoast simulated the entire incident using the digital twin rather than relying on scattered camera feeds. At a grain port in Vung Tau, they loaded the BIM model of a new warehouse and simulated every conceivable grain flow from vessel to storage before construction began. The twin integrates years of environmental data and underwater surveys, enabling presentations to government authorities on a single system. This is not a map you consult. It is an operational instrument that happens to be built from geographic data.

The same operational logic drives municipal GIS projects across India. Ahmedabad approved a ₹240 crore project to geo-code 2.5 million properties, create a 3D city model, and map underground utilities with Ground Penetrating Radar — all in preparation for the 2030 Commonwealth Games. Coimbatore's corporation geo-tagged 1,507 of 2,702 open space reservation sites to prevent encroachment, with drone imagery and field verification building a database that residents will soon access online. In Larimer County, Colorado, updated Flood Insurance Rate Maps incorporating new topography and hydrology data become effective November 27, 2026 — thirteen years after a devastating flood exposed the inadequacy of the previous maps. Each project treats geographic data as administrative infrastructure: the map is not the end product. Tax revenue, encroachment prevention, flood insurance requirements, and infrastructure planning are the end products. The map is the layer beneath them.

Airborne lidar is also rewriting the archaeological record. In Brazil's Acre state, laser pulses pierced the Amazon canopy to reveal between 24,000 and 30,000 monumental earthworks from a previously unknown civilization — the Aquiry — suggesting that 1.25 to 3 million people lived in the region in the first centuries AD, more densely populated than today. On the other side of the world, engineering firm Hatch completed a 5,000-kilometer digital coastline assessment in Australia, mapping flooding and erosion risk for a state government agency. 1.4 million Australian properties are already at risk of flooding, with annual event costs approaching $400 million. Both projects convert raw sensor data into geographic knowledge that changes what governments, researchers, and planners can act on.

## The craft underneath

Not all infrastructure is algorithmic. Some of it is human, and some of it is printed.

At State of the Map 2026 in Paris, T-Kartor — the Swedish cartographic agency behind Legible London, WalkNYC, and Toronto TO360 — presented its work with Île-de-France Mobilités to produce "plans de proximité": location-specific area maps for 35,000+ bus and tram stops across the Paris region. Each stop requires a unique map showing the surrounding area at a scale useful for pedestrians, with consistent styling and regular updates. The production pipeline runs from OpenStreetMap data editing through dozens of intermediate steps to print-ready files delivered to transport operators. Jungle Bus, a French OSM community group, provides local knowledge and quality control. The choice of OSM as the data backbone reflects the maturity of the open-data ecosystem for transit applications — but the choice of print reflects something else. Physical maps at transit stops remain essential for accessibility, quick reference, and users without smartphones. The infrastructure here is both digital and analog, and the printed map at the bus stop is as much a piece of infrastructure as the OSM database behind it.

At Minneapolis-St. Paul International Airport, the infrastructure is tactile. MSP became the first North American airport to install 3D-printed tactile maps with braille outside every bathroom — 146 bathrooms across two terminals, only two of which share identical floor plans. The airport's Travelers with Disabilities Advisory Committee designed the symbology from scratch because no Americans with Disabilities Act standards existed for tactile maps. Ken Rogers, president of the American Council of the Blind of Minnesota, described the result as providing independence and freedom. The maps also help sighted travelers navigate MSP's notoriously complex restrooms. This is universal design as infrastructure: the map works for everyone because it was built for the people who need it most.

John Nelson, a cartographer on Esri's ArcGIS Living Atlas team, builds what he calls "a high-resolution, constantly living and growing model of the world" from a pine woodshed in his Michigan backyard. He teaches nearly 40,000 YouTube followers how to make compelling maps, driven by the principle that "nothing teaches like teaching." His maps support government, engineering, and healthcare applications — medical systems use them for predictive health analysis. Nelson's work is a reminder that the infrastructure beneath maps includes craft, judgment, and aesthetic sensibility. The Living Atlas is not just a database. It is a curated representation of the world, and the quality of that curation determines the quality of everything built on top of it.

## Who controls the plumbing

The infrastructure question is ultimately a question of control. Google's 793 data providers, Oyster Rank's 72 signals, and the Webref layer connecting Maps to the broader web all sit inside a single company. When Google builds a Korea team to launch turn-by-turn navigation in a market where Naver Map has 29 million monthly active users and Google Maps has 9 million, the infrastructure battle is not about features. It is about data partnerships, base-map quality, and the relationships needed to source local POI and traffic information under Korean regulations. Google needs Korean infrastructure — local providers, local data, local compliance — to compete.

TomTom's partnership with Cofiroute, a division of VINCI Autoroutes, tells a parallel story. Cofiroute deployed TomTom Traffic across its French motorway network to monitor conditions in real time, manage incidents, and inform drivers through variable message signs. The traffic data feeds operational decisions made by highway operators, not just routing decisions made by individual drivers. When the infrastructure is shared between a mapping company and a highway operator, the map becomes a tool of infrastructure management itself.

Infinity Code's Online Maps v5 Unity asset pack, supporting 16+ map providers with 88+ map types, gives game developers access to the same geographic infrastructure that navigation apps use — Google Maps, Mapbox, ArcGIS, OpenStreetMap, and many others, with elevation data from HERE, Mapbox, MapTiler, and TomTom. The infrastructure is becoming accessible in some directions even as Google's 72-signal system shows how concentrated it remains in others.

The [previous post in this series](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-map-is-now-in-the-drivers-seat) examined how maps have moved from reference to agency — when navigation apps reroute cities and tribunals order GIS surveys. The infrastructure view adds a layer to that argument. Maps have agency because the infrastructure beneath them has depth. The 72 signals, the BLE beacons, the lidar surveys, the OSM pipelines, the digital twins — these are not features. They are the reason the features work. And as that infrastructure grows more complex, the gap between those who build it and those who depend on it widens. The driver crossing the Cape Fear bridge, the passenger at MSP airport, the resident checking bus times in Paris — none of them see the infrastructure. They see the surface. But the surface is only as honest, as accurate, and as inclusive as the plumbing beneath it allows.
