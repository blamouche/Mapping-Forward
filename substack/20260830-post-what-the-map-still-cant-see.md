# What the map still can't see

*From underground pipes to Himalayan flood zones to what a billion Indians think about climate, the hardest things to map are the ones that matter most*

On the morning of August 27, 2026, a landslide in the Himalayas near the Nepal–Tibet border sent a wall of water roaring down the Trishuli River valley. At least 168 people are confirmed dead. More than 1,300 are missing, including 65 American tourists and 39 Australians. The US Geological Survey registered the event as a 5.2-magnitude landslide — initially misreported as an earthquake. News organisations deployed interactive maps, verified satellite imagery, and geolocated video footage to trace the flood's path from the Gyirong border crossing through downstream communities in Nepal's Nuwakot district. Nepal's National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority relied on geospatial data to track the missing across an area spanning the Nepal–China frontier. The maps were essential. They were also, in a fundamental sense, too late. The landslide happened in minutes. The mapping caught up in hours.

This is the persistent problem of modern cartography. [As a previous post argued](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-map-is-no-longer-just-a-mirror), mapping has shifted from documenting the visible to uncovering the hidden. But some things remain stubbornly difficult to see — not because the technology lacks resolution, but because the subjects are underground, remote, political, or embedded in terrain that resists measurement. Six stories from the past week illuminate the blind spots that still exist in the world's most detailed maps, and the people building tools to close them.

## The physical blind spots

Josh Mackanic spent ten years as an engineer at Pacific Gas and Electric, where he experienced the consequences of one of cartography's most expensive blind spots. A job was shut down for three days because of an unknown pipe in an excavation. The delay cost $60,000. The problem was not that nobody had mapped the pipe. The problem was that PG&E could see its own gas and electric lines but had no visibility into the water mains, sewage pipes, and telecommunications conduits owned by other organisations sitting inches away. This fragmentation causes approximately 200,000 utility strikes every year in the United States alone — excavators hitting buried infrastructure because no single map shows what is underground.

Mackanic's response was CivilGrid, a startup that has raised $26 million in Series A funding to build what he calls "Google Maps for what's underground." The platform aggregates disparate data on utility assets, property ownership, and environmental regulations into a single mapping interface. A PG&E case study found $60 million in avoidable paving costs across 1,600 planned gas distribution projects. The subsurface mapping problem is not new, but Mackanic's insight was that the technical challenge was secondary to the relational one: collecting, sorting, and securing data from organisations that have never had reason to share it. The underground is mapped in fragments. CivilGrid's contribution is assembling the fragments into something a construction crew can actually use before they dig.

The Nepal flood exposes a different kind of physical blind spot. The Trishuli River valley is remote, steep, and prone to cascading disasters — landslides triggering floods, floods triggering more landslides. Crisis cartography deployed in the aftermath combined satellite imagery, geolocated video, and GIS data to document the destruction. But the terrain itself limits what satellites can see. Cloud cover, narrow valleys, and the sheer speed of a flash flood mean that the most critical moments — the minutes between landslide and flood wave — are almost entirely unmapped. The maps that emerge are reconstructive, not predictive. They show what happened, not what was about to happen. As climate change intensifies glacial lake outburst floods and landslide risks across the Himalayas, the gap between event and map is closing, but it has not closed.

## The social blind spots

Not all blind spots are physical. Yale's Program on Climate Change Communication released its 2026 update to the India Climate Opinion Maps, an interactive tool that visualises climate change beliefs, risk perceptions, and policy support across 34 Indian states and 634 districts. The tool draws on more than 25,000 survey responses collected between 2022 and 2026 in partnership with CVoter, using multilevel regression with post-stratification to estimate opinions at district level — accurate to approximately seven percentage points. The results reveal patterns that no physical map captures. Over 75% of Indians in every state believe global warming is happening, once they hear a definition. But fewer than half of people in Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Odisha, and West Bengal understand that it is human-caused. A stark north–south divide exists in personal experience: 94% of people in Himachal Pradesh report personally experiencing the effects of global warming, compared with 59% in Andhra Pradesh. These are not facts about geography. They are facts about perception, and the map that shows them is a different kind of cartographic object — one that represents not what is on the ground but what is in people's minds.

The methodological innovation matters. MRP combines survey data with demographic and geographic population characteristics — gender, age, caste, urbanicity, education, climate vulnerability — weighted using Indian census counts. The result is district-level resolution for opinions that no census measures. India is both the seventh most climate-vulnerable country and the third-largest greenhouse gas emitter. These opinion maps can directly inform State Action Plans on Climate Change, making the tool not just descriptive but operational. The blind spot here is not a missing pipe or an unmapped river. It is the gap between what people experience and what policy assumes they believe.

## The operational blind spots

London is not remote. It is not mountainous. It is one of the most mapped cities on Earth. Yet when TomTom traffic expert Andy Marchant analysed the postponement of robotaxi launches in the British capital, he identified the city as an exceptionally challenging environment for autonomous vehicles. London's narrow roads, changing speed limits, and evolving street layouts create a complex operational design domain for self-driving systems. The issue is not that London is unmapped. The issue is that mapping for human drivers and mapping for autonomous systems are different problems. A human driver handles a temporary road closure with a glance and a detour. An autonomous vehicle needs the closure encoded in its map, updated in real time, with enough context to understand that a temporary barrier means "stop" rather than "navigate around." Marchant calls high-precision mapping combined with live traffic and hazard information "core infrastructure for automated driving" — working alongside vehicle cameras and radar to provide context beyond their immediate line of sight. The same mapping data, he argues, will be critical for UK authorities to plan and govern robotaxi deployments. The blind spot is not geographic. It is temporal. Streets change overnight. Maps that do not change with them are dangerous for systems that cannot improvise.

Two TomTom Traffic Index stories from Peru and Germany show how traffic data reveals blind spots in urban planning. Arequipa, Peru's second-largest city, registered a 69.5% congestion level in 2025 — higher than Lima's 69.3% and ranking eighth globally. Drivers in Arequipa lost an average of 154 hours in traffic, with rush-hour speeds dropping to 16.3 km/h and congestion reaching 114.7% during the worst periods. The data raises a question that applies to rapidly growing secondary cities everywhere: population growth and vehicle ownership have outpaced infrastructure investment, and the road network is under proportionally greater strain than in larger capitals. Arequipa's congestion worsened by 1.9 percentage points from 2024, while Lima's increased by only 0.8. The map of congestion makes visible an urban planning failure that aggregate statistics obscure.

In Munich, TomTom analysed the closure of the Landshuter Allee — a road carrying over 70,000 vehicles daily on the city's Mittlerer Ring — by comparing traffic data from a weekend without closure to a weekend with the northern carriageway shut for maintenance. The impact rippled through Neuhausen-Nymphenburg, Schwabing-West, Moosach, and Milbertshofen-Am Hart. TomTom's Congestion Level metric quantified how much travel times increased in each affected neighbourhood. The analysis is a natural experiment enabled by continuous GPS-based monitoring: by comparing equivalent periods, TomTom isolated the specific effect of the roadworks from normal traffic variation. City planners can use this methodology to predict the impact of future closures and design better diversion strategies. The blind spot here is systemic — the cascading effects of a single road closure across an entire urban network are invisible without the kind of granular, city-wide traffic mapping that most cities still do not possess.

## What remains hidden

The pattern across these stories is consistent. The maps that matter most are the ones that capture what cannot be seen from above — buried infrastructure, remote disaster zones, public opinion, the edge cases of autonomous navigation, the ripple effects of urban disruption. Each story represents someone trying to close a specific blind spot. CivilGrid aggregates subsurface data. Yale's opinion maps make perception legible at district scale. TomTom's traffic data reveals secondary-city strain and closure cascades. Crisis cartographers reconstruct Himalayan flood paths from satellite and ground truth.

The blind spots are shrinking, but they are also shifting. The previous post in this series examined the infrastructure beneath maps — the 72 ranking signals, the Bluetooth beacons, the lidar surveys. [That infrastructure](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-map-is-just-the-surface) gives maps their depth. But depth is not the same as coverage. The underground remains fragmented across utility companies. Remote disaster zones remain accessible only after the fact. Opinions require survey methodology that no satellite can provide. Autonomous driving demands temporal precision that no static map can deliver. Secondary cities generate traffic data that reveals problems their governments may lack the resources to solve.

The Nepal flood is the starkest reminder. The maps that documented its path were built from satellite imagery, geolocated video, and GIS data assembled after the landslide had already swept through the valley. The US government committed $500,000 in emergency assistance. Nepal's disaster authority tracked 826 missing people across a vast, difficult terrain using geospatial tools that were indispensable and insufficient. The next frontier of mapping is not resolution or speed. It is anticipation — building systems that see the blind spot before something fills it.
