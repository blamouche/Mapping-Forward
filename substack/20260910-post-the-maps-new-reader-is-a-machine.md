# The map's new reader is a machine

*TomTom says it out loud: maps are no longer primarily for humans. The question is what happens when the audience for geographic knowledge is an algorithm.*

At its Discovery Event in Amsterdam in September 2026, TomTom's CEO Mike Schoofs said something that would have been heresy at a navigation company a decade ago. Maps, he told the audience, are no longer primarily for guiding people. They are for navigating artificial intelligence and machines. The company that spent forty years building devices to help humans find their way is repositioning itself as a spatial knowledge layer for autonomous driving systems and enterprise AI agents. The human driver is no longer the target audience. The algorithm is.

This is not a marketing pivot. TomTom has made its Orbis Maps platform accessible to AI agents through an MCP server and APIs. It has combined OpenStreetMap, Overture Maps, its own proprietary data, and vehicle sensor data into what it calls "ground truth" — a uniform, verified reference layer built from more than five billion data points. The company aims to provide the critical safety map layer for more than half of all new European cars by 2030. Its gross margins are heading toward 90 percent as it transitions from hardware to pure software. The navigation device that sat on your dashboard has become a data product whose primary consumer is not you.

## When AI writes the map and reads it too

The same week as the Discovery Event, TomTom published a case study that compounded the point. The company used Anthropic's Claude Code — an AI coding agent — to extend its Android Maps and Navigation SDK example app by building a Route Instructions Panel feature. The agent mapped nine routing instruction subtypes to icons and natural-language phrases, handled the SDK's data model, and constructed the UI. It stumbled on SDK-specific quirks: a type returning `TextWithPhonetics?` instead of `String?`, inconsistent package locations, experimental Compose APIs that compiled but crashed at runtime. But it completed the work, and TomTom published the prompt, the configuration, and the reusable skill file in a GitHub repository for anyone to reproduce.

The symmetry is worth pausing on. An AI agent wrote code for a mapping SDK that will be consumed by other AI agents accessing spatial data through APIs. The human developer at TomTom who oversaw the process became a reviewer and a debug specialist — intervening when the agent hit SDK-specific edges, documenting the pitfalls in a skill file that future agents will read. [The map is no longer just a mirror](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-map-is-no-longer-just-a-mirror) of the physical world. It is becoming a medium through which machines communicate with other machines about the physical world.

Google and NASA's JPL published a parallel example of AI as geographic knowledge producer. Their MAPL-EMIT model, trained on 3.6 million physics-simulated methane plumes, detects 50 percent more plumes than human experts analyzing the same satellite imagery. It identified over 23,000 additional plumes globally, including 24 of the 25 largest-emitting landfills on Earth. Google released the plume database on Earth Engine, with open-source models on Kaggle and GitHub. Methane has thirty times the warming potential of CO2 over a century. Finding and fixing methane sources is among the fastest and most cost-effective ways to slow near-term warming. The AI did not just find what humans could not. It found what humans could not process in time, at a scale that made manual analysis impossible.

The pattern repeats across domains. At State of the Map US 2026, reported by HackerNoon, OpenStreetMap contributors discussed AI-drafted map edits with human approval workflows — "the agent drafts, a human approves." Cities are giving their AI chatbots geographic dimensions, integrating GIS data into conversational interfaces. GIS Cloud's generative AI lets users describe spatial queries in natural language while the system translates them into SQL and spatial joins. In each case, the AI is not replacing human cartographers. It is changing where the human sits in the pipeline — from creator to reviewer, from analyst to editor.

## The collective cost of individual optimization

If AI is becoming the primary reader and writer of maps, the consequences of algorithmic routing deserve harder scrutiny. A study published in Nature Communications in September 2026 by researchers at CNR-Isti, the University of Pisa, and Scuola Normale Superiore simulated traffic in Florence, Milan, and Rome using GPS data cross-referenced with Google Maps, TomTom, Bing Maps, and Mapbox routing. The findings are uncomfortable for anyone who assumed navigation apps reduce emissions.

When adoption of navigation apps is low, they reduce CO2 emissions by finding more efficient routes. When adoption exceeds 60 to 80 percent, route diversity decreases by up to 14 percent. Vehicles concentrate on major arteries and highways. The environmental benefits "attenuate, vanish, or even reverse." In some Rome scenarios, emissions actually increased. The algorithms optimizing individual routes produce collectively harmful outcomes at scale because they route everyone to the same "optimal" path.

This is the routing equivalent of a tragedy of the commons, except the commons is managed by a handful of companies whose algorithms were designed to optimize individual outcomes. [The map is now in the driver's seat](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-map-is-now-in-the-drivers-seat) — but the driver is an algorithm serving a billion individual drivers, and no one is steering the aggregate. France's ecological routing decree, covered in [a previous post](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-decree-and-the-display), attempted to address this by mandating emission-minimizing defaults. But as that post noted, the decree's authority stops at the phone's surface. Users can override it with two taps. The Nature Communications study suggests the problem runs deeper than individual compliance: even when everyone follows the algorithm, the system can produce worse outcomes than if they had not.

TomTom's traffic data documents the symptoms. Five Colombian cities — Bogotá at 69.6 percent congestion, Medellín at 66.9 percent, Cali at 65.6 percent, Barranquilla at 62.8 percent, and Cartagena at 57.7 percent — rank among the most congested in the world. Bucharest drivers face ten-kilometer journeys taking over 45 minutes at rush hour. These are not mapping failures. They are mapping successes — the algorithms route accurately, and the result is congestion because the infrastructure cannot absorb the volume the algorithms direct toward it. The map is working. The city is not.

## The human stakes

The shift toward machine-readable maps does not eliminate the human stakes of cartography. It complicates them. Al Jazeera's data visualization of Russian and Ukrainian attacks on civilian targets maps a war that has shifted fundamentally from artillery to drones. Russian attacks on Ukrainian civilian infrastructure rose from 5 percent of all attacks in 2022 to 13 percent by August 2026. Ukrainian attacks on Russian civilian infrastructure rose from 10 percent to 27 percent. Missile interception rates fell from 24 percent in 2025 to 10.2 percent in 2026 as interceptor supplies dwindled. The maps document an escalation that no algorithm predicted and no routing optimization can mitigate. They serve a human reader who needs to understand what is happening, not a machine navigating a road network.

Iceland's decision to summon the US ambassador after President Trump posted a map on Truth Social showing Iceland under the American flag is another reminder that maps remain political instruments regardless of who reads them. The incident follows Trump's persistent territorial ambitions regarding Greenland, his earlier confusion of Greenland with Iceland, and Google Maps' renaming of Lake Ontario to "Lake America" within the United States. The Icelandic Foreign Minister called the image "totally inappropriate." A country that has been independent since 1944 and relies on NATO and a bilateral defense agreement with the US for its security does not treat cartographic claims lightly, even when they appear as captionless social media posts.

The human dimension also appears in quieter corners. Esri India launched a free Digital Atlas Program built on ArcGIS Online, giving schoolteachers interactive mapping tools to shift geography from memorization to real-world investigation. Students examine flooding through data layers rather than place names on a printed page. Frank LaFone, a geography faculty member at Fairmont State University, received the West Virginia Association of Geospatial Professionals Lifetime Achievement Award for over two decades of contributions to GIS in the state — founding WVAGP, co-founding the VerySpatial podcast, and connecting students with geospatial technology opportunities. INMO's GO3 AI glasses won an IFA Innovation Award for integrating HERE Maps into hands-free pedestrian and cycling navigation, with directions displayed directly in the glasses. The map is reaching humans through new interfaces, taught by new educators, and built by professionals whose work rarely makes headlines.

KFF's descriptive mapping of fourteen global health institutions serves yet another human reader: policymakers trying to navigate reform processes in a tightening fiscal environment. The mapping examines mandates, governance models, financing structures, and operational approaches without evaluating performance — a structured comparison intended to inform discussions about duplication, synergies, and architectural reform across organizations that collectively shape health outcomes in low- and middle-income countries.

## Looking ahead

TomTom's strategic repositioning names a shift that has been building for years. The infrastructure beneath maps — the 72 ranking signals, the Bluetooth beacons, the lidar surveys, [the plumbing that sits beneath the surface](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-map-is-just-the-surface) — was already more algorithmic than human-facing. What is new is the explicit framing. Maps are no longer built for humans who need to find their way. They are built for AI agents that need to understand and move within physical space, for autonomous vehicles that need safety-critical ground truth, and for enterprise systems that need spatial context to make decisions. The human who opens Google Maps on their phone is a secondary consumer of infrastructure designed for a different audience.

The risk is that the interests of the machine reader and the human reader diverge. The Nature Communications study shows one version of this divergence: individual route optimization producing collective environmental harm. The methane mapping model shows a more hopeful version: AI detecting what humans could not, at a scale that matters for climate policy. The war maps show a version where the human reader's needs are unchanged: understanding violence, documenting escalation, holding actors accountable. The next challenge for the mapping industry is not technical. It is alignment — ensuring that maps built for machines still serve the humans who live in the spaces those maps describe.
