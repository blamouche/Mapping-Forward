# When the map can be gamed

*From adversarial t-shirts to Nash equilibria on the highway, the systems we trust to navigate the world are showing cracks — and a coalition of open-data projects is betting that transparency is the fix.*

A man walks through a Berlin square wearing a shirt covered in psychedelic patterns. To the human eye, he is plainly visible. To the AI-assisted surveillance cameras recently installed at Kottbusser Tor, he does not exist. The shirt — an art project by Simon Weckert, the same Berlin-based artist who six years earlier fooled Google Maps into showing a phantom traffic jam by dragging a cartful of 99 smartphones through the streets — exploits a known weakness in object detection algorithms. The pattern is iteratively modified until the AI's confidence that a person is present drops below its detection threshold. The cameras, set to activate after Berlin's September 20 election, will see a crowd but not the shirt. Weckert calls it "Digital Camouflage."

The project is small in scale and pointed in intent. It is also a useful lens for examining a broader pattern that surfaced repeatedly in this week's mapping news: the systems millions of people rely on for navigation, spatial intelligence, and geographic knowledge are more fragile — and more gameable — than their ubiquity suggests. And the responses to that fragility are pulling in two directions. One doubles down on proprietary control and AI-driven optimization. The other opens the data up entirely.

## The routing problem nobody can solve

The Drive published a piece this week that put numbers on a frustration many drivers already feel intuitively. Navigation apps have gotten worse at saving time. The reason is structural, not technical. When roughly seven billion devices worldwide depend on GPS, and the most popular routing algorithms send users to the same "faster" alternate route simultaneously, the alternate route becomes congested too. The article frames this as a Nash equilibrium: individually optimal choices produce collectively suboptimal outcomes. Each driver follows the app's recommendation rationally. Everyone ends up worse off.

The piece notes that routing services do not track how many users they have sent down a given road, nor can they know where competing apps are sending their users. Two drivers on Waze and Google Maps may be routed to the same residential side street at the same time, each unaware of the other, both negating the time savings the algorithm promised. Between 2007 and 2017, GPS routing saved an estimated 52 billion gallons of fuel and a trillion miles. Those gains were real. They are also eroding as adoption approaches saturation.

This connects to an earlier analysis of how satellite navigation concentrates traffic on fewer roads, reducing environmental benefits — a problem [previously examined in these pages](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-maps-new-reader-is-a-machine) when TomTom's Discovery Event framed the audience for maps as increasingly algorithmic. But the Drive's contribution is sharper: the problem is not that the algorithms are dumb. It is that they are smart in isolation and blind in aggregate. No single routing service has enough information to optimize the system as a whole, and the competitive structure of the industry means they never will.

## The open-data counter-movement

While routing algorithms struggle with their own success, a different kind of response is taking shape in the geospatial ecosystem. This week, the Overture Maps Foundation — a coalition of Meta, Microsoft, Amazon, TomTom, and the Linux Foundation — released a points-of-interest dataset containing nearly 60 million entries worldwide. The dataset includes POIs that were not previously available in OpenStreetMap, and it is published as open data. For developers building navigation, local search, or urban planning tools, the release means access to a foundational layer of geographic information without licensing fees or platform lock-in.

IBM and NASA made a parallel move in scientific mapping. They released the NASA-IBM Lunar Foundation Model as open source on Hugging Face, trained on 30-plus spatially aligned data layers from nine instruments across four missions (LRO, GRAIL, SELENE/Kaguya). The model identifies craters, potential ice deposits, and volcanic formations on the Moon's surface, outperforming widely used methods by up to 23 percent. It joins the Prithvi family of open foundation models, which already spans geospatial Earth observation, weather, and heliophysics.

The common thread is deliberate openness. Overture's 60 million POIs and IBM-NASA's lunar model both reject the model that has dominated digital mapping for two decades: one or two companies hoard the data, and everyone else pays for access through proprietary APIs. The open-data advocates argue that the problems maps need to solve — routing at scale, climate monitoring, resource identification, disaster response — are too large and too systemic for any single entity to address alone. Openness is not charity. It is a structural bet that the next generation of mapping intelligence will be built on shared foundations, and that the organizations contributing to those foundations will compete on the layers above — interface design, domain expertise, real-time data — rather than on hoarding the base.

NV5's Geo Sessions 2026 virtual event, highlighted this week in GIM International, made the case from the industry side. The event's framing is explicit: the geospatial sector has "largely solved the collection problem." Satellites, drones, and airborne sensors generate more data than anyone can process. The real challenge now is turning that data into operational intelligence — helping decision-makers understand what matters and what action to take. A new GeoAI track at the event underscores how thoroughly AI has moved from novelty to infrastructure in the geospatial world. When the collection problem is solved but the interpretation problem is not, the value migrates from the data itself to the systems that can make sense of it.

## The territory still argues back

The open-data movement addresses one kind of fragility — the fragility of dependence on a single proprietary platform. But another kind of fragility surfaced this week in the realm of political cartography, and openness alone cannot fix it.

The UN General Assembly's adoption of the Equal Earth projection — a resolution championed by the "Correct the Map" campaign and passed 164-1 with six abstentions — was [covered here last week](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-decree-and-the-display) as an example of the gap between political decisions and the technical infrastructure that delivers them. What is new this week is the reaction from countries that find the new map's territorial depictions unacceptable. Japan objects to the Equal Earth map using the same colour for the disputed Kuril Islands as for Russia. India demands that Kashmir be depicted "in accordance with India's official map." Ukraine abstained from the vote, arguing that the shading of Crimea legitimizes Russia's 2014 annexation. Six countries — Estonia, Georgia, Moldova, Serbia, Lithuania, and Ukraine — voted or abstained in solidarity over the Crimea issue.

None of these countries object to the Equal Earth projection itself. They contest what the map says about sovereignty. A "corrected" projection that fixes Africa's relative size does not resolve the question of who owns a set of islands in the Sea of Okhotsk. If anything, by giving the new map the legitimacy of a UN endorsement, it raises the stakes of every colour choice and border line on it. Japan has formally asked the Equal Earth creators to modify the map through its US embassy. The cartographers, for their part, designed Equal Earth to be less politically fraught than the Gall-Peters projection that preceded it in the "map wars" of the 1970s. But no map that shows borders can be politically neutral, and the UN's endorsement only makes that more visible.

The political contestation extends to place names closer to home. New Mexico's congressional delegation wrote to Apple and Google this week asking both companies to commit to keeping the state's name on their maps, preempting a potential "New America" rename floated by the US president on social media. The delegation's letter highlights how digital mapping platforms rely on the federal Geographic Names Information System (GNIS) for US place names, and how previous GNIS updates — the Gulf of Mexico, Lake Ontario — were reflected in Apple Maps and Google Maps after presidential decrees, though at different speeds. Google applied the Lake Ontario change immediately; Apple waited for the GNIS update. The episode turns cartographic naming into a visible test of platform sovereignty: which naming authority does a mapping platform actually answer to?

## What the artist sees that the engineer misses

Simon Weckert's two projects — the 99 smartphones that created a phantom traffic jam on Google Maps in 2020, and the adversarial shirt that defeats surveillance cameras in 2026 — share a method. Both exploit the gap between what an algorithm infers from its inputs and what is actually happening in the world. In the first case, the input was GPS pings from stationary phones that the system interpreted as vehicles in a traffic jam. In the second, the input is visual data that the object detection model fails to classify as a person. Both are small interventions that reveal the assumptions baked into systems millions of people use without questioning.

The Drive's Nash equilibrium analysis shows the same gap from a different angle. The routing algorithm infers an optimal path from traffic data, but it cannot see the other routing algorithms sending their users to the same path. Google Maps' private navigation mode, explained this week by Automobile Magazine, offers another example: activating the mode stops Google from recording your activity in your account, but it does not stop GPS location data from being transmitted for navigation. The user gains privacy from Google's personalization engine but not from the system as a whole. The incognito label promises more than the technology delivers.

Google's dominance in navigation is itself a form of fragility. A MarketWatch study reported this week that 70 percent of US drivers now use Google Maps for radar avoidance, compared to 27 percent for Waze and 25 percent for Apple Maps. Google has been gradually merging Waze's community features — incident reports, hazard alerts, speed camera warnings — into Maps, making the standalone Waze app increasingly redundant. TomTom's launch of AmiGo on Android Auto, offering a free and ad-free alternative with community-powered alerts, represents one of the few remaining challenges to Google's consolidation. But the competitive landscape is thinning, and a market dominated by a single routing engine is a market where the Nash equilibrium problem gets worse, not better.

## Two wagers on the future

The stories from this week split into two wagers on what mapping should become. One wager — call it the open wager — bets that shared, transparent data foundations will produce better outcomes than proprietary control. Overture Maps' 60 million POIs, IBM and NASA's open-source lunar model, and NASA Earthdata's public silicate mineral maps all embody this bet. The geospatial industry's own pivot from data collection to operational intelligence, reflected in NV5's Geo Sessions and TomTom's Orbis Maps platform, suggests that the value is migrating upward anyway — from raw data to the intelligence layer that interprets it. If the base layer is open, competition happens where it should: on interpretation, interface, and domain expertise.

The other wager — call it the closed wager — bets that proprietary control of data and algorithms produces better outcomes through scale, integration, and the ability to optimize across the full stack. Google Maps' dominance in navigation is the clearest expression of this bet. The company's 793 data providers, its integration of Waze features, and its expansion into Android Auto all argue that a single, tightly controlled system can deliver more polished results than a federation of open contributors. The closed wager has a strong empirical case: Google Maps works well enough that 70 percent of US drivers choose it.

The tension between these wagers is not abstract. The routing Nash equilibrium that The Drive identified is a direct consequence of the closed wager's competitive structure: multiple proprietary systems, each optimizing in isolation, produce collectively suboptimal outcomes. An open routing layer — one where traffic data and routing decisions were shared across services — could in principle coordinate better. But no commercial routing provider has an incentive to share, because traffic data is a competitive asset. The open wager works when the participants agree that the foundation should be shared and the differentiation should happen above it. The closed wager works when one participant is large enough to internalize the whole stack.

Mapping is moving toward a hybrid. Overture Maps is itself a hybrid — a coalition of corporations contributing to an open base layer while competing on the services built atop it. TomTom's Orbis Maps combines OpenStreetMap, Overture data, and proprietary sources. The question is not whether open or closed will win. It is whether the open layers will be robust enough — and well-maintained enough — to prevent the closed layers from extracting rents that exceed the value they provide. Weckert's art projects, the routing Nash equilibrium, and the political fights over map projections all point to the same conclusion: the systems are vulnerable, and the vulnerabilities are structural. Openness is one response. Competition regulation is another. The one response that has clearly failed is pretending the systems are not fragile at all.
