# From drug dealers to dashboards

*Maps are becoming environments people act within, not documents they consult — and nobody has figured out who is responsible when something goes wrong.*

A franceinfo investigation published last week found that drug dealers in France have been listing their operations on Google Maps as ordinary businesses. Cannabis dealers created fake listings with precise addresses, star ratings, customer reviews, and phone numbers — the full infrastructure of a legitimate commercial presence, deployed on the world's most widely used mapping platform. Google has since removed some of the listings, but the investigation exposed a gap that runs deeper than a single moderation failure: Google Maps has become a commercial surface that anyone can write to, and the platform's verification mechanisms were not designed to catch actors who exploit the legitimate business registration process for illicit commerce.

The same week, BMW announced that starting in October 2026, its Maps application will merge navigation and driver-assistance visuals into a single 3D view on the central display. The car projects itself into a depth-mapped reconstruction of the road, showing the vehicle in its lane with surrounding traffic drawn from onboard sensors. By 2027, BMW plans to extend this to semi-automated urban driving in Germany — hands-free city driving guided by the navigation system. The map is no longer something the driver consults. It is the visual environment through which the car drives.

These two stories sit at opposite ends of the same shift. Maps have moved from reference documents to operational environments — surfaces that people act within, whether that means buying drugs, driving a car at 130 km/h, fighting a wildfire, or registering a land parcel. The map is no longer a representation of the world. It is where a growing share of the world's activity happens. And the institutions that built these maps — Google, BMW, Mapbox, the UN — are still operating with assumptions about responsibility and trust that belong to the era when maps were something you folded and put in a glove compartment.

## The cockpit as cartography

BMW's 3D cockpit view is the most literal example of a map becoming an environment. The system, developed in-house by BMW and running on Mapbox's rendering engine, places the car inside a three-dimensional model of the road. Highway Assistant 2 enables hands-free driving at up to 130 km/h with lane changes triggered by a glance toward the side mirror. The system draws traffic from the car's sensors rather than from map data, but the map provides the structural frame — lane geometry, road levels, junction layouts — that tells the car where it is and where it needs to go.

BMW's mapping strategy reveals how complex this stack has become. The company licenses Mapbox for rendering and real-time map display, while relying on HERE for certified high-definition maps that provide redundant lane detection for the driver-assistance functions. BMW co-owns HERE with Audi and Daimler, yet chose Mapbox for the visual layer — an acknowledgment that no single mapping provider covers the full range of requirements from real-time rendering to certified lane-level geometry. The map in the cockpit is not one map. It is several, layered and reconciled in real time.

The fragmentation extends beyond BMW. Google Maps, Waze, TomTom AmiGo, Sygic, OsmAnd, and a resurgent MapQuest all now support Android Auto, each targeting different segments. TomTom offers privacy-focused offline navigation. OsmAnd serves off-road users with topographic layers. MapQuest, relaunched in March 2026, went viral for refusing to rename Lake Ontario as "Lake America" and now supports Android Auto in beta. Google Maps is rolling out always-on speed limits and speedometer displays that persist even without an active destination — a feature that makes the map ambient rather than task-specific. The navigation market is not consolidating around Google. It is fragmenting into specialized surfaces, each optimized for a different kind of driving.

## The marketplace and the map

The drug dealer listings on Google Maps expose the other side of the shift. When a map becomes a commercial surface — a place where businesses list themselves, customers leave reviews, and users search for services — it inherits all the problems of a marketplace without the governance structures that markets have developed over centuries. Google's business registration process is open by design, which is what makes Google Maps useful as a directory of the world's commercial activity. But that openness also means that anyone with a Google account can create a listing for anything, and the platform's moderation relies on after-the-fact detection rather than upfront verification.

The franceinfo investigation is not an isolated case. It points to a structural vulnerability in any platform that treats user-generated business listings as legitimate map content. The same openness that lets a bakery in Lyon list itself on Google Maps lets a drug dealer list a pickup point with a five-star rating. The difference between a legitimate business and an illicit operation is not something a mapping platform can easily detect from listing metadata alone — it requires local knowledge, law enforcement coordination, and content moderation at a scale that Google has not demonstrated it can handle.

The map-as-marketplace problem intersects with the map-as-operational-environment problem in ways that should concern anyone building mapping infrastructure. If BMW's 3D cockpit draws traffic and road conditions from a combination of sensor data and map data, and if the map data includes user-generated content, then the integrity of the driving experience depends on the integrity of the underlying map — including the business listings, the traffic reports, and the road attributes that users contribute. A map that can be gamed by drug dealers can also be gamed by someone who wants to trick a semi-autonomous vehicle. As [examined in a previous piece](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/when-the-map-can-be-gamed), the fragility of mapping systems is not theoretical — adversarial inputs have already demonstrated that the systems we trust to navigate the world show cracks under pressure.

This is not hypothetical. The same week as the franceinfo investigation, Copernicus satellite imagery was tracking a wildfire on Croatia's Brac Island that had burned 5,600 hectares — 14 percent of the island's surface — forcing the evacuation of nearly 700 people. The satellite data fed into emergency response maps that firefighters used to track the fire's spread. In that context, the map is not a commercial surface or a driving environment. It is a crisis management tool, and its accuracy is a matter of life safety. The trust requirements are different, but the underlying infrastructure — satellite imagery, geospatial data layers, real-time updates — is built by the same ecosystem of companies and agencies. A mapping infrastructure that cannot keep drug dealers off its commercial surface raises questions about its reliability when the stakes are higher.

India's land records modernization programme, DILRMP 3.0, adds another dimension. The programme, running from 2026 to 2031, will assign every geo-referenced land parcel a 14-digit unique identification number — a system called Bhu-Aadhaar, modelled on India's biometric identity programme. GIS and cadastral maps form the backbone of this effort, which aims to create an integrated digital land-governance ecosystem with paperless registration and judicial integration. When the map becomes the legal record of who owns what, the integrity of the map is the integrity of the property system. India is also deploying drone surveying and LiDAR at scale — Matrix Geo Solutions won a three-year contract from Adani Infra for precision drone surveying across pan-India project locations — building the physical data layer that feeds the governance system.

## Who owns the trust

The common thread across these stories is a trust gap that nobody has closed. Google relies on after-the-fact moderation for user-generated business content. BMW relies on a dual-mapping strategy with HERE providing certified HD maps for safety-critical functions, but the visual layer depends on Mapbox data that is not certified to the same standard. The UN General Assembly voted 164-1 to endorse the Equal Earth projection over Mercator — a resolution that, as [noted earlier](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-decree-and-the-display), carries symbolic weight but cannot change the Web Mercator tile architecture that Google Maps and most web mapping platforms are built on. India's land records system will be only as reliable as the surveying and GIS infrastructure behind it.

Rhodes College, a liberal arts institution in Memphis, launched a GIS undergraduate certificate program the same week — the first interdisciplinary GIS credential at a liberal arts college in the United States. The program includes courses on critical cartographies, remote sensing, and mapping oral histories alongside technical GIS training. It is a small but telling signal: the field is starting to recognize that geographic information science is not just about coordinates and projections. It is about the social, political, and economic systems that maps encode and reproduce. The next generation of GIS professionals will need to think about trust and governance, not just data accuracy.

The mapping industry is building infrastructure that people drive through, buy things on, fight fires with, and register property through. The institutions that govern this infrastructure — from Google's content moderation team to the UN General Assembly to India's land records bureau — are still working with frameworks designed for a world where maps were printed on paper. The maps have moved on. The governance has not.
