# The map that hides what it shows

*From blurred prisons to masked arrival times, the world's mapping platforms are making deliberate choices about what you cannot see — and those choices follow no consistent rule.*

In September 2026, researchers discovered that Apple Maps blurs satellite imagery of detention facilities and military sites across China. The pixelation is visible, deliberate, and applied at scale — not a rendering glitch but a policy. The same month, a French newspaper reported that the Centre Pénitentiaire du Mans-Les Croisettes, a prison in Coulaines, Sarthe, is similarly invisible on Google Maps' satellite view, obscured under a French government decree that restricts aerial photography of sensitive sites. And on Vietnam.vn, a reporter noted that Google Maps sometimes masks estimated arrival times entirely, leaving drivers without a basic piece of navigation information that the platform normally provides.

Three platforms, three countries, three categories of concealment. Apple blurs for a foreign government. Google blurs for a domestic one. Google also withholds from its own users, for reasons it has not fully explained. None of these decisions were made by the same actor, none follow the same logic, and none were subject to public review. They share one thing: a mapping platform decided that something you might expect to see should be hidden, and the platform made that call within its own architecture, behind no deliberative process anyone can inspect.

## The patchwork of state-enforced erasure

The French prison blurring follows a specific legal mechanism. A January arrêté maintains the Zicad list — zones interdites à la captation aérienne de données — which bars aerial photography of prisons, military installations, and other sensitive infrastructure. Google complies by pixelating satellite tiles over these zones. The rule is French, the enforcement is French, and Google's role is implementation. A citizen can look up the arrêté, identify the zones, and understand why a tile is blurry.

The Apple Maps case in China operates under a different logic. No public decree specifies which sites Apple must blur. No accessible registry lists the affected coordinates. Researchers found the blurring by comparing Apple Maps imagery against unblurred sources and noticing systematic pixelation across military and government installations, including detention facilities linked to human rights concerns. Apple, which depends on Chinese regulatory approval to operate its services in the country, appears to have applied the blurring as a condition of market access — a private arrangement between a corporation and a state, with no transparency about its scope or criteria.

The contrast is sharp. France publishes its restrictions. China does not. Both result in hidden map tiles. The difference is that one system allows public scrutiny of what is concealed and why, while the other does not. When a mapping platform serves both jurisdictions, its users get two different versions of the world — one where erasure is documented, one where it is invisible. As [noted in a previous post](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-decree-and-the-display), political authority over maps is decoupling from the power to display them. The concealment cases sharpen that point: the authority to hide is now distributed across governments, platform policies, and bilateral arrangements that no single framework governs.

## What the platform hides from itself

The Google Maps ETA masking is a different species of concealment. No government ordered it. No security concern drives it. Vietnam.vn reported that the estimated arrival time, normally shown with colour-coded reliability indicators, sometimes disappears from the interface entirely. The article speculates about interface choices — transparency versus simplicity — but the underlying issue is that Google's navigation surface includes information that the platform can withhold from users without explanation.

This is not censorship in the political sense. It is a design decision about what the user is allowed to know. The ETA is calculated; the system has the number. It chooses not to show it. In a navigation context, where the user's entire relationship with the platform is built on trust that it will tell them what they need to know, the withholding is notable. It suggests that the map's interface is not a transparent window onto the data but a curated surface where the platform decides which computed values the user sees and which remain internal.

The pattern connects to a broader tension in how mapping platforms manage information asymmetry. Google knows where you are, where you are going, how fast you are moving, and when you will arrive. You know what Google shows you on the screen. The gap between those two states is a design space that Google controls entirely. The ETA masking is a small instance. But it sits alongside a larger architecture of selective disclosure — the 72 ranking signals that determine which businesses appear in local search, the ad placements that promote certain results, the tracking that persists in private navigation mode. [A previous post](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/when-the-map-can-be-gamed) examined how Google Maps' private mode stops recording in your account but does not stop transmitting location data. The map that hides what it shows is not hiding things from adversaries. It is managing what its own users can see.

## Borders that argue with each other

The same week as the blurring discoveries, the United Nations General Assembly's adoption of the Equal Earth projection continued to generate political friction. The "Correct the Map" resolution, passed 164-1 with six abstentions, encourages institutions worldwide to replace Mercator with a projection that preserves the relative size of continents — correcting the historical underrepresentation of Africa, which appears comparable to Greenland on Mercator maps despite being fourteen times larger.

The resolution is non-binding. It does not ban Mercator. But it has produced a cascade of territorial disputes that no projection can resolve. Japan objects to the Equal Earth map using the same colour for the disputed Kuril Islands as for Russia. India demands that Kashmir be depicted according to India's official map. Ukraine abstained from the vote, arguing that the shading of Crimea legitimizes Russia's 2014 annexation. Six countries voted or abstained in solidarity over the Crimea issue alone. Cameroon Tribune reported on the African perspective, quoting Togolese foreign minister Robert Dussey: "A just world begins with a just map." Moustique covered the same vote from Belgium, noting that Equal Earth sacrifices straight lines and distance accuracy to achieve area fidelity — a trade-off that makes the projection politically appealing but practically limited.

The border disputes expose a paradox at the heart of the "Correct the Map" campaign. Equal Earth corrects size distortion. It does not correct sovereignty disputes. By giving the new projection the legitimacy of a UN endorsement, the resolution raised the stakes of every border line and colour choice on the map. Countries that supported the projection for Africa's sake now find themselves fighting the same map over their own territories. The concealment here is not pixelation but ambiguity: the map does not hide anything, but it cannot say who owns what, and the UN's endorsement makes that silence louder.

## The cartography of territorial ambition

Trump's Greenland deal adds another dimension to the political cartography of the week. ThePrint analysed the agreement announced by the Trump administration as a continuation of the 1951 Defense of Greenland Agreement, which grants the United States extensive military access within Danish sovereignty. Despite Trump's rhetoric of annexation and his Truth Social posts showing Greenland as American territory, the final text recognises Danish sovereignty and Greenlandic self-determination. The "maps" Trump posted were not policy. They were performance.

The gap between the cartographic rhetoric and the diplomatic reality is itself a form of concealment. Trump's social media maps presented a world that does not exist — Greenland under the American flag — while the actual agreement preserved the status quo with modest adjustments. The maps were not meant to describe reality but to project power, to test reactions, to create a narrative of territorial expansion that the paperwork does not deliver. This is cartography as propaganda, not as representation, and it sits on a continuum with the more institutional forms of concealment practiced by Apple and Google. In all three cases, the map shows something other than what is true on the ground. The difference is that Apple and Google hide what exists. Trump showed what does not.

## The commercial and the scientific

Not all concealment is political. Some of it is commercial design, and some of it is simply the boundary of what a tool was built to do. The Guernsey solar panel suitability map, launched by the States of Guernsey, shows every rooftop on the island colour-coded by solar potential — dark red for high-yield roofs, dark blue for poor ones. The map reveals something that was previously invisible to homeowners: which roofs are worth investing in. It does not hide anything. It makes a hidden calculation public. The tool, built with the energy provider Little Green, lets residents compare installer quotes against independent data. Here the map is an instrument of transparency, the inverse of the blurring and masking that dominates the week's other stories.

Apple Maps' advantages over Google Maps, as detailed by Android Authority, point to a different kind of design choice. Apple's interface is cleaner, with fewer ads and less promoted content. The map is more legible because Apple has chosen to show less. Google Maps, by contrast, shows more — more businesses, more reviews, more sponsored results — and in doing so, conceals the map itself behind a commercial layer. Apple's hiking tools, with topographic contours and custom route creation, show terrain that Google Maps flattens. The competition between the two platforms is partly a competition about how much to reveal and how much to withhold, and the user's experience of the world is shaped by choices that neither company frames as editorial decisions.

The scientific mapping in this week's corpus sits at the opposite end of the spectrum from concealment. A new geomorphological map of the Piscinas dune system in Sardinia, published in the Journal of Maps, documents a transgressive dune field spanning over two square kilometres — a rare feature in the Mediterranean. The HD maps market analysis from MarketsandMarkets projects growth from $1.34 billion to $2.43 billion by 2033, driven by autonomous vehicle adoption. These are maps that aim to reveal everything: centimetre-level road geometry, dune migration patterns, semantic layers of lane markings and signage. The HD map architecture — geometric, semantic, localisation, and dynamic update layers — is designed to eliminate blind spots, not preserve them. The tension with the concealment stories is direct: the same industry that builds centimetre-accurate maps for machines also builds maps that blur detention camps for governments. The technology can show everything. The policies decide what it actually shows.

## The line nobody has drawn

The week's stories converge on a question that no mapping platform has answered publicly: what is the rule? France has a rule — the Zicad list. The UN has a resolution — non-binding, and contested on its own borders. Apple has an arrangement with China — opaque. Google has interface decisions — unexplained. Trump has social media posts — untethered from policy. The concealment of information on maps follows no consistent principle, answers to no single authority, and produces a world where what you can see depends on which platform you open, which country you are in, and which government has negotiated which agreement with which company.

The Guernsey solar map and the Sardinia dune map are reminders that mapping can be an instrument of revelation. The HD maps for autonomous vehicles are a reminder that the technology exists to see everything. The gap between what the technology can show and what the platforms choose to show is where power lives — and that gap is widening, not closing.
