# The map at work

*Geographic data has quietly entered insurance underwriting, humanitarian coordination, and enterprise AI — and the consumer surface is where the edges show.*

A Slovenian technology magazine recently published a profile of TomTom that reads like a company obituary written too early. The article traces the firm from Palmtop Software, a small Amsterdam operation writing dictionary apps for Psion organizers, through the 2004 TomTom GO — the portable navigation device that became one of the bestselling consumer electronics products in history — to the smartphone disruption that nearly killed the business. The profile ends with TomTom's current incarnation as a provider of location data for Uber, automotive fleets, and autonomous driving systems. The tone is nostalgic. It should not be.

The same week the profile appeared, TomTom announced that it was bringing its location intelligence to Microsoft Fabric and Microsoft Foundry, integrating geospatial context into AI agents built on Microsoft's enterprise data platform. The feature, called IQ Sharing, lets AI agents reason about where goods flow, where infrastructure is stressed, and where risk concentrates — using TomTom's map data, built on the Overture Maps foundation, as shared context across Microsoft's ecosystem. This is not a navigation product. It is a reasoning infrastructure for enterprise AI. The distance between the TomTom GO — a device you mounted on a dashboard to find your way — and this announcement is the distance the entire mapping industry has traveled.

## The institutional footprint

The GIS market will reach $31.8 billion by 2031, according to Mordor Intelligence, growing at a compound annual rate of 13.94 percent. The forecast, published by EIN News, attributes the expansion to smart city deployments, cloud-based GIS, mobile data collection, and the integration of geospatial technology into public and private decision-making. Market forecasts are always partly guesswork. But the direction is consistent with what the individual stories show: geographic data has become a working component of institutional processes, not a consumer convenience.

The insurance industry offers the clearest example. Vinod Poomalai, head of insurtech product marketing at TomTom, told The Insurer TV that geospatial data is helping property and casualty carriers "bring the real world into their risk modeling." For auto insurance, TomTom's road-level traffic and mobility data lets carriers assess not just the driver but the driving environment — the risk profile of specific roads, intersections, and corridors. For home insurance, the data maps exposure to wildfires, floods, and earthquakes. The shift matters because traditional insurance risk models rely on static territorial classifications — postal codes, census tracts, regional boundaries. TomTom's data, updated and stored daily, introduces temporal and spatial granularity that those models lack.

Claims validation is emerging as a second use case. Poomalai described AI agents deployed to validate claims faster and flag suspicious activity, replacing the territorial models that insurers have used for decades. When an adjuster can cross-reference a claim against real-time traffic conditions, road closures, and weather events at a specific location and time, the map becomes an audit instrument. The insurer is not navigating anywhere. The map is working as evidence, as verification, as a component of a financial workflow.

TomTom's analysis of major events — the World Cup, the Olympics, city festivals — demonstrates the same institutional pattern from a different angle. The company's traffic data showed that before the 2026 World Cup final at MetLife Stadium, congestion peaked at 196.3 percent at noon, exactly three hours before kickoff — matching a prediction TomTom had made in advance. Paris used the 2024 Olympics to build 50 kilometers of new cycling lanes and make every Olympic venue accessible by public transit for the first time. AECOM, which managed mobility for seven matches in Miami, now requires candidate cities to submit mobility plans as a condition of hosting. The traffic data is not a product. It is a planning instrument that reveals where a city's mobility system breaks under stress and what fixes might outlast the event.

The same data serves policy debates with no connection to navigation. A German columnist writing in der Freitag used anonymized TomTom traffic data to argue that the 2026 Tankrabatt — a fuel subsidy criticized as climate-damaging — did not change driving behavior because motorists do not alter their habits for 17-cent price variations. In Malta, the TomTom Traffic Index revealed that drivers lost more than four days to congestion in 2025, with a peak of 98 percent around Valletta on October 13. Both cases extend the pattern [examined previously](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-map-as-evidence): traffic data has become a public instrument for evaluating policy, not a private asset for routing algorithms.

## Coordination and community

The humanitarian sector is building its own version of institutional mapping. Coordination SUD, a French umbrella group for 61 humanitarian NGOs, launched the CHD Map — an interactive map documenting 783 actions across 93 countries. The tool, built with CartONG, lets users explore by organization or by country, revealing the geographic footprint of French humanitarian aid for the first time at the collective level. Before the CHD Map, each NGO documented its own presence independently. No one could answer the question "where are French humanitarian organizations working?" without consulting 61 separate sources. The map is a coordination instrument — a donor can see which organizations are active in a country, an NGO can identify gaps, a journalist can verify claims of presence.

At a different scale, "Map patou" performs a similar function in the Hautes-Alpes. The interactive map records which alpine pastures have livestock protection dogs — the large dogs that guard sheep from predators and can create conflicts with uninformed hikers. The community of communes Buëch-Dévoluy integrated the tool in late summer 2026, part of a collective effort to census the dogs and inform mountain users. The map covers parts of the Alps and the Massif Central, and its expansion reflects a demand that is local and practical, driven by community needs. No enterprise platform. No AI agent. Just a map that solves a problem a community identified.

The TomTom-Microsoft Fabric integration points to where the institutional map is heading. When an AI agent built on Microsoft's platform needs to answer a question about supply chain pressure, it needs more than a pin on a map. It needs to understand what a location represents — the road network, the regulations, the historical traffic patterns, the risk profile. TomTom's data, structured on Overture Maps and open standards, provides that context. [The machine's relationship to geographic data](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/the-maps-new-reader-is-a-machine) has moved from theory to implementation. The AI agent does not read a map the way a human does. It queries a geographic database for relationships between places, routes, and conditions. For the agent, there is no map. There is only location context, served through an API, consumed as structured data.

The ACM/SIGGRAPH Pioneers panel on GIS, scheduled for October 14, frames this shift for a broader technical audience. The panel defines GIS as "a map that knows things" — connecting geographic locations to large volumes of additional information. The definition is decades old in the GIS community. What is new is the venue: a computer graphics conference, not a geospatial one. The institutional reach of geographic data has expanded beyond its home discipline into adjacent fields that are discovering they need it.

## The edges of the consumer surface

While institutional mapping expands, the consumer navigation surface is showing its boundaries. BGR published a guide to what stops working when Google Maps goes offline. The list is long: no real-time traffic, no proactive rerouting, no business popularity indicators, no walking, cycling, or transit navigation, no reviews, no photos, no Street View. The offline map covers up to 150 miles of latitude by 300 miles of longitude — enough for a road trip, insufficient for the data-rich experience that makes Google Maps useful. The article shows that the consumer map's intelligence is almost entirely server-side. Download the tiles, and you have a 1990s navigation device with a better interface.

Tom's Guide tested Google Maps' immersive navigation for two weeks on CarPlay and Android Auto and found that the 3D depth rendering and dynamic smart zoom make complex highway exits and multi-layer interchanges legible in ways that flat maps cannot. The reviewer, who drives 300 miles per week, called it sufficient reason to stay on Google Maps. The feature is genuine progress. But it is progress on the consumer surface — making the visual experience better for the driver looking at the screen. The institutional layer beneath it, where geographic data informs insurance underwriting and supply chain reasoning, does not benefit from 3D rendering. The consumer surface and the institutional core are diverging, and the investment in visual polish does not address the structural questions about data access, interoperability, and institutional integration.

A Romanian navigation app poll revealed a shift that would have been unlikely two years ago: HERE Maps overtook Google Maps with 29 percent of the vote against Google's 26 percent. Waze led with 35 percent. The poll is national, not global. But it reflects a pattern visible across the month's coverage: Google Maps and Waze together account for about 60 percent of preferences, while HERE Maps maintains a solid automotive-industry presence in Europe that social media conversations do not capture. The competitive picture is more fragmented than the English-language press suggests.

Toyota's Drive Connect system, compared to Google Maps by BGR, illustrates the embedded-versus-universal tension. Toyota's platform costs roughly $15 per month, integrates deeply with the vehicle, includes a live human agent, and is limited to Toyota and Lexus. Google Maps is free, multimodal, cross-platform, and now integrated with Gemini for conversational assistance. The comparison is not about which is better. It is about two models of the in-car map: one where the map is part of the car, one where the car is part of the map. [BMW made the same choice explicit](https://mappingforward.substack.com/p/from-drug-dealers-to-dashboards) when it selected Mapbox for rendering and HERE for certified lane-level geometry — a dual strategy that no single provider can satisfy.

Google Maps' updated satellite imagery of Gaza, analyzed by EL PAÍS, performs a function that none of the institutional applications address. The February 2026 photographs document destruction at a scale that no report can convey: the vanished Swedish village near the Egyptian border, the ruins of Al-Shifa Hospital, the destruction of the Mushtaha tower — Gaza's tallest building — and the 83 percent building damage rate in Gaza City confirmed by UNOSAT. Google has updated its Gaza imagery three times since the conflict began. Apple Maps has not. The difference is editorial, not cartographic. Google has decided that its satellite layer should document what happened. Apple has decided otherwise, or has not decided. The choice determines whether the most widely used mapping platform in the world serves as a historical record. The map at work, in this case, is not serving an institution. It is serving the future.

The week's stories describe a field that has outgrown its origins. TomTom's trajectory from Palmtop Software to Microsoft Fabric integration is the industry's trajectory in miniature. The consumer navigation device became enterprise data infrastructure. The dashboard map became a reasoning layer for AI agents. The static territorial model became a daily-updated risk instrument. The community map became a coordination tool. The satellite image became a historical document. The GIS market, growing toward $31.8 billion, is the economic measure of a shift that the individual stories make concrete: geographic data is no longer something you look at. It is something institutions work with.
